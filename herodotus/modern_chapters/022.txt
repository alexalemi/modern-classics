Chapter 22: The Shape of the World, the Rivers of Scythia, and the Customs of the Scythians

37. The Persians live in Asia, and their land reaches down to the Southern Sea, the one called the Erythraean, which is the Indian Ocean. Above them, toward the north wind, live the Medes. Above the Medes live the Saspeirians, and above the Saspeirians the Colchians, whose land reaches to the Northern Sea, the Black Sea, where the river Phasis flows out. These four nations fill the land from sea to sea.

38. From there two peninsulas stretch westward out of Asia into the sea, and I will describe them both. The first peninsula has a northern side and a southern side. On the north it begins at the Phasis and runs along the coast of the Black Sea and the Hellespont, as far as Sigeum in the land of Troy. On the south the same peninsula runs from the Myriandric Gulf, which lies near Phoenicia, toward the sea as far as the headland of Triopium. Thirty different peoples live in this peninsula.

39. That is one of the peninsulas. The other begins from the land of the Persians and stretches along the Erythraean Sea. It takes in Persia, then Assyria next to Persia, and Arabia after Assyria. It ends (or rather, it is commonly supposed to end) at the Red Sea, into which Darius dug a channel from the Nile. Now, along the stretch from the land of the Persians as far as Phoenicia, the land is broad and there is plenty of room. But past Phoenicia this peninsula runs along the shore of our own sea, the Mediterranean, by Palestine, Syria and Egypt, where it ends. Only three nations live in it.

40. Those are the parts of Asia that lie west of the Persian land. As for what lies beyond the Persians, the Medes, the Saspeirians and the Colchians, toward the east and the sunrise, on one side the Erythraean Sea runs along it, and on the north both the Caspian Sea and the river Araxes, which flows toward the rising sun. Asia is inhabited as far as India. But from there on to the east it is desert, and no one can say what sort of land it is.

41. That is the shape and size of Asia. Libya is part of the second peninsula, since Libya begins right where Egypt ends. Around Egypt this peninsula is narrow: from our sea to the Erythraean Sea the distance there is 600,000 feet, which comes to about 115 miles. But past this narrow neck, the part of the peninsula called Libya happens to be extremely broad.

42. So I am surprised at the people who have marked out the world and divided it into Libya, Asia and Europe, because the difference between them is not small. In length Europe stretches along beside both of the others, and in breadth it seems to me far larger than either, beyond all comparison. Libya, for its part, gives proof that it is surrounded by sea, except for the part that borders on Asia. The first person we know of to show this was Necho, king of the Egyptians. When he had stopped digging the canal that runs from the Nile to the Red Sea, he sent out some Phoenicians in ships. He ordered them to sail around and come back through the Pillars of Heracles into the Northern Sea, the Mediterranean, and so home to Egypt. So the Phoenicians set out from the Red Sea and sailed into the Southern Sea. Whenever autumn came, they would put in to shore wherever in Libya they happened to be on their voyage, sow the land, and wait for the harvest. Once they had reaped the grain they sailed on. In this way, after two years had gone by, in the third year they rounded the Pillars of Heracles and came back to Egypt. And they reported something I cannot believe, though someone else may: that as they sailed around Libya they had the sun on their right hand.

43. That is how this country was first found to be what it is. After this it is the Carthaginians who report on it. For Sataspes son of Teaspis, the Achaemenid, did not sail around Libya, though he was sent to do exactly that. The length of the voyage and the emptiness of the land frightened him, so he turned back and never finished the task his mother had set him. Here is how that came about. This man had raped a young unmarried daughter of Zopyrus son of Megabyzus. King Xerxes was about to have him impaled for this crime, when Sataspes' mother, who was a sister of Darius, begged for his life. She said that she herself would lay a heavier penalty on him than Xerxes would: he would be forced, she said, to sail around Libya, until in sailing around it he came to the Red Sea. Xerxes agreed to these terms. So Sataspes went to Egypt, got a ship and sailors from the Egyptians, and sailed to the Pillars of Heracles. He sailed through them, rounded the point of Libya called Cape Soloeis, and sailed on toward the south. He crossed a great deal of sea over many months, but there was always more and more still to sail, so he turned around and sailed back to Egypt. From there he went to King Xerxes and made his report. At the farthest point he reached, he said, he was sailing past a people of very small stature who wore clothes made from the palm tree. Whenever he and his crew came to land with their ship, these people would leave their towns and run away to the mountains. His men, he said, did them no harm when they went into the towns; they only took food. And the reason he had not sailed all the way around Libya, he said, was that the ship could go no farther but was stuck fast. Xerxes did not believe he was telling the truth. Since Sataspes had not done the task he was given, the king had him impaled, carrying out the penalty pronounced before. Sataspes had a eunuch who, as soon as he heard that his master was dead, ran away to Samos with a great deal of money. A man of Samos took that money for himself. I know his name, but I am leaving it out on purpose.

44. Most of Asia was explored by Darius. He wanted to know where the river Indus flows out into the sea. (The Indus is the second of all the rivers in the world that produce crocodiles.) So he sent ships, with men he trusted to tell the truth, and among them was Scylax, a man of Caryanda. They started from the city of Caspatyrus and the land of Pactyice and sailed down the river toward the east and the sunrise until they reached the sea. Then they sailed west across the sea, and in the thirtieth month they came to the very place from which the king of the Egyptians had sent out the Phoenicians I spoke of before, to sail around Libya. After these men had made their voyage along the coast, Darius conquered the Indians and made use of this sea. In this way it has been found that Asia too, except for the parts toward the rising sun, is like Libya, surrounded by water.

45. But as for Europe, no one clearly knows whether it is surrounded by sea, either on the side toward the rising sun or on the north. What is known is that in length it stretches along beside both of the other two. And I cannot understand why the earth, which is one, should have three different names, all of them women's names, or why the boundaries set to divide it should be the Nile, the river of Egypt, and the Phasis in Colchis (or, as some say, the Don, the river of Maeotis, and the Cimmerian Strait). Nor can I find out who the people were who drew these boundaries, or why they gave the names. Most Greeks say that Libya is named after Libya, a woman of that country, and Asia after the wife of Prometheus. But the Lydians claim this last name for themselves. They say Asia was named after Asies son of Cotys son of Manes, and not after Asia the wife of Prometheus, and they say the Asian tribe in Sardis takes its name from him too. As for Europe, no one knows whether it is surrounded by sea, and it is not clear where it got this name or who gave it, unless we say that the land took its name from Europa of Tyre. If so, it would seem that before this it had no name, like the others. But this Europa was plainly from Asia, and she never came to the land the Greeks now call Europe. She went only from Phoenicia to Crete, and from Crete to Lycia. Enough said about these matters; we will go by the accounts that are generally accepted.

46. Now the region of the Black Sea, which Darius was preparing to invade, holds the most ignorant nations of any land, apart from the Scythians. We cannot point to a single nation living around the Black Sea that stands out for its intelligence, nor do we know of any man of learning who has come from there, except the Scythian nation and Anacharsis. The Scythians have worked out the most important thing in all human affairs more cleverly than any other people we know of. In other respects I do not admire them much. But this most important thing they have discovered is this: no one who comes to attack them can escape, and no one can catch them if they do not want to be found. A people that has no towns and no walls, that carries its houses along with it, that fights as mounted archers, that lives not by the plow but by its herds, and whose homes are on wagons: how could such people be anything but unbeatable and impossible to come to grips with?

47. They have found this out because their land is suited to it and their rivers fight on their side. First of all, the land is flat, grassy and well watered. Then there are rivers running through it, not many fewer in number than the canals of Egypt. I will name the ones that are worth mentioning and can be sailed up from the sea. There is the Danube, with five mouths, and after it the Dniester, the Bug, the Dnieper, the Panticapes, the Hypacyris, the Gerrhus and the Don. Here is how they flow.

48. The Danube, the greatest of all the rivers we know, always flows with the same volume, summer and winter alike. It is the first of the Scythian rivers from the west, and it has become the greatest of all rivers because other rivers flow into it. These are the ones that make it great. Five of them flow through the land of Scythia: the one the Scythians call the Porata and the Greeks the Pyretus, and besides that the Tiarantus, the Ararus, the Naparis and the Ordessus. The first of these is a large river lying to the east, and there it joins its waters with the Danube. The second, the Tiarantus, is farther west and smaller. The Ararus, the Naparis and the Ordessus run between these two and flow into the Danube.

49. Those are the native Scythian rivers that swell its stream. From the land of the Agathyrsians the Maris flows in and joins the Danube. From the peaks of Haemus three other large rivers, the Atlas, the Auras and the Tibisis, flow north and fall into it. Through Thrace and the land of the Thracian Crobyzians flow the rivers Athrys, Noes and Artanes, which empty into the Danube. From the Paeonians and Mount Rhodope the river Cius cuts through the middle of Haemus and runs into it as well. From the Illyrians the river Angrus flows north into the Triballian plain and into the river Brongus, and the Brongus flows into the Danube; so the Danube takes in both of them, and they are large rivers. From the region above the Ombricans, the river Carpis and another river, the Alpis, also flow north and run into it. For the Danube in fact flows through the whole of Europe. It begins in the land of the Celts, who, after the Cynesians, live farthest west of all the peoples of Europe. So it flows through all of Europe and empties into the sea along the side of Scythia.

50. So it is because all these rivers I have named, and many others, add their water to it that the Danube becomes the greatest of rivers. If we compare single streams, river against river, the Nile carries more water, since no river or spring flows into the Nile to add to its volume. And the reason the Danube always runs at the same level, summer and winter, is something like this, I think. In winter it is its natural size, or only a little larger than its nature, because that country gets very little rain in winter but has snow all the time. In summer, though, the snow that fell in great quantities during the winter melts and runs into the Danube from every side. This snow runs into the river and helps swell it, and so do many violent rainstorms, since it rains there in summer. So the water that pours into the Danube is greater in summer than in winter by about as much as the water the sun draws up to itself in summer is greater than what it draws up in winter. When these things are set against each other, they balance out, and so the river is always seen at the same volume.

51. So the Danube is one of the Scythians' rivers. After it comes the Dniester, which starts in the north and begins its course from a large lake that forms the boundary between the land of the Scythians and the land of the Neuri. At its mouth live Greeks who are called Tyritae.

52. The third river is the Bug. It starts in Scythia and flows out of a great lake around which white wild horses graze, and this lake is rightly called the Mother of the Bug. This is where the Bug rises, and for five days' sail it runs shallow and still sweet. But from there to the sea, for four days' sail, it is very bitter. That is because the water of a bitter spring flows into it, a spring so extremely bitter that, small as it is, it changes the water of the Bug when it mixes with it, though the Bug is a river few can equal in size. This spring lies on the border between the land of the farming Scythians and that of the Alazones. The spring, and the place it flows from, are called Exampaeus in Scythian, which is in Greek Hierai Hodoi, the Sacred Ways. Now the Dniester and the Bug come close to each other in their windings in the land of the Alazones, but after that each turns away, and they flow on with a widening space between them.

53. The fourth is the Dnieper. It is the largest of these rivers after the Danube, and in my opinion it is the most useful, not only of the Scythian rivers but of all the rivers in the world besides, except the Nile of Egypt, since no other river can be compared with the Nile. Of the rest, though, the Dnieper is the most useful. It provides the finest and richest pastures for cattle, and fish that are far better and more plentiful than in any other river. Its water is the sweetest to drink and runs clear, while the rivers beside it are muddy. Along its banks crops grow better than anywhere else, and where the land is not sown the grass grows deepest. At its mouth, salt forms by itself in great quantities. It also produces huge fish with no spine, which they call antacaei and use for salting, and many other things worth wondering at. As far as the region of the Gerrhi, which is forty days' voyage upstream, the Dnieper is known to flow from the north. Above that no one can say what nations it flows through. It is certain, though, that it runs through empty country down to the land of the farming Scythians, since these Scythians live along its banks for ten days' sail. This river and the Nile are the only two whose sources I cannot tell, and I think no other Greek can either. When the Dnieper comes near the sea, the Bug joins it, running out into the same marsh. The land between these two rivers, which juts out like a ship's beak, is called the Point of Hippoles. A temple of the Mother stands on it, and opposite the temple, on the Bug, live the Borysthenites.

54. So much for these rivers. After them there is a fifth river as well, called the Panticapes. This one too flows from the north and from a lake. The farming Scythians live in the land between this river and the Dnieper. It runs out into the region of Hylaea, and after passing through it, it joins the Dnieper.

55. Sixth comes the river Hypacyris. It starts from a lake, flows through the middle of the nomad Scythians, and runs out into the sea by the city of Carcinitis, skirting on its right bank the region of Hylaea and the place called the Racecourse of Achilles.

56. Seventh is the Gerrhus. It branches off from the Dnieper at about the point in that country where the Dnieper stops being known. It branches off, as I say, in this region, and it has the same name as the region itself, Gerrhus. As it flows to the sea it forms the border between the land of the nomad Scythians and that of the Royal Scythians, and it runs out into the Hypacyris.

57. The eighth is the Don. It starts at first from a large lake and runs out into a still larger lake called Maeotis, which is the boundary between the Royal Scythians and the Sauromatae. Another river, called the Hyrgis, flows into this Don.

58. These are the notable rivers the Scythians are supplied with. As for their cattle, the grass that grows in Scythia produces more bile in them than any other grass we know of, and you can see for yourself that this is so when you cut the cattle open.

59. So they have plenty of the things that matter most. As for the rest, their customs are these. The only gods they worship are these: first and foremost Hestia, then Zeus and Earth (they believe Earth is the wife of Zeus), and after these Apollo, Heavenly Aphrodite, Heracles and Ares. All the Scythians have established worship for these gods, and the ones called the Royal Scythians sacrifice to Poseidon as well. In Scythian, Hestia is called Tabiti; Zeus is called Papaeus, which means Father, and in my opinion that is exactly the right name for him; Earth is Api; Apollo is Oetosyrus; Heavenly Aphrodite is called Argimpasa; and Poseidon is Thagimasadas. It is not their custom to make images, altars or temples for any god except Ares; for him it is their custom to make them.

60. They have one and the same way of sacrificing established for all their rites alike, and it is done like this. The animal stands with its forefeet tied together. The priest who is making the sacrifice stands behind it, pulls the end of the cord, and throws the beast down. As it falls he calls on the god he is sacrificing to. Then at once he throws a noose around its neck, puts a small stick through the noose, and twists it until the animal is strangled. He lights no fire, makes no first offering from the victim, and pours no wine over it. When he has strangled it and skinned it, he sets about boiling it.

61. Since the land of Scythia has very little wood, they have invented this way of boiling the meat. When they have skinned the victims, they strip the flesh off the bones and put it into cauldrons, if they happen to have any. These are made in their own country and look very much like the mixing bowls of Lesbos, except that they are much larger. They put the meat into these and boil it by lighting the victim's bones underneath. If they have no cauldron at hand, they put all the meat into the animals' stomachs, add water, and light the bones underneath. The bones burn beautifully, and the stomachs easily hold the meat once it has been stripped off the bones. So an ox is made to boil itself, and each of the other victims boils itself too. When the meat is cooked, the man making the sacrifice takes a first offering of the meat and of the vital organs and throws it on the ground in front of him. They sacrifice various kinds of livestock, but horses most of all.

62. That is how they sacrifice to the rest of the gods, and those are the animals they use. But to Ares they sacrifice as follows. In each district of each of their provinces they have a temple of Ares, built like this. Bundles of brushwood are heaped up to make a pile about 1,800 feet long and as many wide, but not as high. On top of it a flat square is made. Three of its sides are sheer, but on the fourth side the pile can be climbed. Every year they heap on a hundred and fifty wagonloads of brushwood, because the pile is always settling under the weather. On top of this pile each people has an ancient iron sword set up, and this is the sacred image of Ares. Every year they bring offerings of cattle and horses to this sword, and on top of what they offer the other gods, they make it one more sacrifice besides: of all the enemies they take prisoner in war, they sacrifice one man in every hundred. They do not do it the way they sacrifice cattle, but in a different way. First they pour wine over the men's heads. Then they cut their throats over a bowl, so that the blood runs into it. Then they carry the bowl up to the top of the brushwood pile and pour the blood over the sword. That is what they carry up. Meanwhile, down below beside the temple, they do this: they cut off the right arms of all the slaughtered men, hands and all, and throw them up into the air. Then, when they have finished offering the other victims, they go away. Each arm lies wherever it happened to fall, and the body lies apart from it.

63. Those are the sacrifices established among them. They make no use of pigs, and in fact they will not keep them in their country at all.

64. War is arranged among them like this. When a Scythian kills his first man, he drinks some of his blood. He takes the heads of all the men he kills in battle to the king, because if he brings a head he gets a share of the plunder they have taken, and if not, he gets none. He takes the skin off the head by cutting all the way around it at the ears, then taking hold of the scalp and shaking it loose. After that he scrapes off the flesh with an ox's rib and works the skin with his hands. When he has softened it this way, he keeps it as a napkin to wipe his hands on. He hangs it from the bridle of the horse he rides himself, and he is proud of it, because the man who has the most of these skins to wipe his hands on is judged the bravest. Many of them also make cloaks to wear out of the skins they have taken off, stitching them together like shepherds' leather coats. Many also take the skin, fingernails and all, off the right hands of their dead enemies and make it into covers for their quivers. Human skin, it seems, is thick and glossy, a brighter white than any other skin. And many take the skin off whole bodies, stretch it on wooden frames, and carry it around on their horses.

65. Those are their established customs in these matters. With the skulls themselves (not everyone's, but those of their worst enemies) they do this. The man saws off everything below the eyebrows and cleans out the inside. If he is poor, he just stretches oxhide around the outside and uses it as it is. If he is rich, he stretches oxhide around it and also gilds the inside, and uses it as a drinking cup. They do this with their own relatives as well, if one of them has quarreled with a man and the man has beaten him in a trial before the king. When guests he thinks highly of come to visit, he brings out these skulls and tells them how these men, though they were his own family, made war on him, and how he beat them. They count this as proof of a man's courage.

66. Once a year each governor of a district mixes a bowl of wine in his own district, and the Scythians who have killed enemies drink from it. Those who have not done this do not taste the wine, but sit apart in disgrace, and this is the greatest shame there is among them. But those who have killed a great many men drink from two cups together at the same time.

67. There are many soothsayers among the Scythians, and they tell the future with a lot of willow rods, in this way. They bring large bundles of rods, lay them on the ground and untie them. Then they set out each rod separately and make their prophecies. While they are speaking, they gather the rods together again, and then lay them out one by one a second time. This way of divining has come down to them from their fathers. But the Enarees, the "man-women," say that Aphrodite gave them the gift of prophecy, and they divine with the bark of the linden tree. The man splits the linden bark into three strips, braids them together in his fingers and unbraids them again, and as he does this he speaks the oracle.

68. When the king of the Scythians falls ill, he sends for the three soothsayers with the best reputation, who divine in the way I have described. What they usually say is something like this: that so-and-so has sworn falsely by the king's hearth. And they name one of the citizens, whoever it happens to be. (It is the Scythians' general custom to swear by the king's hearth when they want to take their most solemn oath.) The man they say has sworn falsely is seized at once and brought in, held on both sides. When he arrives, the soothsayers accuse him: their divination shows, they say, that he has sworn falsely by the king's hearth, and that this is why the king is in pain. He denies it, insists he did not swear falsely, and protests in outrage. When he denies it, the king sends for twice as many other soothsayers. If these too look into their divination and find him guilty of false swearing, they cut off his head at once, and the first soothsayers divide his property among themselves by lot. But if the soothsayers who came in second acquit him, more soothsayers come in, and then more after them. If most of them acquit the man, the sentence is that the first soothsayers must be put to death themselves.

69. They put them to death in this way. First they fill a wagon with brushwood and yoke oxen to it. Then they tie the soothsayers' feet, bind their hands behind them, gag their mouths, and fasten them down in the middle of the brushwood. Then they set it on fire, frighten the oxen, and let them go. Often the oxen are burned to death along with the soothsayers. Often, too, they get away singed, once the pole they are yoked to has burned through. They burn soothsayers in this way for other reasons as well, calling them false prophets. And when the king puts anyone to death, he does not leave their sons alive either. He kills all the males, but does no harm to the females.

70. When the Scythians swear an oath to anyone, they do it like this. They pour wine into a large earthenware cup and mix into it blood from the men who are swearing to each other, taking it either by pricking themselves with an awl or by making a small cut in their bodies with a dagger. Then they dip a sword, arrows, a battle-axe and a javelin into the cup. When they have done this, they call down many curses on whoever breaks the oath, and then they drink it, both the men making the oath and the most respected of their followers.

71. The kings are buried in the land of the Gerrhi, at the point up to which the Dnieper can be navigated. There, when their king dies, they dig a large square pit in the ground. When they have it ready, they take up the body. It has been coated all over with wax, and its belly has been slit open, cleaned out, filled with chopped galingale, spices, parsley seed and anise, and sewn up again. They carry it on a wagon to another nation. The people who receive the body when it is brought to them do the same as the Royal Scythians: they cut off part of an ear, shave their heads all around, cut their arms all over, gash their foreheads and noses, and drive arrows through their left hands. From there they carry the king's body on the wagon to another of the nations they rule, and the people they visited before go along with them. When they have carried the body around to all of them, they are in the land of the Gerrhi, who live farthest away of all the nations they rule, and they have reached the place of burial. Then they lay the body in the tomb on a bed of leaves, plant spears on either side of it, lay pieces of wood across them, and roof the place over with matting. In the rest of the space in the tomb they strangle and bury one of the king's concubines, his cupbearer, his cook, his groom, his personal servant and his messenger, and also horses, and first portions of everything else, and gold cups; they use no silver at all, and no bronze either. When they have done this, they all work together to heap up a great mound, competing with one another and striving hard to make it as large as possible.

72. Then, when the year comes around again, they do this. They take the most capable of the king's remaining servants. These are native Scythians, since the king is served by whomever he himself orders to serve him; his servants are not bought with money. Of these attendants they strangle fifty, and also fifty of the finest horses. They take out their guts, clean out the belly, fill it with chaff and sew it up again. Then they fix half of a wheel, hollow side up, on two stakes, and the other half of the wheel on two other stakes, and they set up a number of these in this way. After that they run thick poles through the horses lengthwise, up to the neck, and mount them on the half-wheels. The front half-wheels hold up the horses' shoulders, and the back ones support their bellies near the thighs, and both the front and back legs hang in the air. They put bridles and bits on the horses, pull the reins tight in front of them, and tie them to pegs. Then they take each of the fifty strangled young men and mount him on his horse. To do this they first run a straight stake through each body along the spine up to the neck. Part of this stake sticks out at the bottom, and they fix it into a socket in the other pole, the one that runs through the horse. When they have set horsemen like these in a circle around the tomb, they ride away.

73. That is how they bury their kings. As for the other Scythians, when they die their nearest relatives lay them in wagons and carry them around to their friends one after another. Each friend, when he receives the body, gives a feast for the people who come with it, and serves the dead man about the same amount of every dish as he serves the others. Ordinary people are carried around like this for forty days, and then they are buried. After the burial the Scythians purify themselves in this way. They soap their heads and wash them well. Then, for their bodies, they set up three poles leaning toward one another and stretch woolen felt blankets around them. When they have closed these up as tightly as they can, they throw red-hot stones into a basin set in the middle, among the poles and the felt blankets.

74. Now, hemp grows in their country. It is very much like flax, except in thickness and height, and in these the hemp is far greater. It grows both wild and under cultivation. The Thracians even make clothes out of it, very much like linen ones, so that anyone who did not know it well could not tell whether the clothes were made of flax or of hemp. And someone who had never seen hemp cloth before would think the garment was made of flax.

75. The Scythians take the seed of this hemp, creep in under the felt blankets, and throw the seed onto the red-hot stones. It smolders like incense and gives off so thick a vapor that no steam bath in Greece could beat it. The Scythians are delighted with this steam bath and howl like wolves. It takes the place of washing for them, since in fact they never wash their bodies in water at all. Their women, though, grind cypress, cedar and frankincense wood on a rough stone, adding water as they grind, and they plaster this thick paste all over their bodies and faces. This not only makes them smell sweet, but when they take the plaster off the next day, their skin is clean and shining.
