Chapter 3: Sparta, the War of Croesus with Cyrus, and the Fall of Sardis

65. That was the state of things Croesus heard was going on among the Athenians at this time. About the Spartans, he heard that they had come through great troubles and had now got the upper hand over the Tegeans in their war. For back when Leon and Hegesicles were kings of Sparta, the Spartans had done well in all their other wars, but had met with disaster in just one: the war they fought against the men of Tegea, a city in Arcadia, their neighbor to the north. What is more, in the times before this, the Spartans had had the worst laws of almost any people in Greece, both in their dealings among themselves and in that they would have nothing to do with strangers. And this is how they changed over to good government. Lycurgus, a Spartan who was highly respected, came to the oracle at Delphi, and as soon as he entered the inner hall of the temple, the Pythia said this:

	"You have come, Lycurgus, to my rich temple,
	dear to Zeus and to all who live in the halls of Olympus.
	Shall I call you a god or a man? My prophet's voice is in doubt,
	god or man; but rather a god, I think, Lycurgus."

66. Some people say that, on top of this, the Pythia also laid out for him the whole system of government the Spartans have now. But the Spartans themselves say that Lycurgus brought it from Crete, when he became guardian of his nephew Leobotes, who was king of the Spartans. As soon as he became guardian, he changed all the laws then in force, and he made sure that nobody broke the new ones he set up. After this Lycurgus organized their military life: the sworn bands called enomoties, the companies of thirty, and the common meals where the men ate together. Besides this he set up the ephors, the five officials elected each year, and the Council of Elders. With these changes the Spartans came to have good laws. When Lycurgus died they built a temple to him, and they still honor him greatly. So, as you would expect in a fertile land with a good number of men living in it, they shot up at once and grew prosperous. Then sitting still was no longer enough for them. They thought they were stronger than the Arcadians, so they asked the oracle at Delphi about conquering the whole of Arcadia. The Pythia answered them like this:

	"You ask me for Arcadia. You ask a great deal; I will not give it.
	There are many men in Arcadia, tough men who live on acorns,
	and they will stop you. But I do not grudge you everything:
	I will give you Tegea, to dance on with stamping feet,
	and her fair plain, to measure out with a line and divide."

When the Spartans heard this report, they left the rest of the Arcadians alone and marched against the Tegeans, carrying chains with them. They trusted a deceptive oracle and expected to make slaves of the men of Tegea. But they were beaten in the fight, and the ones who were taken alive were put to work wearing the very chains they had brought with them, "measuring out with a line" the plain of the Tegeans after all. Those chains they had been bound with were still kept at Tegea in my own time, hanging around the temple of Athena Alea.

67. So in that earlier war, I say, they kept fighting the Tegeans and kept losing. But in Croesus' time, when Anaxandrides and Ariston were kings at Sparta, the Spartans had at last come out on top in the war, and this is how it happened. Since they were always being beaten by the men of Tegea, they sent messengers to consult the oracle at Delphi and asked which god they should win over in order to defeat the Tegeans. The Pythia answered that they must bring home to their own land the bones of Orestes son of Agamemnon. But they could not find Orestes' grave, so they sent again to ask the god where Orestes lay buried. When the messengers asked this, the priestess said:

	"There is a Tegea in the land of Arcadia, built on level ground,
	where two winds blow, driven by hard force together,
	and there is blow and answering blow, and trouble laid on trouble.
	There the son of Agamemnon lies in the life-giving earth.
	Bring him home with you, and you will be master of Tegea."

When the Spartans heard this, they were still just as far from finding the grave, though they searched everywhere, until Lichas, one of the Spartans called "Well-doers," discovered it. The "Well-doers" are the oldest citizens leaving the ranks of the "Horsemen," the king's guard of young men, five of them each year. During the year in which they leave the "Horsemen," they are bound to let the Spartan state send them, without a break, wherever it needs them.

68. Lichas was one of these men, and he found the grave in Tegea by a mix of luck and cleverness. At that time there was a truce and the two sides had dealings with each other, and he had come into a smithy in Tegea and was watching iron being worked. He was amazed at what he saw being done. The smith noticed how amazed he was, stopped working, and said: "Well, my Spartan friend, if you had seen what I once saw, you would really have something to marvel at, since you marvel so much now at ironwork. I wanted to dig a well in this yard, and as I was digging I came upon a coffin ten and a half feet long. I didn't believe there had ever been men bigger than the men of today, so I opened it, and I saw that the body was as long as the coffin. I measured it, and then I filled the earth back in over it." So the smith told him what he had seen. Lichas thought over what he had been told and guessed that this was Orestes, as the oracle had said. He reasoned it out like this. The smith had two bellows, and Lichas concluded that these were the two winds; the anvil and the hammer were the blow and the answering blow; and the iron being worked was the trouble laid on trouble, since, he thought, iron was discovered to be an evil for mankind. Having worked this out, he went back to Sparta and told the Spartans the whole thing. They then brought a trumped-up charge against him and drove him into exile. So he came to Tegea, told the smith about his bad luck, and tried to rent the yard from him. At first the smith would not let him have it, but in the end Lichas talked him into it, and he moved in. Then he dug up the grave, gathered up the bones, and took them away to Sparta. From that time on, whenever the two sides tested each other in battle, the Spartans had much the better of the war. And by now they had also conquered most of the rest of the Peloponnese.

69. So Croesus learned all this, and he sent messengers to Sparta with gifts, to ask for an alliance, after telling them exactly what to say. When they arrived, they said: "Croesus, king of the Lydians and of other nations, has sent us here, and this is his message: 'Spartans, the god told me in an oracle to make the Greek my friend. Now I am told that you are the leaders of Greece. So, as the oracle says, I invite you to be my friends and allies, with no trickery and no deceit.'" That was Croesus' message to the Spartans through his messengers. The Spartans had heard about the oracle given to Croesus themselves, and they were glad the Lydians had come, and they swore oaths of friendship and alliance. They were also bound to Croesus by some favors he had done them before this. The Spartans had sent to Sardis to buy gold, which they meant to use for the statue of Apollo that now stands on Mount Thornax in Laconia, and when they asked to buy it, Croesus gave it to them as a gift.

70. That was one reason the Spartans accepted the alliance; the other was that he had chosen them as his friends over all the other Greeks. Not only were they ready to answer his call themselves, they also had a bronze mixing bowl made, covered with figures around the outside of the rim and big enough to hold three hundred amphoras, about 3,000 gallons. They sent it off, wanting to give it to Croesus as a gift in return. This bowl never reached Sardis, and two stories are told about why. The Spartans say that when the bowl was on its way to Sardis and came off the island of Samos, the Samians heard about it, sailed out in warships, and seized it. But the Samians themselves say that the Spartans who were carrying the bowl found they were too late: they heard that Sardis had fallen and Croesus was a prisoner, so they sold the bowl on Samos. Some private citizens bought it, they say, and dedicated it in the temple of Hera. And it is likely that the men who sold it said, when they got back to Sparta, that the Samians had robbed them of it.

71. So that is what happened with the mixing bowl. Meanwhile Croesus, who had mistaken the meaning of the oracle, was preparing to invade Cappadocia, expecting to overthrow Cyrus and the power of the Persians. While he was getting ready to march against the Persians, one of the Lydians gave him some advice. This man had been thought wise even before, but because of what he said now he won a very great name for wisdom among the Lydians. His name was Sandanis, and this is what he said: "My king, you are getting ready to march against men who wear trousers of leather, and the rest of their clothes are leather too. They eat not what they want but what they can get, because the land they live in is rough. What is more, they don't drink wine; they drink water. They have no figs for dessert, or anything else that is good. Now, if you beat them, what will you take from them, since they have nothing? But if you are beaten, think how many good things you will lose. Once they have had a taste of our good things, they will hold on to them tight, and there will be no driving them away. For my part, I thank the gods that they don't put it into the Persians' heads to march against the Lydians." That was his speech, but he did not persuade Croesus. And it is true that before the Persians conquered the Lydians they had no luxuries and nothing good at all.

72. The Greeks call the Cappadocians Syrians. Before the Persians came to power, these Syrians were subjects of the Medes, but at this time they were subjects of Cyrus. For the border between the Median empire and the Lydian was the river Halys. The Halys flows down from the mountains of Armenia through the land of the Cilicians. As it flows on, it has the Matienians on its right and the Phrygians on the other side. Then, passing by these and turning north, it marks the border with the Cappadocian Syrians on one side and the Paphlagonians on the left. In this way the river Halys cuts off from the rest of Asia almost all of its lower part, by a line running from the sea opposite Cyprus to the Black Sea. This stretch of land is the neck of the whole peninsula, and the distance across it is a five days' journey for a man traveling light.

73. Croesus was marching into Cappadocia for these reasons. First, he wanted to add the land to what he already had. But above all he trusted the oracle, and he wanted to punish Cyrus on behalf of Astyages. For Cyrus son of Cambyses had conquered Astyages and was holding him prisoner, and Astyages, the king of the Medes, was Croesus' brother-in-law. This is how he had become Croesus' brother-in-law. A horde of nomad Scythians, who had quarreled with the rest of their people, left and took refuge in the land of the Medes. At that time the ruler of the Medes was Cyaxares son of Phraortes son of Deioces. At first he treated these Scythians well, since they had come to him asking for protection. He thought very highly of them, and he handed some boys over to them to learn their language and the art of archery. As time went on, the Scythians would go out hunting all the time and always bring something back. Then once it happened that they caught nothing. When they came back empty-handed, Cyaxares (who was not, as he showed on this occasion, a man of the best temper) treated them very harshly and insulted them. After this treatment from Cyaxares, the Scythians felt they had been shamed. So they made a plan: they would kill one of the boys they were teaching and cut him up, prepare his flesh the way they used to prepare wild game, and bring it to Cyaxares as if it were game from the hunt. Once they had given it to him, they meant to get away as fast as they could to Alyattes son of Sadyattes at Sardis. And that is what was done. Cyaxares and the guests who ate at his table tasted that meat, and the Scythians, having done this, went to Alyattes and asked for his protection.

74. After this, since Alyattes would not hand the Scythians over when Cyaxares demanded them, war broke out between the Lydians and the Medes and went on for five years. In those years the Medes often beat the Lydians and the Lydians often beat the Medes, and among other battles they even fought one at night. They were still at war, and evenly matched, when in the sixth year a battle took place in which, after the fighting had begun, the day suddenly turned into night. Thales of Miletus had predicted this change in the day to the Ionians, naming as its limit the very year in which it actually happened. When the Lydians and the Medes saw night come on in place of day, they stopped fighting, and both sides became much more eager to make peace. The men who brought about the peace between them were Syennesis the Cilician and Labynetus the Babylonian. They were the ones who pressed them to swear an oath, and they arranged an exchange of marriages: they decided that Alyattes should give his daughter Aryenis to Astyages son of Cyaxares, since without some strong bond to hold them, agreements tend not to hold firm. These nations swear oaths with the same ceremonies as the Greeks, but in addition they cut the skin of their arms and lick up each other's blood.

75. This was the Astyages, then, the father of Cyrus' mother, whom Cyrus had conquered and taken prisoner, for a reason I will explain later in my history. This was the grievance Croesus had against Cyrus when he sent to the oracles to ask whether he should march against the Persians. And when a deceptive answer came back, he marched into the Persians' territory, thinking the answer was in his favor. When Croesus reached the river Halys, he took his army across, in my own account, by the bridges that were there. But according to the story most Greeks tell, Thales of Miletus got the army across for him. When Croesus was at a loss how to get his army over the river (for, they add, the bridges that are there now did not yet exist at that time), Thales, who was there with the army, made the river, which was flowing on the army's left, flow on its right as well. He did it like this, they say. Starting upstream of the camp, he dug a deep channel and curved it around like a crescent moon, so that the river would be turned out of its old bed into the channel, flow around behind the camp where it was pitched, and, once past the camp, fall back into its old bed. So as soon as the river was split in two, both branches could be waded. Some even say that the old bed of the river dried up completely. But I don't accept this story as true, because how did they cross the river on the way back?

76. When Croesus had crossed over with his army, he came to the place in Cappadocia called Pteria. Pteria is the strongest place in this region, and it lies roughly in line with the city of Sinope on the Black Sea. There he made camp and laid waste the farms of the Syrians. He also took the Pterians' city and sold its people as slaves, and he took all the towns around it too, and he drove the Syrians from their homes, though they had done him no wrong. Meanwhile Cyrus had gathered his own army, picked up in addition everyone who lived in the lands along the way, and was coming to meet Croesus. But before he set out with his army, he had sent heralds to the Ionians and tried to persuade them to revolt from Croesus; the Ionians would not do it. Then, when Cyrus had arrived and camped facing Croesus, the two sides tested their strength against each other in the land of Pteria. The fighting was hard, and many fell on both sides. In the end night came on, and they parted with neither side the winner.

77. That is how the two armies fought it out. Croesus blamed his own army for its size, since the army he fought with was far smaller than Cyrus'. Being unhappy with it for this reason, when Cyrus made no attempt to attack him the next day, he marched back to Sardis. He meant to call on the Egyptians to help him, according to the oath they had sworn, since he had made an alliance with Amasis, king of Egypt, even before his alliance with the Spartans. He meant to summon the Babylonians as well, since he had made an alliance with them too, and Labynetus was ruler of the Babylonians at that time. And he meant to send word to the Spartans to come at a set time. Once he had gathered all these together along with his own army, his plan was to let the winter pass and then march against the Persians at the start of spring. With this in mind, as soon as he reached Sardis he began sending heralds to each of his allies, telling them to gather at Sardis in the fifth month from then. But the army he had with him, the one that had fought the Persians, which was made up of hired soldiers, he dismissed and sent away completely. He never expected that Cyrus, after fighting such an even battle with him, would march on Sardis after all.

78. While Croesus was making these plans, the outskirts of the city suddenly filled up with snakes. When the snakes appeared, the horses left off grazing in their pastures and kept going there and eating them. When Croesus saw this, he believed it was an omen, as indeed it was. So he sent messengers at once to the Telmessians, who interpret omens. The messengers he sent to consult them got there and learned from the Telmessians what the omen meant, but they never managed to report the answer to Croesus. For before they sailed back to Sardis, Croesus had been taken prisoner. The Telmessians' verdict was this: Croesus should expect an army speaking a foreign language to invade his land, and when it came it would conquer the people who lived there. For the snake, they said, is born of the land, while the horse is an enemy and a stranger. That was the answer the men of Telmessus gave Croesus when he was already a prisoner, though they did not yet know anything of what had happened to Sardis or to Croesus himself.

79. But Cyrus, as soon as Croesus marched away after the battle in Pteria, learned that Croesus meant to disband his army once he had marched off. He thought it over and decided that the best thing for him was to march to Sardis as fast as he could, before the Lydians' strength could be gathered together again. Once he had decided this, he did it without delay. He led his army into Lydia so fast that he himself was the first to bring Croesus the news that he was coming. Croesus was now in a desperate position, since things had turned out the exact opposite of what he expected. Even so, he led the Lydians out to battle. At that time there was no nation in Asia braver or tougher in battle than the Lydians. They fought on horseback, carrying long spears, and they were excellent horsemen.

80. The armies met on the plain in front of the city of Sardis. It is a wide, open plain, with rivers running through it, especially the Hyllus, which all rush down to join the largest of them, the Hermus. The Hermus flows from the mountain sacred to the Mother goddess, called Dindymene, and runs out into the sea near the city of Phocaea. When Cyrus saw the Lydians lining up for battle, he was afraid of their cavalry, and on the advice of Harpagus, a Mede, he did this. He gathered together all the camels that were traveling with his army carrying food and baggage. He took off their loads and put men on them, equipped as cavalry. When he had fitted them out, he ordered them to go in front of the rest of the army, toward Croesus' horsemen. He ordered the infantry to follow behind the camels, and behind the infantry he placed all his cavalry. When all his men were in their places, he gave them orders to spare none of the Lydians, but to kill everyone who got in their way; only Croesus himself they were not to kill, not even if he fought back when they caught him. Those were his orders. He set the camels facing the horsemen for this reason: horses are afraid of camels and cannot stand either the sight of them or their smell. That was why the trick had been thought up, so that Croesus' cavalry would be useless, the very force with which the Lydian king expected to win the most glory. And when they came together for battle, as soon as the horses smelled the camels and saw them, they wheeled around and bolted, and all Croesus' hopes came to nothing at once. Even so, the Lydians did not turn cowards. When they saw what was happening, they jumped down from their horses and fought the Persians on foot. In the end, after many had fallen on both sides, the Lydians turned and fled. They were driven back inside the walls of their citadel and besieged there by the Persians.

81. So the Persians laid siege to them. But Croesus, thinking the siege would last a long time, sent more messengers from the citadel to his allies. The first messengers had gone around to tell them to gather at Sardis in the fifth month. But these he was sending to ask them to come to his help as fast as they could, because Croesus was under siege.

82. He sent to his other allies, and he sent to Sparta as well. But the Spartans too, as it happened, had a quarrel of their own on their hands at that very moment, with the Argives, over the district called Thyrea. This Thyrea was part of the Argives' territory, but the Spartans had cut it off and taken it for themselves. At that time the whole region to the west, as far down as Cape Malea, belonged to the Argives, both the land on the mainland and the island of Cythera and the other islands. When the Argives came out to save their territory from being cut away from them, the two sides met to talk and agreed that three hundred men from each side would fight, and whichever side won that fight would have the disputed land. They also agreed that the main body of each army would go back to its own country and not stay to watch the contest. They were afraid that if the armies were there, one side, seeing its own men losing, would come up to help them. When they had made this agreement, the armies withdrew, and the chosen men from both sides were left behind and fought each other. They fought and turned out to be evenly matched. In the end, out of six hundred men, only three were left: Alcenor and Chromius on the Argive side, and Othryades on the Spartan side. These three were still alive when night came on. The two Argives, thinking they had won, ran off to Argos. But the Spartan Othryades stripped the armor from the Argive dead, carried it to his own camp, and stayed at his post. The next day both sides came back to find out the result. For a while each claimed the victory. One side said more of their men had survived. The other side said those men had run away, while their own man had stood his ground and stripped the bodies of the other side's dead. In the end the argument led them to fall on each other and start fighting, and after many had fallen on both sides, the Spartans won. After this the Argives cut their hair short, though before this they had been required by law to wear it long. They made a law, with a curse on anyone who broke it, that from then on no Argive man would grow his hair long, and no Argive woman would wear gold jewelry, until they had won back Thyrea. The Spartans made the opposite law for themselves: from that time on they would wear their hair long, though before that they had not. And they say that Othryades, the one man left alive out of the three hundred, was ashamed to go back to Sparta when all his comrades had been killed, and killed himself there at Thyrea.

83. That was the state of things at Sparta when the herald from Sardis arrived to ask them to help Croesus, who was under siege. Even so, in spite of their own troubles, as soon as they heard the herald's news they were eager to go to his help. But when they had finished their preparations and their ships were ready, another message came, reporting that the Lydians' citadel had fallen and Croesus had been taken prisoner. Then, and only then, they gave up the effort, grieving over what had happened as a great disaster.

84. This is how Sardis was taken. On the fourteenth day after Croesus came under siege, Cyrus sent horsemen around to every part of his army to announce that he would give rewards to the first man to climb the wall. After this the army made an attempt, and it failed. When all the rest had given up the attack, a Mardian named Hyroeades tried to climb up on one side of the citadel where no guard had been posted. Nobody was afraid the citadel would ever be taken from that side, because there it is a sheer cliff and cannot be attacked. This was also the only part of the wall around which Meles, who had been king of Sardis in earlier times, had not carried the lion that his concubine bore him. The Telmessians had ruled that if the lion were carried around the wall, Sardis would never be captured. So Meles carried it around the rest of the wall, the parts of the citadel where the fortress could be attacked, but he skipped this part, since it was a sheer cliff and could not be attacked. This is the part of the city that faces toward Mount Tmolus. Now the day before, this Mardian Hyroeades had seen one of the Lydians climb down on that side of the citadel to fetch his helmet, which had rolled down from above, and pick it up. He noticed this and turned it over in his mind. So he climbed up himself, first of all, and other Persians came up after him. Then many more made their way up, and so Sardis was finally taken, and the whole city was plundered.

85. Meanwhile this is what happened to Croesus himself. He had a son, whom I mentioned earlier, who was a decent enough young man in other ways but could not speak. In his earlier days of good fortune, Croesus had done everything he could for him. Among the other things he tried, he had also sent messengers to Delphi to ask about him. And the Pythia told him this:

	"Lydian, lord of many, Croesus, so blind to what is fated,
	do not wish to hear in your halls that longed-for voice,
	the voice of your son. It would be far better for you to be without it,
	for the first day he speaks will be a day of disaster."

Now as the citadel was being taken, one of the Persians, not knowing who Croesus was, was about to kill him. Croesus saw him coming, but he did not care, because of the disaster that had fallen on him; it made no difference to him whether he was struck down and killed. But when this son who could not speak saw the Persian coming at him, fear and grief broke open his voice, and he said: "Man, do not kill Croesus!" That was the first time the son ever spoke, and from then on he could speak for the rest of his life.
