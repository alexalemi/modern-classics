Chapter 21: The Origins of the Scythians, and the Peoples of the Far North

1. After Babylon was taken, Darius himself marched against the Scythians. Asia was now flourishing, full of people, and large sums were coming in as revenue, so Darius conceived a desire to take revenge on the Scythians. They had been the first to invade the land of the Medes and had beaten in battle those who stood against them; so they had started the wrong. In fact, as I said before, the Scythians had ruled Upper Asia for twenty-eight years. They had invaded Asia in pursuit of the Cimmerians, and they had ended the rule of the Medes, who had ruled Asia before the Scythians came. But when the Scythians had been away from their own land for twenty-eight years and were coming home after all that time, they met a struggle no less hard than their war with the Medes. They found a sizable army drawn up against them. The reason was that the Scythians' wives, since their husbands had been away from them for so long, had been sleeping with the slaves.

2. Now, the Scythians blind all their slaves, because of the milk they drink. This is how they do it. They take blowpipes made of bone, just like flutes, insert them into the mare's vagina, and blow with their mouths, while others milk as they blow. They say they do this because blowing fills the mare's veins with air and makes the udder let down its milk. When they have drawn the milk, they pour it into hollowed-out wooden vessels, set the blind slaves around the vessels, and have them churn the milk. They skim off what rises to the top, which they think the more valuable part; what settles to the bottom they consider less good. That is why the Scythians blind everyone they capture: they are not farmers but nomads.

3. So it was from these slaves and the Scythians' wives that a generation of young men had been born and raised. When these young men learned how they had been born, they set out to resist the Scythians on their way home from Media. First they cut off their land by digging a wide trench from the Tauric mountains, the mountains of the Crimea, to Lake Maeotis, at the point where the lake is widest. Then, when the Scythians tried to invade, they took up a position against them and fought. They fought many times, and the Scythians could gain no advantage in the fighting. At last one of the Scythians said: "Scythians, what are we doing? We are fighting our own slaves. Every time we fight, more of us are killed, so there are fewer of us; and we are killing them too, so we will have fewer to rule in the future. So here is what I think we should do. Let's put down our spears and bows, and let each man take his horsewhip and go right up to them. As long as they saw us with weapons in our hands, they thought they were our equals and as well born as we are. But when they see us carrying whips instead of weapons, they will understand that they are our slaves, and once they admit that, they will not stand their ground."

4. When the Scythians heard this, they did what he said. The others were so terrified by what they saw that they forgot about fighting and ran. That is how the Scythians had ruled Asia, and how, after the Medes drove them out again, they had come back to their own land. And this is what Darius wanted to take revenge for, and why he was gathering an army to march against them.

5. The Scythians say that their nation is the youngest of all nations, and that this is how it began. The first man ever to live in their country, which was empty at that time, was a man named Targitaus. They say his parents were Zeus and the daughter of the river Borysthenes, the Dnieper. I do not believe this myself, but that is what they say. That, they say, was where Targitaus came from, and he had three sons: Lipoxais, Arpoxais, and the youngest, Colaxais. While these three were ruling, certain objects made of gold fell from the sky into the land of Scythia: a plow, a yoke, a battle-axe, and a cup. The eldest saw them first and went up to them, wanting to take them, but as he came near, the gold blazed up in fire. When he had gone away, the second brother went up to it, and the gold did the same thing again. So the gold drove these two away by blazing with fire. But when the third and youngest came up to it, the flames went out, and he carried the objects home. The two older brothers understood what this meant, and they handed over the whole of the kingship to the youngest.

6. From Lipoxais, they say, are descended the Scythians called the Auchatae clan; from the middle brother, Arpoxais, those called the Catiari and the Traspians; and from the youngest, the royal clan, called the Paralatae. All of them together, they say, are called Scoloti, after the name of their king; it was the Greeks who named them Scythians.

7. That is how the Scythians say they came into being. From their beginning, that is, from their first king, Targitaus, to Darius' crossing into their land, they say there were a thousand years and no more. The kings guard this sacred gold with the greatest care and visit it every year with solemn sacrifices to win the gods' favor. If anyone who is guarding the gold out in the open during the festival falls asleep, the Scythians say he will not live out the year; and he is given as much land as he can ride around on his horse in one day. Since the country was large, they say, Colaxais set up three kingdoms for his sons, and he made one of them larger than the others, and that is where the gold is kept. As for the regions farther north, beyond the people who live above their land, they say you cannot see any farther or travel any farther because of the feathers that pour down. The earth and the air are full of feathers, and that is what blocks the view.

8. That is what the Scythians say about themselves and about the country beyond them. But the Greeks who live around the Black Sea tell it this way. Heracles, driving the cattle of Geryon, came to this land, which was empty then and is now inhabited by the Scythians. Geryon, the story goes, lived far from the Black Sea, on the island the Greeks call Erythea, near Gadeira, which is Cádiz, outside the Pillars of Heracles, on the Ocean. (As for the Ocean, they say it flows around the whole earth, starting from where the sun rises, but they do not prove it with facts.) From there Heracles came to the land now called Scythia. A storm came over him, along with bitter cold, so he pulled his lion's skin over himself and went to sleep. While he slept, the mares that were harnessed to his chariot were grazing, and by some miraculous chance they disappeared.

9. When Heracles woke up, he went looking for them. He searched the whole country and at last came to the region called Hylaea, the Woodland. There, in a cave, he found a creature of double form, half girl and half serpent: from the buttocks up she was a woman, but below she was a snake. He looked at her in amazement and then asked whether she had seen any mares wandering anywhere. She said she had them herself and would not give them back until he had sex with her, and Heracles slept with her on condition that he would get them back. Then she kept putting off returning the mares, because she wanted to keep Heracles with her as long as she could, while he wanted to get the mares and leave. At last she gave them back and said: "When these mares wandered here, I kept them safe for you, and you have paid me for keeping them, because I am carrying three sons of yours. So tell me, what should I do with them when they grow up? Should I settle them here, since I alone rule this land, or send them away to you?" That was her question, and he answered, they say: "When you see that the boys have grown to be men, do this and you will not go wrong. Whichever of them you see can draw this bow the way I do now, and can put on this belt, make him the one who settles this land. Whichever of them fails at the tasks I am setting, send him out of the country. If you do this, you will be happy yourself, and you will have done what you were told."

10. With that he drew one of his bows (up to that time, they say, Heracles used to carry two) and showed her the belt. Then he gave her both the bow and the belt, which had a golden cup hanging from the end of its clasp, and once he had given them to her, he left. When her sons were born and had grown to be men, she first gave them names: she called one Agathyrsus, the next Gelonus, and the youngest Scythes. Then, remembering his instructions, she did what she had been told. Two of her sons, Agathyrsus and Gelonus, could not manage the task set for them, and they left the country, driven out by their own mother. But Scythes, the youngest, did the task and stayed in the land. From Scythes son of Heracles, they say, all the later kings of the Scythians are descended. And they say that it is because of that cup that the Scythians still wear cups hanging from their belts to this day. That one thing his mother arranged for Scythes' sake. That is the story told by the Greeks who live around the Black Sea.

11. But there is another story too, and this is the one I lean toward myself. It goes like this. The nomad Scythians who lived in Asia were hard pressed in war by the Massagetae, so they left their home, crossed the river Araxes, and moved toward the land of the Cimmerians. (The land the Scythians now occupy is said to have belonged to the Cimmerians in the old days.) When the Scythians were coming against them, the Cimmerians held a council, since they could see a great host coming to attack them. It turned out that they were split between two opinions, each held fiercely, though the better one was the kings'. The common people thought they ought to leave and not risk a fight against so many. The kings thought they ought to fight the invaders for their land. The people would not agree to the kings' plan, and the kings would not agree to the people's. So the people planned to leave without a fight and hand the country over to the invaders, while the kings decided to die and be buried in their own land rather than flee with the crowd. They thought of all the good fortune they had enjoyed there, and of all the misery that would likely come on them if they fled their native country. Once they had decided this, they split into two groups of equal size and fought each other. When they had all died at one another's hands, the Cimmerian people buried them beside the river Tyras, the Dniester, where their grave can still be seen. After they had buried them, they left the country, and when the Scythians arrived they found the land empty.

12. Even now there are Cimmerian walls in Scythia, and a Cimmerian ferry. There is also a region called Cimmeria, and the strait called the Cimmerian Bosporus, between the Crimea and the land to its east. It is also known that the Cimmerians, fleeing into Asia from the Scythians, settled on the peninsula where the Greek city of Sinope now stands. And it is known that the Scythians, chasing them, lost their way and invaded the land of Media. The Cimmerians kept to the coast all along as they fled, while the Scythians pursued them with the Caucasus on their right until finally they turned inland and burst into Media. So that is the other story, and Greeks and non-Greeks both tell it.

13. But Aristeas son of Caystrobius, a man of Proconnesus, said in the poem he wrote that he was seized by Phoebus Apollo and traveled to the land of the Issedonians. Beyond the Issedonians, he said, live the Arimaspians, a one-eyed people; beyond them, the griffins that guard the gold; and beyond them, the Hyperboreans, whose land stretches all the way to the sea. All of these except the Hyperboreans, starting with the Arimaspians, were always making war on their neighbors. The Issedonians were gradually pushed out of their country by the Arimaspians, the Scythians by the Issedonians, and so the Cimmerians, who lived on the southern sea, were pressed by the Scythians and left their land. So he does not agree with the Scythians' account of this country either.

14. I have already said where this Aristeas, the author of that poem, came from. Now I will also tell the story I heard about him in Proconnesus and Cyzicus. They say that Aristeas, who was as well born as any citizen there, went into a fuller's shop in Proconnesus, a cloth-cleaner's, and died there. The fuller shut up his workshop and went off to tell the dead man's relatives. The news that Aristeas was dead had spread around the city when a man of Cyzicus, who had come from the town of Artace, began to argue with the people who were saying so. He said he had met Aristeas on the road to Cyzicus and talked with him. While he was arguing hotly, the dead man's relatives came to the fuller's shop with everything they needed to take up the body for burial. But when the shop was opened, Aristeas was not there, dead or alive. In the seventh year after this he appeared in Proconnesus and composed the poem that the Greeks now call the Arimaspea, and after he had composed it he disappeared a second time.

15. That is what those cities tell. But I know that the following happened to the people of Metapontum in Italy two hundred and forty years after Aristeas disappeared the second time, as I worked out by putting together what I learned at Proconnesus and at Metapontum. The people of Metapontum say that Aristeas himself appeared in their country and told them to set up an altar to Apollo, and beside it a statue bearing the name of Aristeas of Proconnesus. He told them that theirs was the only land of all the Greeks in Italy that Apollo had come to, and that he, who was now Aristeas, had come with him; at the time when he went with the god, he had been a raven. After saying this he disappeared. The Metapontines say they sent to Delphi to ask the god what the apparition of the man meant. The Pythia told them to obey the apparition, and said that if they obeyed, it would go better for them. So they accepted this answer and did what they had been told. And there stands a statue bearing the name of Aristeas right beside the altar dedicated to Apollo, with laurel trees standing around it; the altar is set up in the marketplace. So much for Aristeas.

16. As for the land that this part of my account has begun to describe, nobody knows for certain what lies beyond it. I have not been able to hear of anyone who claims to know it as an eyewitness. Even Aristeas, the man I was just talking about, did not claim, even in his poem, that he went farther than the Issedonians. What lies beyond them he described from hearsay, and he said it was the Issedonians who told him these things. But as far as we have been able to reach certain knowledge by hearsay, pushing our inquiries as far as they would go, all of it will be told.

17. Start from the trading post of the Borysthenites, since of all the coast of Scythia this is the midpoint. Starting from there, the first land is held by the Callipidae, who are Greek Scythians. Beyond them is another people, called the Alazones. The Alazones and the Callipidae live like the Scythians in every other way, but they both sow grain and eat it, and also onions, leeks, lentils, and millet. Beyond the Alazones live Scythians who farm, and these sow their grain not to eat but to sell.

18. Beyond them live the Neuri, and beyond the Neuri, toward the north wind, the land is uninhabited, as far as we know. These peoples live along the river Hypanis, the Bug, west of the Dnieper. But once you cross the Dnieper, the first land from the coast is Hylaea. Beyond that, as you go up the river, live farming Scythians. The Greeks who live on the Bug call them Borysthenites, though they call themselves citizens of Olbia. These farming Scythians hold the land that stretches east for three days' journey, as far as a river called the Panticapes, and north for eleven days' sailing up the Dnieper. Right beyond them begins the desert, which runs a long way. On the far side of the desert live the Androphagi, the Man-eaters, a people all their own, with no connection to the Scythians. Beyond them begins land that is truly empty, with no people living in it, as far as we know.

19. East of these farming Scythians, once you cross the river Panticapes, live nomad Scythians, who neither sow anything nor plow the ground. This whole region is bare of trees except for Hylaea. These nomads hold a country that stretches east for fourteen days' journey, as far as the river Gerrhus.

20. On the other side of the Gerrhus are the lands called royal, and the Scythians who are the bravest and most numerous and who regard all the other Scythians as their slaves. These reach south as far as the Tauric land, the Crimea, and east as far as the trench dug by the sons of the blind slaves, and as far as the trading post called Cremni on Lake Maeotis. Some parts of their country reach the river Tanais, the Don. North of the Royal Scythians, toward the north wind, live the Melanchlaeni, the Black Cloaks, a different people and not Scythian. Beyond the Melanchlaeni the land is marshy and empty of people, as far as we know.

21. Once you cross the Don, you are no longer in Scythia. The first of the regions there belongs to the Sauromatae. Starting from the corner of Lake Maeotis, they hold land that stretches north toward the north wind for fifteen days' journey, completely bare of trees, both cultivated and wild. Above them, in the next region, live the Budini, whose land is entirely covered in forest of every kind of tree.

22. North of the Budini there is first desert, seven days' journey across. After the desert, turning somewhat more toward the east, you come to the land of the Thyssagetae, a large people and a separate race from the others. They live by hunting. Next to them, in the same regions, live a people called the Irycae, who also live by hunting, and this is how they do it. The hunter climbs a tree and lies in wait there for his game (there are plenty of trees all through this country). Each man keeps a horse ready, trained to lie down flat on its belly to keep low, and a dog as well. When he spots a wild animal from the tree, he first shoots his arrow, then mounts his horse and chases it, and the dog seizes it. Beyond these, toward the east, live other Scythians, who rebelled against the Royal Scythians and so came to this region.

23. As far as the country of these Scythians, all the land I have described is level plain with deep soil, but from there on it is stony and rough. After you have crossed a long stretch of this rough country, there are people living at the foot of high mountains who are said to be bald from birth, men and women alike. They have flat noses and large chins and speak a language of their own. They dress like Scythians and live on the fruit of trees. The tree whose fruit they live on is called the Pontic tree, and it is about the size of a fig tree. Its fruit is the size of a bean and has a stone in it. When the fruit is ripe, they strain it through cloths, and a thick black juice runs out of it, which is called aschy. They lick this up or drink it mixed with milk, and from the dregs, the solid part, they make cakes and eat them; they do not have many cattle, because the pasture there is not good at all. Each man lives under a tree. In winter he covers the tree all around with thick white felt, and in summer he leaves it uncovered. No one harms these people, because they are said to be sacred, and they own no weapons of war. They are also the ones who settle the quarrels that arise among their neighbors, and besides, any fugitive who takes refuge with them is harmed by no one. They are called the Argippaei.

24. As far as these bald men, there is plenty of clear information about the land and the nations on this side of them. Some Scythians travel to them, and it is not hard to get information from them, and so do some of the Greeks from the trading post on the Dnieper and the other trading posts on the Black Sea coast. The Scythians who go to them do their business through seven interpreters, in seven different languages.

25. As far as these people, then, the land is known. But nobody can say for certain what lies north of the bald men, because high mountains that no one can cross cut it off, and no one goes over them. The bald men do say (though I do not believe it) that the mountains are inhabited by men with goats' feet, and that beyond them are other people who sleep for six months of the year. This I do not accept at all. But the country east of the bald men is known for certain: the Issedonians live there. What lies north of both the bald men and the Issedonians, toward the north wind, is unknown, except for what we know from the reports of these peoples I have just mentioned.

26. The Issedonians are said to have these customs. When a man's father dies, all his relatives bring cattle to the house. They kill the animals and cut up the meat, and they also cut up the dead body of their host's father, mix all the meat together, and serve it as a feast. The skull, however, they strip of flesh, clean out, and cover with gold, and from then on they treat it as a sacred object and offer great sacrifices for the dead man every year. Each son does this for his father, just as the Greeks keep a memorial day for their dead. In other respects this people too is said to live justly, and their women have equal rights with the men.

27. So these people too are known. But as for the region beyond them, it is the Issedonians who report that one-eyed men live there, and griffins that guard the gold. The Scythians repeat this, having heard it from the Issedonians, and we, the rest of mankind, have taken our belief from the Scythians. We call these people by a Scythian name, Arimaspians, because the Scythians call the number one arima, and the eye spu.

28. The whole country I have described has a climate so harsh that for eight months of the year the frost is unbearably hard. During that time, if you pour out water, you will not make mud; you can make mud only by lighting a fire. The sea freezes, and so does the whole Cimmerian Bosporus, so that the Scythians who live inside the trench make expeditions across the ice and drive their wagons over into the country of the Sindians. So it is winter for eight months on end, and even in the other four it is cold there. This winter is different in character from the winters anywhere else in the world. In the season when rain usually falls, it hardly rains at all, while in summer it rains all the time. Thunder does not come when it comes in other countries; it is very frequent in summer, and if there is thunder in winter, people wonder at it as a portent. In the same way, if there is an earthquake in Scythia, in summer or in winter, it is taken as a portent. Horses can endure this winter, but mules and donkeys cannot endure it at all; whereas in other countries horses that stand in the frost get frostbite and lose their limbs to it, while donkeys and mules endure it.

29. I think it is also for this reason that the hornless breed of cattle there grow no horns. There is a line of Homer in the Odyssey that supports my view. It goes:

	"And Libya, where the lambs grow horns so quickly,"

and it is rightly said that in hot places horns grow quickly, while in extreme cold animals grow no horns at all, or hardly any.

30. In Scythia, then, this happens because of the cold. But (since my account has gone looking for side-stories from the start) I am puzzled that in the whole land of Elis no mules can be bred, though the region is not cold and there is no other obvious cause. The Eleans themselves say that mules are not conceived in their country because of a curse. So when the time comes for the mares to conceive, they drive them out into the neighboring lands, and there, in their neighbors' country, they put the male donkeys to them until the mares are pregnant, and then they drive them home again.

31. As for the feathers that the Scythians say fill the air, so that they cannot see or travel through the farther parts of the continent, here is my opinion. In the lands beyond Scythia it snows all the time, though less in summer than in winter, as you would expect. Anyone who has seen thick snow falling close up knows what I mean without further explanation, because snow is like feathers. And because the winter there is as harsh as I have described, the northern parts of this continent are uninhabitable. So I think that when the Scythians and their neighbors talk about feathers, they are speaking figuratively and mean snow. That, then, is as far as the reports go.

32. About a Hyperborean people the Scythians report nothing, and neither do any of the other peoples who live in that region, except perhaps the Issedonians. But in my opinion the Issedonians do not report anything either, because if they did, the Scythians would report it too, as they do about the one-eyed people. Hesiod, however, has spoken of the Hyperboreans, and so has Homer in the poem the Epigoni, at least if Homer really was the author of that epic.

33. But much the most is said about them by the people of Delos. They say that sacred offerings wrapped in wheat straw are carried from the land of the Hyperboreans and come to the Scythians. From the Scythians, each neighboring nation in turn receives them and carries them westward, and they end up as far west as the Adriatic. From there they are sent on south, and the people of Dodona are the first of the Greeks to receive them. From Dodona they come down to the Malian Gulf and are carried across to Euboea, where city passes them on to city as far as Carystus. After that Andros is skipped, because the Carystians carry them to Tenos, and the Tenians carry them to Delos. That, they say, is how these sacred offerings come to Delos. But in the beginning, they say, the Hyperboreans sent two girls to carry the offerings, whose names, according to the Delians, were Hyperoche and Laodice. With them, to protect them, the Hyperboreans sent five men of their own people as an escort, the ones now called the Perpherees, the Bearers, who are greatly honored on Delos. But since the people they had sent out never came back, the Hyperboreans were troubled at the thought that it would always be their lot to send people out and never get them back. So they carried the offerings, wrapped in wheat straw, to the border of their country and charged their neighbors to send them on from there to the next nation. That, they say, is how these things are passed along until they reach Delos. And I know for myself that something similar to these offerings is done: the women of Thrace and Paeonia, when they sacrifice to Artemis the Queen, never make their offerings without wheat straw.

34. These women, I know, do as I have said. And for the Hyperborean girls who died on Delos, the girls and boys of Delos cut their hair. The girls, before their marriage, cut off a lock, wind it around a spindle, and lay it on the tomb (the tomb is on the left as you go into the temple of Artemis, and an olive tree grows over it). All the Delian boys wind some of their hair around a green shoot of some plant and lay it on the tomb as well.

35. That is the honor the people of Delos pay these girls. The same people say that Arge and Opis also came to Delos from the Hyperboreans, passing through the same nations I have named, and that they came even before Hyperoche and Laodice. Hyperoche and Laodice, they say, came bringing Eileithyia, the goddess of childbirth, the tribute their people had pledged in return for a quick and easy birth. But Arge and Opis came along with the gods themselves, and the people of Delos have given them honors of a different kind. The women, they say, take up a collection for them and call on them by name in the hymn that Olen, a man from Lycia, composed in their honor. The people of the other islands and the Ionians have learned from the Delians to sing hymns calling on Opis and Arge by name and to take up collections. (This Olen came from Lycia and also composed the other ancient hymns that are sung on Delos.) They say, too, that when the thighbones of the sacrificed animals have been burned on the altar, their ashes are thrown on the grave of Opis and Arge. Their grave is behind the temple of Artemis, facing east, close to the banquet hall of the Ceans.

36. That is enough about the Hyperboreans. I will not tell the story of Abaris, who is said to have been a Hyperborean, and how he carried the arrow all over the earth without eating anything. But if there are Hyperboreans, people beyond the north wind, then there must also be Hypernotians, people beyond the south wind. And I laugh when I see how many people before now have drawn maps of the earth, and not one of them has explained it sensibly. They draw Ocean flowing all around the earth, and the earth perfectly round, as if drawn with a compass, and they make Asia the same size as Europe. In a few words I will show how big each of these divisions is and what shape each one has.
