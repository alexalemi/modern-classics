Herodotus came from Halicarnassus, a Greek city on the coast of what is now Turkey and at that time part of the Persian empire. He wrote around 430 BC, fifty years after the war his book builds toward. His purpose is in his first sentence: to set down what he had found out, so that great deeds would not fade with time, and above all so that the reason the Greeks and the Persians fought each other would be preserved. Nobody had written a book like it before. Cicero called him the father of history and the name stuck.

He takes his time getting to the war. Book One is mostly about Croesus of Lydia, a rich king who misread an oracle and lost his kingdom to the Persians. Book Two is Egypt from end to end — the Nile, the pyramids, mummification, the crocodile, the priests and what they eat. Book Four is Scythia and Libya: burial mounds, scalps used as hand towels, gold-digging ants bigger than foxes. Then the Persians move west, the Greek cities of Asia revolt, Darius sends an army that is beaten at Marathon in 490 BC, and ten years later his son Xerxes comes with everything he has. The nine books are named after the Muses, a tidy piece of work by later editors rather than by Herodotus.

The digressions are the book, not an interruption of it, and the reason is his method. He collects. He tells you who told him a story, gives you two or three versions when he has them, and says plainly when he does not believe what he has just written down — his rule is that he is obliged to report what people say and not obliged to believe it. Thucydides, writing a generation later about a war he had fought in, worked the opposite way: he composed the speeches himself and stated a method at the start. That history is in this collection too, and the two men together are where the whole business of writing history comes from.

What most readers come for is in Books Seven to Nine. Xerxes has the Hellespont whipped for wrecking his bridges. Three hundred Spartans hold the pass at Thermopylae. The Greek fleet fights in the narrows at Salamis while the king watches from a throne on the shore, and the following summer the armies meet at Plataea. Herodotus is at his best there and he is still the same man who wanted to know how they embalm a cat.

This edition is the complete work, all nine books and every digression, modernized from G. C. Macaulay's English translation of 1890.
