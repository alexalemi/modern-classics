TEXT ANALYSIS — HERODOTUS, THE HISTORIES
Retranslation, set up 2026-10-10. Supersedes legacy_v1/text_analysis.txt.

This is a scholarly modernization of a canonical historical text: the
founding work of Western history, written c. 430 BC, reaching us through
G. C. Macaulay's English translation of 1890. Our job is to take Macaulay's
Victorian English off Herodotus and give the reader Herodotus whole, in
present-day English a fourteen-year-old can read aloud and follow.


=== 0. WHY THIS IS A RETRANSLATION ===

The first modernization (Feb 2026, now legacy_v1/) was made in 25 files of
10-17k words each, before the project capped a file at about 7k words. A
translating agent must WRITE as much as it reads, and those agents could
not: every file came back at 0.66-0.81 of the source, and the army
catalogue (VII.61-99) was plainly summarized: about 35 named commanders in
Macaulay, 9 in the modern text, whole peoples gone. Nothing on the page
announced the loss. The modern text read smoothly; it simply was not all
there.

So the rule that governs everything below is COMPLETENESS. Every section,
every sentence, every name, every number, every "as the priests told me",
every version of a story Herodotus gives two of. The new files are 3.8-7.0k
words, and check.py now counts Herodotus' own section numbers and the
proper names in each file, so a dropped section fails outright and a
thinned catalogue shows up as a list of missing names.


=== 1. THE SOURCE AND HOW IT WAS PREPARED ===

Source: Standard Ebooks' text of Macaulay (fetch.sh pins the commit), built
by SE from Gutenberg #2707/#2456 against the 1890 scans. The old
herodotus.txt (Gutenberg-derived) is kept as the second witness; check.py
holds each Book's word count to within 0.5% of it (they agree to 0.1%).

Apparatus. Macaulay's 1,454 notes (21.6k words) are textual criticism:
Greek readings, manuscript variants, "or 'because she was ashamed'". They
are the translator's apparatus, not the author's book, and they are
dropped, with their reference numbers, as is his preface. The editor's
introduction does their job for a modern reader. Where a note would have
helped (a place, a custom, a god's Greek name), the storyteller lets the
sentence carry a few words of gloss, as in every book in this collection.

Section numbers. Macaulay prints the traditional chapter numbers ("43.")
that every edition and every scholar uses to cite Herodotus ("Hdt. 7.228").
prep.py starts a paragraph at each one, so chapters/NNN.txt reads
"43. Then when the army had come to the river Scamander ...". The agents
KEEP them: each modern section opens a paragraph with the same number, so
check.py can prove every section is present, once, in order. A section may
run to several modern paragraphs; only the first carries the number.
About 130 of Macaulay's sections begin mid-sentence ("192. and as to the
resources ..."), because the traditional division cuts the Greek where it
cuts it: in the modern text, end the previous sentence and open the new
section on a sentence of its own. Whether the numbers SHOW on the page is
a separate question, for Alex (see the setup report); keeping them in the
files costs nothing either way.

VII.35, the whipping of the Hellespont, has no number in either witness:
Macaulay ran it on into VII.34. It is there, inside 34, and must be
translated; check.py knows the exception.

Verse. The oracles and inscriptions (151 lines in 41 passages) are set as
tab-indented lines, the house convention (quixote/, nights/), which renders
as verse. See section 6.

Structure. 51 files, each a whole chapter of 3.8-7.0k words, cut at
narrative joints (never mid-story, never mid-sentence), under nine word-form
Book dividers, "Book One: Clio" ... "Book Nine: Calliope", which assemble.py
emits from the manifest. Chapter titles are editorial, new writing, as the
Peloponnesian War's are; Herodotus' Books have no chapters of their own.
Numbering runs 1-51 through the whole work (assemble.py anchors chapters as
ch-N, so restarting per Book would collide). manifest.json carries each
file's section range ("I.1-33").


=== 2. WHO HERODOTUS IS ON THE PAGE ===

Herodotus is a storyteller first and an inquirer always. His Greek was
plain, fluent Ionic, not archaic even to his own readers (Macaulay's preface
says so), and it moves fast. Macaulay, aiming at "faithfulness to the
manner of expression and to the structure of sentences", keeps the Greek
word order and particles: "having so done", "these, I say", "that which",
"forthwith", "thou", the delayed main verb. That is the costume we remove.
It is the TRANSLATOR's archaism, not the author's (see memory
whose-archaism-is-it): the Greek reader of 430 BC heard a lively voice, and
so should ours.

His registers, and what to do with each:

NARRATIVE. Wars, successions, intrigues. Chronological, cause-and-effect,
"and after this ... and then". Keep the momentum. Break Macaulay's 80-word
chains into sentences a reader can hold, and make the connections explicit
("so", "because", "but then"). Do not add scene-painting, emotion or motive
he did not write. He is often laconic about horrors: stay laconic.

THE INQUIRER'S VOICE. The single most precious thing in the book, and what
the old version thinned first. "As I was told by the priests at Memphis",
"the Persians say ... but the Phoenicians say ...", "this I saw myself",
"I do not believe it, but others may", "I cannot say for certain", "so
much for that", "I have mentioned this before", "my account has from the
start sought out the side-issues". Every one stays, in first person, in
his tone: curious, skeptical, fair, occasionally pleased with himself.
These are not padding; they ARE his method. When he gives two or three
versions of a story, all of them stay, in his order, with his verdict.

ETHNOGRAPHY AND MARVELS. Egypt, Scythia, Libya, India, Arabia, the edges
of the world. Concrete, specific, wonder-struck and skeptical by turns.
Render like the best travel writing: exact (every measurement, every
ingredient, every step of the embalming), and never mocking. Report what
he reports as his report.

SPEECHES AND DIALOGUE. Solon and Croesus, Artabanus and Xerxes, the
debate on government, Demaratus, Socles, Themistocles. Short, pointed,
often epigrammatic. Make them sound like people talking, keep their
argument whole, and keep the sharpness of the punchline. Do NOT turn
reported speech into direct speech or the reverse: when Herodotus says
that Dieneces said they would fight in the shade, he says it indirectly.

LISTS. Catalogues of peoples and commanders (VII.61-99), of ships
(VIII.1-2, VIII.43-48), of contingents and battle lines (IX.28-32), of
tribute (III.89-97), of the stages of a march, of the kings of Egypt, of
the suitors of Agariste (VI.127). These are the passages most at risk and
they must survive name for name, number for number, and in order. A
catalogue may be set more readably (shorter sentences, one people per
sentence), never shortened.


=== 3. READABILITY (THE DELIVERABLE) ===

Alex's standing correction (memory: readability-is-the-deliverable): a
faithful-but-opaque draft is a crib. Every sentence should be one a bright
fourteen-year-old can read aloud and follow, with every clause of the source
present and nothing summarized.

- Plain words. "forthwith" -> at once; "having so done" -> after this /
  then; "that which" -> what; "inasmuch as" -> since; "it chanced that" ->
  it happened that / narrate directly; "the greater part of" -> most of;
  "made answer" -> answered; "set forth" -> explained / set out (by sense).
- Modern word order; active voice where it is clearer.
- thou/thee/thy/ye -> you, and every -est/-eth form goes.
- Where Herodotus leans on a story his Greek readers knew (Io, the Argonauts,
  Helen, the Heracleidae, Minos), let the sentence carry what the reader
  needs in a few words. No footnotes, no brackets.
- Patronymics are information and stay: "Leonidas son of Anaxandrides".
  In a catalogue every one stays. In running narrative, where Macaulay
  repeats a patronymic within a few sentences purely as Greek idiom, the
  repeat may go; the first mention in each file keeps it.


=== 4. LOCKED VOCABULARY ===

The full table is in running_notes.txt; it is the ledger and wins over
anything here. The decisions behind it:

NAMES. Conventional Latinized English forms, as in the published
introduction and the old version: Darius, Croesus, Cyrus, Cambyses, Xerxes,
Mardonius, Histiaeus, Psammetichus, Pisistratus, Miletus, Plataea,
Thermopylae, Artemisium, Aegina, Cyrene, Athena, Dionysus, Halicarnassus.
Macaulay's Greek-style forms (-os, k, -ai, -oi) are converted:
Mardonios -> Mardonius, Kyrene -> Cyrene, Plataia -> Plataea, Thermopylai
-> Thermopylae, Alcmaionidai -> Alcmaeonids. Rare names with no settled
English form: Latinize by the same rule (-os -> -us, k -> c, ai -> ae,
oi -> oe or -i in plurals) and record the choice in your notes.

PEOPLES AND PLACES.
- Hellenes / Hellas / Hellenic -> Greeks / Greece / Greek, always.
- Barbarians. Herodotus' "barbaroi" means non-Greeks, anyone whose language
  is not Greek; it is not an insult, and Herodotus admires many of them. In
  the proem: "Greeks and non-Greeks". Elsewhere by referent: "non-Greeks" /
  "foreigners" for the category; for Xerxes' army, "the Persians", "the
  king's army", "the invaders", "the enemy" (it is a multi-national host:
  "the Persians" is the Greeks' own shorthand and is acceptable). Do not use
  "barbarian(s)" except where a Greek speaker is plainly being contemptuous.
- Lacedaemonians / Lacedaemon -> Spartans / Sparta (the state) or Laconia
  (the territory). EXCEPTION: where Herodotus counts Spartiates against the
  other Lacedaemonians (Thermopylae VII.202, 228; Plataea IX.10-11, 28-29,
  61, 85), keep the distinction: "Spartans" or "Spartan citizens" for the
  Spartiates, "other Lacedaemonians (free men of the Laconian towns, not
  citizens of Sparta)" glossed once, then "Lacedaemonians".
- The Mede / the Medes: the Medes are a real people (Book I, the
  catalogue) and stay Medes. Where Greeks call the Persian enemy "the Mede"
  it becomes "the Persians"; "medize" -> "go over to the Persians".
- Magians -> the Magi (one Magus).
- Rivers and seas take their modern names where the identification is
  certain: Ister -> the Danube; Borysthenes -> the Dnieper; Tanais -> the
  Don; Tyras -> the Dniester; Hypanis -> the Bug; Pontus / Euxine -> the
  Black Sea; Arabian Gulf -> the Red Sea; Erythraean Sea -> the Persian
  Gulf where Herodotus means its head (Euphrates and Tigris mouths, Susa,
  Ampe, the Phoenicians' origin in I.1), otherwise the Indian Ocean (his
  "Southern Sea"). Where the identification is disputed (Araxes, Gerrhus,
  Oarus, Lycus, the Scythian rivers in IV.48-57 other than those above),
  keep Herodotus' name. Hellespont, Bosporus, Propontis (the Sea of
  Marmara, glossed once), Nile, Halys, Euphrates stay.
- Peoples and cities keep Herodotus' names; Ethiopians stay Ethiopians (his
  word for the dark-skinned peoples south of Egypt and in the far east).
- Ilion -> Troy.

GODS. Greek names (Zeus, Apollo, Athena, Heracles, Dionysus, Hephaestus,
Poseidon, Aphrodite). Herodotus' identifications of foreign gods with Greek
ones stay as he makes them ("the Egyptians call Zeus Amun"). Macaulay's
"God", "the Deity", "the divine power" for theos / to theion -> "the gods",
"heaven" or "the divine", never a Christian capital-G God; "the god" when
a particular god is meant (usually Apollo at Delphi: say "Apollo" or "the
god at Delphi" where it helps). The Pythian prophetess -> the Pythia, the
priestess at Delphi (glossed once). "Oracle" lower-case.

OFFICES AND THINGS. despot -> tyrant (a man who seized sole power outside
the law; not necessarily cruel; glossed once); satrap -> governor (or
"satrap, the governor of a province"); trireme stays (glossed once:
three-banked warship); hoplite stays (heavy-armed infantryman); ephors stay
(Sparta's five annual magistrates); polemarch: war-archon, glossed;
proxenos -> representative / consul; Medism -> siding with the Persians.

UNITS. Alex's standing rule for retellings (memory: modern-units-in-
retellings): convert obsolete measures to modern US units and redo the sums
so the reasoning still adds up. Herodotus defines his own units (II.6, V.53,
VI.42); stade = 600 ft, parasang = 30 stades, schoenus = 60 stades. Table:

    stade (Macaulay "furlong")  600 ft; 9 stades ~ 1 mile
    plethron                    100 ft
    fathom (orguia)             6 ft
    cubit                       1.5 ft (18 in); the royal cubit is 3
                                finger-widths longer, about 20 in
    foot                        foot (the Greek foot is within 3%)
    palm / finger / span        3 in / 3/4 in / 9 in
    parasang                    about 3 1/3 miles
    schoenus                    about 6 2/3 miles
    aroura (Egyptian)           about 2/3 acre
    medimnus                    about 1 1/2 bushels
    artaba (Persian)            about 1 2/3 bushels
    choenix                     about a quart
    amphora / metretes          about 10 gallons
    kotyle                      about half a pint
    myriad                      10,000: "twelve myriads" -> 120,000

Round as Herodotus rounds: "about 1,500 miles", not "1,534.1 miles". When
he does arithmetic (the Royal Road, V.53; the coast of Egypt, II.6-9; the
Pontus, IV.85-86; Solon's days, I.32), redo it in the converted figures and
check that it adds up the way his does; if his own figures do not add up,
keep the slip, converted, and say so in your notes. Where he is talking ABOUT
the unit (II.6: "those who have little land measure it in fathoms ..."),
keep the unit's name and give its modern size. Days' journeys and days'
sailing stay as they are.

Money is not a measure and stays: talents, minas, drachmas, obols, staters,
darics. Gloss "talent" once (a huge sum: a talent of silver was about 57 lb
of it). Where Herodotus gives a weight in talents of a physical object
(Croesus' gold ingots and lion, I.50), keep talents and add the pounds once.
Time stays: years, generations ("three generations make a hundred years",
II.142), months, festivals by name.


=== 5. SENSITIVE CONTENT ===

Herodotus describes, without flinching and mostly without moralizing: human
sacrifice, impalement and crucifixion (Polycrates III.125, Artayctes IX.120,
Pheretime IV.202), mutilation (Zopyrus III.154, Masistes' wife IX.112),
castration and the eunuch trade (Panionius and Hermotimus VIII.104-106),
a father fed his own son (Harpagus I.119), Scythian scalps, skull-cups and
royal burials with strangled servants (IV.64-73), sacred prostitution in
Babylon (I.199) and its marriage auction (I.196), public sex among certain
peoples (I.203, III.101, IV.172, IV.180), the goat of Mendes (II.46),
incest (Cambyses, III.31), the stripping of Candaules' wife (I.8-12), a
Persian queen burying children alive (VII.114), the mass rapes and
enslavements that follow sieges (VI.19, VI.32), ethnographic statements
about hair, skin and diet (the Ethiopians of the catalogue, VII.70; the
Indians, III.101).

The rule is the project's (memory: sensitive-content-tests; the Verne
rule): render every act and every judgement as HIS, in his measured,
reporting register, completely, with no added gore, no softening, no
editorial wink and no modern reassurance. Herodotus' tone is usually cool
and exact, and that coolness is part of the meaning; do not heat it up. Use
plain modern words, not euphemism and not slang: "had sex with" or "slept
with" where Macaulay says "lay with" or "had intercourse"; "slave"; "cut off
his nose and ears". Ethnographic description stays description in modern
neutral words ("thick, tightly curled hair" for Macaulay's "woolly";
"dark-skinned"); no slur appears in Macaulay and none may appear in ours.
Where he says he does not believe a report, his disbelief stays; where he
believes something we would not, his belief stays, unhedged.

Does the reader lose information or gain a false one? Herodotus' famous
even-handedness ("custom is king", III.38) is itself a subject of the book;
the reader should meet it exactly as strong as he wrote it.

If you think anything should be cut, do not cut it: translate it and say
so in your notes.


=== 6. VERSE: ORACLES AND INSCRIPTIONS ===

Macaulay renders the hexameter oracles and the epitaphs as loose English
hexameters full of "hath" and "thou". Render them as modern English verse
lines: one tab-indented line per source line, same number of lines, keeping
every image (the tortoise boiling in bronze, the mule on the Median throne,
the tunnies in the moonlit net, the wooden wall, divine Salamis). Plain,
strong, a little elevated; oracles are meant to sound like oracles. No rhyme
needed; no "thee". Locked versions of the most famous are in
running_notes.txt.

=== 8. RATIO AND LENGTH ===

English to English modernization of a Victorian translator runs near 1:1
(Thucydides from Crawley, 0.96; Malory, 1.02; memory note: ~1.0-1.05).
Macaulay's word-for-word Greek syntax contracts in places ("that which" ->
"what") and expands in others (glosses, unit conversions, unpacked
participles). Expect 0.90–1.05 per file. Below 0.92 something was dropped.

    python3 verify.py herodotus --min-ratio 0.85 --max-ratio 1.20

The floor is set so that every file of the old version (0.66-0.81) would
fail it. Source: 285,036 words; expected modern text about 285-305k.
