Socrates was executed by Athens in 399 BC, at about seventy, on charges of impiety and of corrupting the young. He wrote nothing. Everything we have of him comes from people who heard him talk, and chiefly from Plato, who was in his late twenties when his teacher was killed and who spent the next fifty years writing him down. Plato founded a school outside the city, the Academy, and died around 348 BC. How much of the Socrates in these pages is the man and how much is Plato arguing under a borrowed name has never been settled.

The dialogues are scripts. They have casts, settings and stage business: a porch outside a courtroom, a wrestling school, a walk along a river on a hot day, a drinking party that goes on till dawn. People arrive late, interrupt, get angry, walk out. Socrates asks questions because the question is the method. He says he knows nothing, finds a man who claims to know something, and asks him to define it — piety, courage, justice, self-control, love — then follows the answer until it comes apart. Often nothing replaces it. Several of these end with everybody agreeing they have got nowhere, and that is not a defect in the text. They were written to be read aloud and argued with, and a summary of the conclusions would leave out the entire book.

This selection is twenty-four works: Euthyphro, the Apology, Crito, Phaedo, Meno, Protagoras, Gorgias, the Symposium, Phaedrus, the Republic complete in ten books, Ion, Laches, Lysis, Charmides, Euthydemus, Cratylus, Theaetetus, Parmenides, the Sophist, the Statesman, Philebus, Timaeus, Critias, and the Laws complete in twelve books. The Apology is not a dialogue at all but a speech — Socrates defending himself to a jury of several hundred Athenians, and losing.

The first four make one continuous story and can be read straight through as such. Euthyphro is set on the porch of the court where the charge has just been filed. The Apology is the trial. Crito is the cell, some weeks later, with a friend who has bribed the guard and wants him to run. Phaedo is the last afternoon, the conversation with his friends, and the hemlock. It is four acts, and nobody wrote anything like it again.

Plato taught Aristotle, who came to the Academy at seventeen and stayed twenty years; Aristotle taught Theophrastus, who took over his school. Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics and Theophrastus' Characters are both in this collection, as is Xenophon's Memorabilia, which is another man's Socrates and a useful check on this one. The text here is modernized from Benjamin Jowett's Victorian English rather than from the Greek.
