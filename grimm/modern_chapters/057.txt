Donkey Cabbages

There was once a young huntsman who went into the forest to lie in wait for game. He had a fresh and cheerful heart; and as he was going along, whistling on a leaf, an ugly old woman came up and spoke to him and said, "Good day, dear huntsman. You're merry and contented, to be sure; but I'm suffering from hunger and thirst. Do give me something."

The huntsman felt sorry for the poor old creature, and felt in his pocket and gave her what he could spare. He was about to go on then, but the old woman stopped him and said, "Listen, dear huntsman, to what I'm going to tell you. I'll make you a present in return for your kindness. Go on your way now; and in a little while you'll come to a tree with nine birds sitting in it that have a cloak in their claws and are pulling at it. Take your gun and shoot into the middle of them, and they'll let the cloak drop down to you; but one of the birds will be hit and will fall down dead. Take the cloak away with you: it's a wishing cloak. When you throw it over your shoulders, you only have to wish to be in a certain place and you'll be there in the twinkling of an eye. And take the heart out of the dead bird and swallow it whole, and every morning early, when you get up, you'll find a gold piece under your pillow."

The huntsman thanked the wise woman, and thought to himself, "Those are fine things she's promised me — if only it all comes true."

And sure enough, when he had walked about a hundred paces, he heard such a screaming and twittering in the branches above him that he looked up and saw a crowd of birds tearing at a piece of cloth with their beaks and claws, tugging and fighting as if each one wanted it all to itself.

"Well," said the huntsman, "this is wonderful. It's really come about just as the old woman foretold." And he took the gun off his shoulder, aimed and fired right into the middle of them, so that the feathers flew about. The birds instantly took flight with loud cries, but one dropped down dead, and the cloak fell at the same moment.

Then the huntsman did as the old woman had told him: cut the bird open, found the heart and swallowed it down, and took the cloak home with him.

Next morning when he woke, the promise came back to him, and he wanted to see whether that had come true as well. When he lifted the pillow, the gold piece shone up at him; and next day he found another, and so it went on, every time he got up.

He gathered a heap of gold together; but at last he thought, "What use is all my gold to me if I stay at home? I'll go out and see the world."

So he took his leave of his parents, buckled on his huntsman's pouch and his gun, and went out into the world.

It happened that one day he travelled through a dense forest; and when he came out of it, on the plain in front of him stood a fine castle. An old woman was standing at one of the windows with a wonderfully beautiful girl, looking out.

But the old woman was a witch, and said to the girl, "There's one coming out of the forest who has a wonderful treasure inside him. We must steal it from him, my dear daughter; it suits us better than it does him. He has a bird's heart in him, and because of it a gold piece lies under his pillow every morning."

She told her what she was to do to get it, and what part she had to play; and finally she threatened her, and said with angry eyes, "And if you don't do as I say, it will be the worse for you."

Now when the huntsman came nearer he caught sight of the girl, and said to himself, "I've been travelling about for such a long while, I'll take a rest for once and go into that beautiful castle. I certainly have money enough." But the real reason was that he had caught sight of the pretty girl.

He went into the house and was well received and courteously entertained. Before long he was so much in love with the young witch that he thought of nothing else, and saw everything only as she saw it, and did whatever she wanted.

Then the old woman said, "Now we must have the bird's heart. He'll never miss it."

She prepared a drink; and when it was ready she poured it into a cup and gave it to the girl, who was to hand it to the huntsman. She did so, saying, "Now, my dearest, drink to me."

So he took the cup; and when he had swallowed the draught, he brought up the bird's heart. The girl had to take it away secretly and swallow it herself, for the old woman would have it so.

From then on he found no more gold under his pillow; it lay under the girl's instead, and the old woman fetched it away every morning. But he was so much in love and so befooled that he thought of nothing but spending his time with the girl.

Then the old witch said, "We have the bird's heart, but we must take the wishing cloak away from him too."

The girl answered, "Let's leave him that. He's lost his wealth already."

The old woman was angry and said, "A cloak like that is a wonderful thing and rarely to be found in this world. I must and will have it." She struck the girl several blows, and said that if she did not obey it would go badly for her.

So she did as the old woman told her: stationed herself at the window and looked out over the distant country as if she were very sorrowful.

The huntsman asked, "Why are you standing there so sadly?"

"Ah, my beloved," she answered, "over yonder lies the Garnet Mountain, where the precious stones grow. I long for them so much that when I think of them I feel quite miserable. But who can get them? Only the birds — they fly and can reach them; a man never can."

"Is that all you have to complain of?" said the huntsman. "I'll soon take that burden off your heart."

And with that he drew her under his cloak, wished himself on the Garnet Mountain, and in the twinkling of an eye they were sitting on it together. Precious stones were glittering on every side, so that it was a joy to see them; and together they gathered the finest and costliest of them.

Now the old woman had contrived by her sorcery that the huntsman's eyes should grow heavy. He said to the girl, "Let's sit down and rest a while. I'm so tired I can't stand on my feet any longer."

So they sat down, and he laid his head in her lap and fell asleep. And when he was asleep she unfastened the cloak from his shoulders and wrapped herself in it, picked up the garnets and the stones, and wished herself home again with them.

But when the huntsman had had his sleep out and woke and saw that his sweetheart had betrayed him and left him alone on the wild mountain, he said, "Oh, what treachery there is in the world!" — and sat down there in care and sorrow, not knowing what to do.

But the mountain belonged to some wild and monstrous giants who lived on it and led their lives there; and he had not sat long before he saw three of them coming towards him, so he lay down as if he were sunk in a deep sleep.

Then the giants came up, and the first kicked him with his foot and said, "What sort of an earthworm is this, lying curled up here?"

The second said, "Step on him and kill him."

But the third said, "That would hardly be worth your trouble. Just let him live — he can't stay here; and when he climbs higher, towards the summit of the mountain, the clouds will take hold of him and carry him away."

And so saying they went on by.

But the huntsman had paid attention to their words; and as soon as they were gone he got up and climbed to the summit of the mountain. And when he had sat there a while, a cloud floated towards him and caught him up and carried him away, and travelled about the sky with him for a long time. Then it sank lower and let itself down on a great walled cabbage garden, so that he came softly to the ground among cabbages and vegetables.

Then the huntsman looked around him and said, "If only I had something to eat! I'm so hungry, and my hunger will only get worse as time goes on. But I see neither apples nor pears here, nor any other kind of fruit — nothing anywhere but cabbages."

At length, though, he thought, "At a pinch I can eat some of the leaves. They don't taste particularly good, but they'll refresh me." So he picked himself out a fine head of cabbage and ate it; but hardly had he swallowed a couple of mouthfuls before he felt very strange, and quite different.

Four legs grew on him, and a large head and two thick ears; and he saw with horror that he had been changed into a donkey. All the same, as his hunger grew greater every minute, and as the juicy leaves suited his present nature, he went on eating with great relish.

At last he came to a different kind of cabbage; but as soon as he had swallowed it he felt another change, and took his old human shape again.

Then the huntsman lay down and slept off his weariness. When he woke next morning he broke off one head of the bad cabbages and one of the good ones, and thought to himself, "This shall help me to get back my own and punish treachery."

Then he took the cabbages with him, climbed over the wall and set out to look for his sweetheart's castle. After wandering about for a couple of days he was lucky enough to find it again.

He dyed his face brown, so that his own mother would not have known him, and asked for shelter. "I'm so tired," said he, "that I can go no further."

The witch asked, "Who are you, countryman, and what's your business?"

"I'm a King's messenger, and I was sent out to look for the most delicious salad that grows under the sun. I've even been lucky enough to find it, and I'm carrying it about with me; but the sun's heat is so fierce that the delicate cabbage is threatening to wither, and I don't know whether I can carry it any further."

When the old woman heard about this exquisite salad she was greedy for it, and said, "Dear countryman, do let me just taste this wonderful salad."

"Why not?" answered he. "I've brought two heads with me, and I'll give you one of them." And he opened his pouch and handed her the bad cabbage.

The witch suspected nothing amiss, and her mouth watered so for this new dish that she went into the kitchen and dressed it herself. When it was ready she could not wait until it was set on the table, but took a couple of leaves at once and put them in her mouth. But hardly had she swallowed them before she lost her human shape, and ran out into the courtyard in the form of a donkey.

Presently the servant girl came into the kitchen and saw the salad standing there ready prepared, and was about to carry it up. But on the way, out of habit, she was seized with the wish to taste it, and ate a couple of leaves. Instantly the magic worked, and she became a donkey too and ran out to the old woman; and the dish of salad fell to the floor.

Meanwhile the messenger was sitting beside the beautiful girl; and as nobody came with the salad, and she too was longing for it, she said, "I don't know what has become of the salad."

The huntsman thought, "The salad must have taken effect already" — and said, "I'll go down to the kitchen and ask about it."

As he went down he saw the two donkeys running about the courtyard; but the salad was lying on the floor. "All right," said he, "those two have taken their portion" — and he picked up the remaining leaves and laid them on the dish and carried them up to the girl.

"I bring you the delicate dish myself," said he, "so that you don't have to wait any longer."

Then she ate of it, and like the others she was immediately deprived of her human shape, and ran out into the courtyard in the form of a donkey.

After the huntsman had washed his face, so that the transformed ones could recognise him, he went down into the courtyard and said, "Now you shall have the wages of your treachery." And he tied all three of them together with one rope and drove them along until he came to a mill.

He knocked at the window; the miller put his head out and asked what he wanted.

"I have three unmanageable beasts here," answered he, "that I don't want to keep any longer. Will you take them in and give them food and stable room and treat them as I tell you? And then I'll pay you what you ask."

The miller said, "Why not? But how am I to treat them?"

The huntsman said that he was to give the old donkey — that was the witch — three beatings and one meal a day; the younger one, which was the servant girl, one beating and three meals; and the youngest, which was the girl, no beatings and three meals — for he could not bring himself to have the girl beaten.

After that he went back to the castle and found everything there that he needed.

After a couple of days the miller came and said he must inform him that the old donkey, which had had three beatings and only one meal a day, was dead. "The other two," he went on, "certainly aren't dead, and they're fed three times a day; but they're so sad that they can't last much longer."

The huntsman was moved to pity, put his anger away and told the miller to drive them back to him. And when they came, he gave them some of the good salad, so that they became human again.

The beautiful girl fell on her knees before him and said, "Ah, my beloved, forgive me for the wrong I've done you. My mother drove me to it; it was done against my will, for I love you dearly. Your wishing cloak is hanging in a cupboard; and as for the bird's heart, I'll take a potion to bring it up."

But he thought otherwise, and said, "Keep it. It's all the same, for I'll take you for my true wife."

So the wedding was celebrated, and they lived happily together until their deaths.

The Old Woman in the Wood

A poor servant girl was once travelling through a great forest with the family she was in service with; and when they were in the middle of it, robbers came out of the thicket and murdered everybody they found. All of them died together except the girl, who had jumped out of the carriage in fright and hidden herself behind a tree.

When the robbers had gone away with their plunder, she came out and saw the great disaster. Then she began to weep bitterly and said, "What can a poor girl like me do now? I don't know how to get out of the forest, and no human being lives in it, so I must certainly starve."

She walked about and looked for a road, but could find none. When it was evening she sat down under a tree and gave herself into God's keeping, and resolved to sit and wait there and not go away, come what might.

But when she had sat there a while, a white dove came flying to her with a little golden key in its beak. It put the little key in her hand and said, "Do you see that great tree? There's a little lock in it, and this tiny key opens it, and inside you'll find food enough, and go hungry no more."

So she went to the tree and opened it, and found milk in a little dish and white bread to break into it, so that she could eat her fill.

When she had had enough she said, "This is the hour when the hens at home go to roost. I'm so tired I could go to bed too."

Then the dove flew to her again and brought another golden key in its beak, and said, "Open that tree there, and you'll find a bed."

So she opened it and found a beautiful white bed; and she prayed God to protect her during the night, and lay down and slept.

In the morning the dove came for the third time and again brought a little key, and said, "Open that tree there, and you'll find clothes." And when she opened it she found garments set with gold and jewels, more splendid than any king's daughter's.

So she lived there for some time, and the dove came every day and provided her with everything she needed; and it was a quiet, good life.

Once, though, the dove came and said, "Will you do something for my sake?"

"With all my heart," said the girl.

Then the little dove said, "I'll guide you to a small house. Go in; and inside it an old woman will be sitting by the fire, and she'll say, 'Good day.' But for your life, give her no answer; let her do what she likes, but pass by her on the right side. Further on there's a door; open it, and you'll come into a room where a quantity of rings of all kinds are lying, among them some magnificent ones with shining stones. Leave those where they are, though, and look out a plain one, which must be among them too, and bring it here to me as fast as you can."

The girl went to the little house and came to the door. There sat an old woman, who stared when she saw her, and said, "Good day, my child."

The girl gave her no answer, and opened the door.

"Where are you going?" cried the old woman, and seized her by the gown and tried to hold her fast, saying, "This is my house; nobody can go in there if I choose not to allow it."

But the girl was silent, and got away from her and went straight into the room. Now on the table lay an enormous quantity of rings, which gleamed and glittered before her eyes. She turned them over and looked for the plain one, but could not find it.

While she was searching, she saw the old woman stealing away and trying to make off with a birdcage she had in her hand. So she went after her and took the cage out of her hand; and when she raised it up and looked inside, there was a bird in it with the plain ring in its beak.

So she took the ring and ran home with it quite joyfully, and thought the little white dove would come and get the ring; but it did not.

Then she leaned against a tree and made up her mind to wait for the dove; and as she stood there, it seemed just as if the tree were growing soft and pliant, and letting its branches down. And suddenly the branches twined round her and turned into two arms; and when she looked round, the tree was a handsome man, who embraced her and kissed her heartily and said, "You have delivered me from the power of the old woman, who is a wicked witch. She had changed me into a tree, and every day for two hours I was a white dove; and as long as she had the ring, I could not get my human shape back."

Then his servants and his horses, who had likewise been changed into trees, were freed from the enchantment too, and stood beside him. And he led them away to his kingdom, for he was a King's son; and they married, and lived happily.

The Three Brothers

There was once a man who had three sons and nothing else in the world but the house he lived in. Now each of the sons wanted to have the house after his father's death; but the father loved them all alike and did not know what to do. He did not want to sell the house, because it had belonged to his forefathers, or else he could have divided the money among them.

At last a plan came into his head, and he said to his sons, "Go out into the world and each of you try to learn a trade; and when you all come back, whoever makes the best masterpiece shall have the house."

The sons were well content with this; and the eldest decided to be a blacksmith, the second a barber and the third a fencing master. They fixed a time when they should all come home again, and then each went his own way.

It happened that they all found skilful masters who taught them their trades well. The blacksmith had to shoe the King's horses, and he thought to himself, "The house is mine, without a doubt." The barber shaved only great people, and he too already looked on the house as his own. The fencing master got many a blow, but he only bit his lip and let nothing upset him; "for," he said to himself, "if you're afraid of a blow, you'll never win the house."

When the appointed time had gone by, the three brothers came home to their father; but they did not know how to find the best opportunity for showing their skill, so they sat down and talked it over together.

As they were sitting there, all at once a hare came running across the field. "Aha, just in time!" said the barber. So he took his basin and soap and lathered away until the hare came up; and then he soaped and shaved off the hare's whiskers while it was running at top speed, and did not even cut its skin or injure a hair on its body.

"Well done," said the old man. "Your brothers will have to exert themselves wonderfully, or the house will be yours."

Soon afterwards a nobleman came along in his coach, dashing by at full speed. "Now you shall see what I can do, father," said the blacksmith. And away he ran after the coach, and took all four shoes off the feet of one of the horses while it was galloping, and fitted it with four new shoes without stopping it.

"You're a fine fellow, and as clever as your brother," said his father. "I don't know which of you I ought to give the house to."

Then the third son said, "Father, let me have my turn, if you please." And as it was beginning to rain, he drew his sword and flourished it backwards and forwards above his head so fast that not a drop fell on him. It rained harder and harder, until at last it came down in torrents; but he only flourished his sword faster and faster, and stayed as dry as if he were sitting indoors.

When his father saw this he was amazed, and said, "This is the masterpiece. The house is yours."

His brothers were satisfied with this, as had been agreed beforehand; and as they loved one another very much, all three of them stayed together in the house and followed their trades. And since they had learned them so well and were so clever, they earned a great deal of money.

So they lived together happily until they grew old; and at last, when one of them fell sick and died, the two others grieved over it so sorely that they fell ill as well, and soon died too. And because they had been so clever, and had loved one another so much, all three of them were laid in the same grave.
