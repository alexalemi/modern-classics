Frederick and Catherine

There was once a man called Frederick and a woman called Catherine, who had married each other and were living together as a young married couple.

One day Frederick said, "I'm going out to plough now, Catherine. When I come back there must be some roast meat on the table for my hunger and a fresh drink for my thirst."

"Off you go, Frederick," said Kate. "Off you go, I'll have it all ready for you."

So when dinner time drew near she got a sausage down out of the chimney, put it in the frying pan with some butter and set it on the fire. The sausage began to fry and hiss; Catherine stood beside it holding the handle of the pan, and had her own thoughts while she did it.

Then it occurred to her, "While the sausage is cooking you could go down to the cellar and draw the beer." So she set the frying pan safely on the fire, took a can and went down into the cellar to draw the beer.

The beer ran into the can and Kate watched it; and then she thought, "Oh dear! The dog upstairs isn't shut up — it might get the sausage out of the pan. Well thought of."

And in a trice she was up the cellar steps again; but the dog already had the sausage in his mouth and was dragging it away along the ground.

But Catherine, who was not idle, set off after him and chased him a long way into the field. The dog, though, was faster than Catherine and did not let the sausage travel easily, but went skipping over the furrows with it.

"What's gone is gone," said Kate, and turned round; and as she had run herself weary, she walked back quietly and comfortably and cooled herself down.

All this time the beer was still running out of the cask, because Kate had not turned the tap off. And when the can was full and there was nowhere else for it to go, it ran out over the cellar floor and did not stop until the whole cask was empty.

As soon as Kate was on the steps she saw the disaster. "Good gracious!" she cried. "What shall I do now to stop Frederick finding out?"

She thought for a while, and at last remembered that up in the garret there was still a sack of the finest wheat flour from the last fair; she would fetch that down and scatter it over the beer.

"Yes," she said, "whoever saves a thing when he ought has it afterwards when he needs it." And she climbed up to the garret and carried the sack down and threw it straight down onto the can of beer, which she knocked over, so that Frederick's drink was swimming in the cellar too.

"It's all right," said Kate. "Where the one is, the other ought to be as well." And she scattered the flour all over the cellar.

When it was done she was heartily delighted with her work, and said, "How clean and wholesome it does look down here!"

At midday Frederick came home. "Now, wife, what have you got ready for me?"

"Ah, Freddy," she answered, "I was frying a sausage for you; but while I was drawing the beer to go with it, the dog took it out of the pan; and while I was running after the dog, all the beer ran out; and while I was drying up the beer with the flour, I knocked the can over as well. But don't fret — the cellar is quite dry again."

Said Frederick, "Kate, Kate, you shouldn't have done that! To let the sausage be carried off, and the beer run out of the cask, and throw away all our flour into the bargain!"

"Really, Frederick, I didn't know. You should have told me."

The man thought, "If my wife is like this, I shall have to look after things more."

Now he had got together a good number of thalers, which he changed into gold; and he said to Catherine, "Look, these are counters for playing games. I'll put them in a pot and bury them in the stable under the cow's manger. But mind you keep away from them, or it will be the worse for you."

"Oh, no, Frederick," she said, "I certainly won't go near them."

And when Frederick had gone, some pedlars came into the village with cheap earthenware bowls and pots, and asked the young woman whether there was nothing she wanted to bargain for.

"Oh, my dear people," said Catherine, "I've no money and can't buy anything. But if you have any use for yellow counters, I'll buy from you."

"Yellow counters? Why not? But let's have a look at them."

"Then go into the stable and dig under the cow's manger, and you'll find the yellow counters. I'm not allowed to go there."

The rogues went off, dug, and found pure gold. Then they took hold of it and ran away, and left their pots and bowls behind in the house.

Catherine thought she had better make use of her new things; and as she had no shortage in the kitchen without them, she knocked the bottom out of every pot and set them all as ornaments on the fence that ran round the house.

When Frederick came and saw the new decorations, he said, "Catherine, what have you been up to?"

"I bought them, Frederick, with the counters that were under the cow's manger. I didn't go there myself — the pedlars had to dig them out for themselves."

"Ah, wife," said Frederick, "what have you done? Those weren't counters, they were pure gold, and all we had in the world. You shouldn't have done that."

"Really, Frederick," she said, "I didn't know. You should have warned me."

Catherine stood a while and thought to herself; then she said, "Listen, Frederick, we'll soon get the gold back. We'll run after the thieves."

"Come on then," said Frederick, "we'll try it. But bring some butter and cheese with you so that we have something to eat on the way."

"Yes, Frederick, I'll bring them."

They set out; and as Frederick was the better walker, Catherine followed behind. "It's to my advantage," she thought. "When we turn back I'll be a little way ahead."

Then she came to a hill with deep ruts on both sides of the road. "There, you can see," said Catherine, "how they've torn and skinned and galled the poor earth. It'll never be whole again as long as it lives." And out of the compassion of her heart she took her butter and smeared the ruts to right and left, so that they should not be so hurt by the wheels.

And as she was bending down in her charity like this, one of the cheeses rolled out of her pocket and away down the hill.

Said Catherine, "I've made my way up here once; I'm not going down again. Somebody else can run and fetch it back." So she took another cheese and rolled it down after it.

But the cheeses did not come back; so she let a third one run down, thinking, "Perhaps they're waiting for company and don't like to walk alone."

And as all three stayed away, she said, "I don't know what that can mean. Perhaps the third one didn't find the way and has gone wrong. I'll just send a fourth to call them."

But the fourth did no better than the third. Then Catherine got angry, and threw down the fifth and the sixth as well, and those were her last.

She stood for some time watching for them to come; but when they still did not come, she said, "Oh, you're fine ones to send to fetch death! You stay away a good long time. Do you think I'm going to wait any longer for you? I'm going on my way — you can run after me. You've got younger legs than I have."

Catherine went on and found Frederick, who had stopped and was waiting for her, because he wanted something to eat.

"Now let's have what you brought with you," he said.

She gave him the dry bread.

"Where are the butter and the cheeses?" asked her husband.

"Ah, Freddy," said Catherine, "I smeared the cart ruts with the butter, and the cheeses will be along soon. One of them ran away from me, so I sent the others after it to call it back."

Said Frederick, "You shouldn't have done that, Catherine — smearing the butter on the road and letting the cheeses run away down the hill!"

"Really, Frederick, you should have told me."

Then they ate the dry bread together, and Frederick said, "Catherine, did you make the house secure when you came away?"

"No, Frederick — you should have told me to before."

"Then go back home and make the house secure before we go any further, and bring something else to eat with you. I'll wait here for you."

Catherine went back and thought, "Frederick wants something more to eat. He doesn't like butter and cheese, so I'll take a handkerchief full of dried pears and a jug of vinegar for him to drink."

Then she bolted the top half of the door fast, but lifted the bottom half off its hinges and took it on her back, believing that when she had got the door safe, the house must be well taken care of.

Catherine took her time on the way, and thought, "Frederick will get all the longer rest."

When she reached him at last she said, "Here's the house door for you, Frederick, and now you can look after the house yourself."

"Oh, heavens," he said, "what a wise wife I have! She takes the bottom half of the door off its hinges so that anything at all can walk in, and bolts the top half. It's too late to go home again now; but since you've brought the door here, you can just carry it further."

"I'll carry the door, Frederick, but the dried pears and the vinegar jug will be too heavy for me. I'll hang them on the door — it can carry them."

And now they went into the forest and looked for the rogues, but did not find them. At length, as it grew dark, they climbed into a tree and decided to spend the night there.

But hardly had they settled at the top of it before the very rascals came along who carry off what does not want to go and find things before they are lost. They sat down under the very tree Frederick and Catherine were sitting in, lit a fire and were about to share out their booty.

Frederick got down on the other side and collected some stones. Then he climbed up again with them, meaning to throw them at the thieves and kill them. But the stones did not hit them, and the rogues said, "It'll soon be morning — the wind is shaking the fir cones down."

Catherine still had the door on her back; and as it pressed so heavily on her, she thought it was the fault of the dried pears, and said, "Frederick, I must throw the pears down."

"No, Catherine, not now," he said. "They might give us away."

"Oh, but Frederick, I must. They weigh me down far too much."

"Do it, then, and be hanged!"

So the dried pears came rolling down through the branches, and the rascals below said, "The leaves are falling."

A short time afterwards, as the door was still heavy, Catherine said, "Ah, Frederick, I must pour the vinegar out."

"No, Catherine, you mustn't. It might give us away."

"Ah, but Frederick, I must. It weighs me down far too much."

"Then do it and be hanged!"

So she emptied the vinegar out, and it sprinkled all over the robbers. They said among themselves, "The dew is falling already."

At length Catherine thought, "Can it really be the door that weighs me down so?" — and said, "Frederick, I must throw the door down."

"No, not now, Catherine. It might give us away."

"Oh, but Frederick, I must. It weighs me down far too much."

"Oh no, Catherine, do hold onto it."

"Ah, Frederick, I'm letting it fall!"

"Let it go, then, in the devil's name."

Then it fell down with a tremendous crash, and the rascals below cried, "The devil is coming down the tree!" — and they ran away and left everything behind them.

Early next morning, when the two of them came down, they found all their gold again and carried it home.

When they were home once more, Frederick said, "And now, Catherine, you must be industrious and do some work."

"Yes, Frederick, I'll do that at once. I'll go into the field and cut the corn."

When Catherine got to the field she said to herself, "Shall I eat before I cut, or shall I sleep before I cut? Oh, I'll eat first."

So Catherine ate, and the eating made her sleepy; and she began to cut, and half in a dream cut all her clothes to pieces — her apron, her gown and her shift.

When Catherine woke up after a long sleep, she was standing there half naked, and said to herself, "Is it me, or isn't it me? Alas, it isn't me."

Meanwhile night came on, and Catherine ran into the village, knocked at her husband's window and called, "Frederick!"

"What's the matter?"

"I should very much like to know whether Catherine is in."

"Yes, yes," said Frederick, "she must be in and asleep."

Said she, "Well then, I'm certainly at home already" — and ran away.

Outside, Catherine met some vagabonds who were going out to steal. So she went up to them and said, "I'll help you steal."

The rascals thought she knew the lie of the land, and were willing. Catherine went along in front of the houses and called out, "Good people, have you got anything? We want to steal."

The thieves thought to themselves, "That's a fine way of going about it" — and wished themselves rid of Catherine again. So they said to her, "Outside the village the parson has some turnips in his field. Go there and pull up some turnips for us."

Catherine went to the field and began pulling them up, but was so lazy that she did not gather them together.

Then a man came by and saw her, and stood still, and thought it was the devil rooting about among the turnips like that. He ran off into the village to the parson and said, "Reverend sir, the devil is in your turnip field, rooting up turnips."

"Ah, heavens," said the parson, "I have a lame foot. I can't go out and drive him away."

Said the man, "Then I'll carry you on my back" — and carried him out on his back.

And when they came to the field, Catherine stood up to her full height.

"Ah, the devil!" cried the parson; and they both hurried away; and in his great fright the parson could run better on his lame foot than the man who had carried him could on his sound one.
