The Golden Goose

There was a man who had three sons, the youngest of whom was called Simpleton, and was despised and mocked and put down at every turn.

It happened that the eldest wanted to go into the forest to cut wood; and before he went, his mother gave him a beautiful sweet cake and a bottle of wine, so that he should not go hungry or thirsty.

When he came into the forest, a little grey-haired old man met him and wished him good day, and said, "Do give me a piece of the cake in your pocket, and let me have a drink of your wine. I'm so hungry and thirsty."

But the prudent young man answered, "If I give you my cake and my wine, I shall have none for myself. Be off with you." And he left the little man standing there and went on.

But when he began to cut down a tree, it was not long before he made a false stroke and the axe cut into his arm, so that he had to go home and have it bound up. And that was the little grey man's doing.

After this the second son went into the forest, and his mother gave him a cake and a bottle of wine, as she had the eldest. The little old grey man met him too, and asked him for a piece of the cake and a drink of the wine. But the second son said, just as sensibly, "Whatever I give you is taken away from me. Be off!" And he left the little man standing there and went on.

His punishment was not slow in coming either: he had struck the tree only a few times when he struck his own leg, and had to be carried home.

Then Simpleton said, "Father, do let me go and cut wood."

His father answered, "Your brothers have hurt themselves at it. Leave it alone — you know nothing about it."

But Simpleton begged so long that at last he said, "Off you go, then. Hurting yourself will teach you sense."

His mother gave him a cake made with water and baked in the ashes, and a bottle of sour beer to go with it.

When he came to the forest, the little old grey man met him as well, and greeted him and said, "Give me a piece of your cake and a drink out of your bottle. I'm so hungry and thirsty."

Simpleton answered, "I've nothing but a cinder-cake and sour beer. If that suits you, we'll sit down and eat."

So they sat down; and when Simpleton pulled out his cinder-cake, it was a fine sweet cake, and the sour beer had turned into good wine. So they ate and drank; and afterwards the little man said, "Since you have a good heart and are willing to share what you have, I'll give you good luck. There's an old tree standing over there — cut it down, and you'll find something among the roots." And with that the little man took his leave.

Simpleton went and cut down the tree; and when it fell there was a goose sitting among the roots, with feathers of pure gold. He picked her up and took her with him to an inn, where he meant to stay the night.

Now the innkeeper had three daughters, who saw the goose and were curious to know what sort of wonderful bird it might be, and would have liked one of its golden feathers.

The eldest thought, "I shall soon find a chance to pull out a feather"; and as soon as Simpleton had gone out, she took hold of the goose by the wing — and her finger and hand stuck fast to it.

The second came soon afterwards, thinking of nothing but how she might get a feather for herself; but she had hardly touched her sister before she was held fast too.

At last the third came with the same idea, and the others screamed out, "Keep away! For goodness' sake keep away!" But she could not see why she should keep away. "The others are there," she thought. "I may as well be there too" — and she ran to them; but the moment she touched her sister she stuck fast to her. So they had to spend the night with the goose.

Next morning Simpleton took the goose under his arm and set out, without troubling himself about the three girls hanging on behind. They had to keep running after him, now left, now right, wherever he happened to want to go.

Out in the middle of the fields the parson met them; and when he saw the procession he said, "For shame, you good-for-nothing girls! Why are you running across the fields after this young man? Is that proper?" And at the same time he took hold of the youngest by the hand to pull her away — but as soon as he touched her he stuck fast as well, and had to run along behind.

Before long the sexton came by and saw his master the parson running along behind three girls. He was astonished at this and called out, "Hi there, your reverence, where are you off to in such a hurry? Don't forget we've a christening today!" And he ran after him and took him by the sleeve, and was held fast to it.

While the five of them were trotting along one behind the other, two labourers came in from the fields with their hoes. The parson called out to them and begged them to set him and the sexton free; but they had hardly touched the sexton before they were held fast too, and now there were seven of them running along behind Simpleton and the goose.

Soon afterwards he came to a city where a king reigned who had a daughter so solemn that nobody could make her laugh. So he had put out a proclamation that whoever could make her laugh should marry her.

When Simpleton heard this he went with his goose and all its train before the King's daughter; and as soon as she saw the seven people running along, on and on, one behind the other, she began to laugh out loud, and as if she would never stop. At that Simpleton asked to have her for his wife, and the wedding was celebrated. After the King's death Simpleton inherited the kingdom, and lived contentedly with his wife for a long time.

Allerleirauh

There was once a King who had a wife with golden hair, and she was so beautiful that her equal was not to be found on earth. It came about that she fell ill; and as she felt that she must soon die, she called the King and said, "If you wish to marry again after my death, take nobody who isn't quite as beautiful as I am, and who hasn't golden hair just like mine. You must promise me that." And after the King had promised her, she closed her eyes and died.

For a long time the King could not be comforted, and had no thought of taking another wife. At length his councillors said, "There's no help for it — the King must marry again, so that we may have a Queen." And now messengers were sent far and wide to look for a bride who was the late Queen's equal in beauty. But in the whole world none was to be found; and even if one had been found, there would still have been nobody with such golden hair. So the messengers came home no wiser than they went.

Now the King had a daughter who was just as beautiful as her dead mother, and had the same golden hair. When she was grown up, the King looked at her one day and saw that in every respect she was the image of his late wife, and all at once he felt a violent love for her. Then he said to his councillors, "I'll marry my daughter, for she is the very likeness of my late wife; and apart from her I can find no bride who resembles her."

When the councillors heard that they were appalled, and said, "God has forbidden a father to marry his daughter. No good can come of such a crime, and the kingdom will be dragged down in the ruin."

The daughter was still more appalled when she learned of her father's decision, but she hoped to turn him from his plan. So she said to him, "Before I do what you wish, I must have three dresses: one as golden as the sun, one as silvery as the moon, and one as bright as the stars. And besides that I want a cloak made of a thousand different kinds of fur and hide sewn together, and every kind of animal in your kingdom must give a piece of its skin for it." But she thought, "Getting that will be quite impossible, and so I shall turn my father away from his wicked intentions."

The King, however, did not give up. The cleverest girls in his kingdom had to weave the three dresses — one as golden as the sun, one as silvery as the moon, and one as bright as the stars; and his huntsmen had to catch one of every kind of animal in the whole kingdom and take a piece of its skin, and out of these a cloak of a thousand kinds of fur was made. At last, when everything was ready, the King had the cloak brought and spread out in front of her, and said, "The wedding shall be tomorrow."

So when the King's daughter saw that there was no hope left of turning her father's heart, she made up her mind to run away from him. In the night, while everybody was asleep, she got up and took three things out of her treasures: a golden ring, a golden spinning wheel and a golden reel. The three dresses of the sun, the moon and the stars she packed into a nutshell; then she put on her cloak of all kinds of fur and blackened her face and hands with soot. Then she commended herself to God and went away, and walked the whole night until she came to a great forest. And as she was tired she climbed into a hollow tree and fell asleep.

The sun rose and she slept on, and she was still sleeping when it was broad day. Now it happened that the King to whom this forest belonged was hunting in it. When his dogs came to the tree they sniffed and ran round it barking.

The King said to his huntsmen, "Just see what sort of wild beast has hidden itself in there."

The huntsmen did as he ordered; and when they came back they said, "There's a wonderful beast lying in the hollow tree. We've never seen anything like it. Its coat is fur of a thousand different kinds — but it's lying there asleep."

Said the King, "See if you can catch it alive; then tie it to the carriage and we'll take it with us."

When the huntsmen laid hold of the girl she woke in terror and cried out to them, "I'm a poor child, deserted by father and mother. Have pity on me and take me with you."

Then they said, "Allerleirauh — All-Kinds-of-Fur — you'll do to work in the kitchen. Come along with us, you can sweep up the ashes."

So they put her in the carriage and took her home to the royal palace. There they showed her a cupboard under the stairs where no daylight came, and said, "Hairy animal, you can live and sleep in there." Then she was sent into the kitchen, and there she carried wood and water, swept the hearth, plucked the fowls, picked over the vegetables, raked out the ashes, and did all the dirty work.

Allerleirauh lived there a long time in great wretchedness. Ah, lovely princess, what is to become of you now!

It happened one day, though, that a feast was held in the palace, and she said to the cook, "May I go upstairs for a while and watch? I'll stand outside the door."

The cook answered, "Yes, go along; but you must be back here in half an hour to sweep the hearth."

So she took her oil lamp and went into her den, took off her fur dress and washed the soot off her face and hands, so that her full beauty came to light again. And she opened the nut and took out her dress that shone like the sun; and when she had put it on she went up to the feast, and everybody made way for her, because nobody knew her, and they took her for nothing less than a king's daughter. The King came to meet her and gave her his hand and danced with her, and thought in his heart, "My eyes have never seen anyone so beautiful." When the dance was over she curtsied; and when the King looked round again she had vanished, and nobody knew where. The guards standing outside the palace were called and questioned, but not one of them had seen her.

She had run into her little den, though, and quickly taken off her dress and made her face and hands black again and put on her fur cloak, and was Allerleirauh once more. And now, when she went into the kitchen and was about to get to work and sweep up the ashes, the cook said, "Leave that until morning, and make the King his soup. I want to go up for a while too and take a look. But don't let any hairs fall into it, or you shall have nothing to eat in future."

So the cook went off, and Allerleirauh made the King's soup, and made it bread soup and as good as she knew how; and when it was ready she fetched her golden ring from her little den and put it in the bowl the soup was served in.

When the dancing was over the King had his soup brought and ate it, and liked it so much that it seemed to him he had never tasted better. But when he came to the bottom of the bowl he saw a golden ring lying there, and could not imagine how it had got in. So he ordered the cook to come before him.

The cook was terrified when he heard the order, and said to Allerleirauh, "You've certainly let a hair fall into the soup; and if you have, you'll be beaten for it."

When he came before the King, the King asked who had made the soup.

The cook replied, "I made it."

But the King said, "That's not true. It was far better than usual, and cooked differently."

He answered, "I must admit that I didn't make it. It was made by the hairy animal."

The King said, "Go and tell it to come up here."

When Allerleirauh came, the King said, "Who are you?"

"I'm a poor girl who has no father or mother any more."

He asked further, "What use are you in my palace?"

She answered, "I'm good for nothing but to have boots thrown at my head."

He went on, "Where did you get the ring that was in the soup?"

She answered, "I know nothing about any ring."

So the King could learn nothing, and had to send her away again.

After a while there was another feast, and then, as before, Allerleirauh begged the cook for leave to go and watch. He answered, "Yes, but come back in half an hour and make the King the bread soup he likes so much."

Then she ran into her den, washed herself quickly, and took out of the nut the dress that was as silvery as the moon, and put it on. Then she went up, and was like a princess; and the King stepped forward to meet her and was delighted to see her again, and as the dance was just beginning they danced it together. But when it ended she disappeared again so quickly that the King could not see where she went.

She sprang into her den, though, and made herself a hairy animal once more, and went into the kitchen to make the bread soup. When the cook had gone upstairs, she fetched the little golden spinning wheel and put it in the bowl, so that the soup covered it.

Then it was carried to the King, who ate it and liked it as much as before, and had the cook brought in; and this time too he was forced to admit that Allerleirauh had made the soup. Allerleirauh came before the King again; but she answered that she was good for nothing but to have boots thrown at her head, and that she knew nothing at all about the little golden spinning wheel.

When the King held a feast for the third time, it all happened as it had before. The cook said, "Upon my word, rough-skin, you're a witch. You're always putting something in the soup that makes it so good that the King likes it better than mine." But she begged so hard that he let her go up at the appointed time.

And now she put on the dress that shone like the stars, and went into the hall in it. Again the King danced with the beautiful girl, and thought that she had never yet been so lovely. And while she was dancing he contrived, without her noticing it, to slip a golden ring onto her finger; and he had given orders that the dance should last a very long time. When it ended he wanted to hold her fast by the hands, but she tore herself free and sprang away through the crowd so quickly that she vanished from his sight.

She ran as fast as she could into her den under the stairs; but as she had been too long, and had stayed more than half an hour, she could not get her pretty dress off, and only threw the fur cloak over it; and in her haste she did not make herself quite black, but left one finger white. Then Allerleirauh ran into the kitchen and cooked the King's bread soup; and as the cook was away, she put her golden reel into it.

When the King found the reel at the bottom, he had Allerleirauh summoned; and then he caught sight of the white finger, and saw the ring he had put on it during the dance. Then he took her by the hand and held her fast; and when she tried to get free and run away, her fur cloak fell open a little and the star dress shone out. The King seized the cloak and tore it off. Then her golden hair came shining out, and she stood there in her full splendour and could hide herself no longer. And when she had washed the soot and ashes off her face, she was more beautiful than anyone who had ever been seen on earth.

But the King said, "You are my dear bride, and we shall never part from each other again."

And with that the marriage was celebrated, and they lived happily until their deaths.

The Hare’s Bride

There was once a woman and her daughter who had a pretty garden with cabbages in it; and a little hare got in and ate up all the cabbages during the winter.

Then says the mother to the daughter, "Go into the garden and chase that hare away."

The girl says to the little hare, "Shoo, shoo, hare, you're still eating up all our cabbages."

Says the hare, "Come along, my girl, and sit yourself down on my little hare's tail, and come with me to my little hare's hut."

The girl won't do it.

Next day the hare comes again and eats the cabbages; so the mother says to the daughter, "Go into the garden and drive that hare away."

The girl says to the hare, "Shoo, shoo, little hare, you're still eating all the cabbages."

The little hare says, "My girl, sit yourself down on my little hare's tail and come with me to my little hare's hut."

The girl refuses.

The third day the hare comes again and eats the cabbages. At that the mother says to the daughter, "Go into the garden and chase that hare off."

Says the girl, "Shoo, shoo, little hare, you're still eating all our cabbages."

Says the little hare, "Come along, my girl, sit yourself down on my little hare's tail and come with me to my little hare's hut."

The girl sits down on the little hare's tail, and then the hare carries her far away to his little hut, and says, "Now cook green cabbage and millet, and I'll go and invite the wedding guests."

Then all the wedding guests came together. (Who were the wedding guests? I can tell you that, just as another told it to me.) They were all hares; and the crow was there as parson to marry the bride and bridegroom, and the fox as clerk, and the altar was under the rainbow.

The girl, though, was sad, because she was all alone.

The little hare comes and says, "Open the doors, open the doors, the wedding guests are merry."

The bride says nothing, but weeps. The little hare goes away.

The little hare comes back and says, "Take off the lid, take off the lid, the wedding guests are hungry."

The bride again says nothing, and weeps. The little hare goes away.

The little hare comes back and says, "Take off the lid, take off the lid, the wedding guests are waiting."

Then the bride says nothing, and the hare goes away; but she dresses a straw doll in her clothes and gives her a spoon to stir with, and sets her by the pan of millet, and goes back to her mother.

The little hare comes once more and says, "Take off the lid, take off the lid" — and gets up and hits the doll on the head, so that her cap falls off.

Then the little hare sees that it is not his bride, and goes away, and is sorrowful.
