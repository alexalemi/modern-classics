The Valiant Little Tailor

One summer morning a little tailor was sitting on his table by the window, in good spirits and sewing with all his might. Along the street came a peasant woman crying, "Good jam, cheap! Good jam, cheap!"

That rang pleasantly in the tailor's ears. He put his delicate head out of the window and called, "Up here, my good woman — here's where you'll get rid of your goods."

The woman came up the three steps to the tailor with her heavy basket, and he made her unpack every one of the pots for him. He inspected them all, lifted them up, put his nose to them, and at last said, "The jam seems good to me. Weigh me out four ounces, my good woman — and if it comes to a quarter of a pound, that's no matter."

The woman, who had been hoping for a good sale, gave him what he asked for, but went off thoroughly annoyed and grumbling.

"Now, God bless this jam to my use," cried the little tailor, "and give me health and strength." And he fetched the bread out of the cupboard, cut himself a slice right across the loaf and spread the jam on it. "That won't taste bitter," he said, "but I'll just finish the jacket before I take a bite."

He laid the bread beside him, sewed away, and in his happiness made his stitches bigger and bigger.

Meanwhile the smell of the sweet jam went up the wall, where the flies were sitting in great numbers, and they were drawn to it and came down on it in swarms.

"Hey! Who invited you?" said the little tailor, and drove the uninvited guests away. But the flies, who understood no German, would not be driven off and came back in ever greater companies.

At last the little tailor lost all patience, got a scrap of cloth from the hole under his work table and, saying, "Wait, and I'll give it to you," struck at them without mercy. When he took the cloth away and counted, there lay no fewer than seven of them in front of him, dead, with their legs stretched out.

"Are you that sort of fellow?" he said, and could not help admiring his own bravery. "The whole town shall hear of this!"

And the little tailor hurriedly cut himself a belt, stitched it, and embroidered on it in big letters, "Seven at one stroke!"

"The town!" he went on. "The whole world shall hear of it!" And his heart wagged for joy like a lamb's tail.

The tailor put on the belt and resolved to go out into the world, because he thought his workshop was too small for such valour. Before he went, he hunted about the house to see whether there was anything he could take with him; but he found nothing except an old cheese, and that he put in his pocket. In front of the door he noticed a bird that had caught itself in a bush. That had to go into his pocket along with the cheese.

Then he took to the road boldly; and as he was light and nimble he felt no weariness. The road led him up a mountain; and when he had reached the highest point of it, there sat a powerful giant, looking about him quite comfortably.

The little tailor went up to him bravely and spoke to him: "Good day, friend. So there you sit, looking out over the wide world. I'm just on my way there myself, to try my luck. Have you any mind to come with me?"

The giant looked at the tailor contemptuously and said, "You ragamuffin! You miserable little creature!"

"Oh, indeed?" answered the little tailor, and unbuttoned his coat and showed the giant his belt. "There you may read what sort of a man I am."

The giant read, "Seven at one stroke," and supposed they had been men the tailor had killed, and began to feel a little respect for the tiny fellow. All the same, he thought he would test him first. He took a stone in his hand and squeezed it until water dripped out of it.

"Do the same," said the giant, "if you have the strength."

"Is that all?" said the tailor. "That's child's play where I come from." And he put his hand in his pocket, took out the soft cheese and squeezed it until the liquid ran out of it. "There," he said, "that was a bit better, wasn't it?"

The giant did not know what to say, and could not believe it of the little man. Then he picked up a stone and threw it so high that the eye could hardly follow it. "Now then, you mite of a man, do the same."

"Well thrown," said the tailor, "but after all, the stone came down to earth again. I'll throw you one that will never come back at all."

And he put his hand in his pocket, took out the bird and threw it into the air. The bird, delighted with its freedom, rose and flew away and did not come back.

"How do you like that shot, friend?" asked the tailor.

"You can throw, certainly," said the giant, "but now we'll see whether you can carry anything properly."

He took the little tailor to a mighty oak tree that lay felled on the ground and said, "If you're strong enough, help me carry this tree out of the forest."

"Gladly," said the little man. "You take the trunk on your shoulders, and I'll lift the branches and twigs — they're the heaviest part, after all."

The giant took the trunk on his shoulder, but the tailor sat himself down on a branch; and the giant, who could not look round, had to carry away the whole tree and the little tailor into the bargain. And the tailor behind was quite merry and happy, and whistled the song "Three tailors rode out at the gate," as if carrying the tree were child's play.

When the giant had dragged the heavy load part of the way he could go no further, and cried, "Look here, I shall have to drop the tree."

The tailor jumped nimbly down, took hold of the tree with both arms as if he had been carrying it all along, and said to the giant, "Such a big fellow as you, and you can't even carry a tree!"

They went on together; and as they were passing a cherry tree, the giant took hold of the top of it, where the ripest fruit was hanging, bent it down, put it into the tailor's hand and told him to eat. But the little tailor was far too weak to hold the tree; and when the giant let go, it sprang back and the tailor was whipped up into the air with it.

When he had come down again unhurt, the giant said, "What's this? Haven't you strength enough to hold a weak little branch?"

"There's no lack of strength," said the little tailor. "Do you suppose that would be anything to a man who struck down seven at one blow? I jumped over the tree because the huntsmen are shooting down there in the thicket. Jump it yourself, if you can."

The giant tried, but could not get over the tree and stuck fast in the branches; so the tailor kept the upper hand there too.

The giant said, "If you're such a valiant fellow, come with me to our cave and spend the night with us."

The little tailor was willing and went along. When they came into the cave, other giants were sitting there by the fire, each with a roasted sheep in his hand, eating it. The little tailor looked round and thought, "There's a good deal more room here than in my workshop."

The giant showed him a bed and told him to lie down in it and sleep. But the bed was too big for the little tailor; he did not lie down in it, but crept into a corner.

When it was midnight, and the giant thought the little tailor was sound asleep, he got up, took a great iron bar and cut the bed through with one blow, thinking he had finished off the grasshopper for good.

At the first light of dawn the giants went off into the forest, having quite forgotten the little tailor — when all at once he came walking up to them, perfectly cheerful and bold. The giants were terrified, and afraid that he would strike them all dead, and ran away in a great hurry.

The little tailor went on, always following his own sharp nose. After he had walked a long time he came to the courtyard of a royal palace; and as he was tired, he lay down on the grass and fell asleep.

While he lay there, people came and looked him over on all sides, and read on his belt, "Seven at one stroke."

"Ah," they said, "what is a great warrior doing here in the middle of peacetime? He must be a mighty lord."

They went and told the King about him, and gave it as their opinion that if war were to break out this would be a weighty and useful man, who ought on no account to be let go. The advice pleased the King, and he sent one of his courtiers to the little tailor to offer him a commission when he woke.

The messenger stood by the sleeper, waited until he stretched himself and opened his eyes, and then put the offer to him.

"That is the very reason I came here," said the tailor. "I am ready to enter the King's service."

So he was received with honour and given quarters of his own.

But the soldiers were set against the little tailor and wished him a thousand miles away. "What is to come of this?" they said among themselves. "If we quarrel with him and he lays about him, seven of us will fall at every blow. Not one of us can stand against him."

So they came to a decision, went in a body to the King and asked to be discharged. "We are not prepared," they said, "to stay with a man who kills seven at one stroke."

The King was sorry to lose all his faithful servants for the sake of one man, and wished he had never set eyes on the tailor, and would gladly have been rid of him again. But he did not dare dismiss him, for fear he should strike him and all his people dead and set himself on the royal throne.

He thought about it a long time, and at last hit on a plan. He sent to the little tailor to say that, as he was such a great warrior, he had one request to make of him. In a forest of his kingdom there lived two giants who did great damage with their robbing and murdering, their ravaging and burning, and nobody could go near them without risking his life. If the tailor conquered and killed these two giants, the King would give him his only daughter for a wife and half the kingdom for a dowry; and a hundred horsemen should go with him to help.

"That would be a fine thing for a man like me," thought the little tailor. "A man isn't offered a beautiful princess and half a kingdom every day of his life."

"Oh, yes," he answered, "I'll soon settle the giants, and I don't need a hundred horsemen to do it. A man who can kill seven at one blow needn't be afraid of two."

The little tailor set off, and the hundred horsemen followed him. When he came to the edge of the forest he said to his followers, "Just wait here — I'll soon finish off the giants by myself."

Then he sprang into the forest and looked about him, right and left. After a while he caught sight of the two giants. They lay asleep under a tree, snoring so that the branches swayed up and down.

The little tailor, not being idle, gathered two pocketfuls of stones and climbed up into the tree with them. When he was half way up he slipped along a branch until he was sitting just above the sleepers, and then let one stone after another drop onto the chest of one of the giants.

For a long time the giant felt nothing; but at last he woke, shoved his companion and said, "Why are you hitting me?"

"You must be dreaming," said the other. "I'm not hitting you."

They lay down to sleep again; and then the tailor dropped a stone onto the second one.

"What's the meaning of this?" cried the second. "Why are you pelting me?"

"I am not pelting you," growled the first.

They argued about it for a while; but as they were tired they let it drop, and their eyes closed again.

Then the little tailor began his game once more, picked out the biggest stone and threw it with all his strength onto the chest of the first giant.

"This is too much!" he cried, and sprang up like a madman and shoved his companion against the tree until it shook. The other paid him back in the same coin, and they got into such a rage that they tore up trees and belaboured each other with them so long that at last they both fell dead on the ground at the same moment.

Then the little tailor jumped down. "It's a lucky thing," he said, "that they didn't tear up the tree I was sitting in, or I'd have had to jump to another like a squirrel. But we tailors are nimble."

He drew his sword and gave each of them a couple of thrusts in the chest, and then went out to the horsemen and said, "The work is done. I've given them both their finishing stroke — but it was hard work. In their extremity they tore up trees and defended themselves with them; but none of that is any use when a man like me comes along, who can kill seven at one blow."

"Aren't you wounded?" asked the horsemen.

"Not at all," answered the tailor. "They haven't bent a hair of my head."

The horsemen would not believe him and rode into the forest; and there they found the giants swimming in their blood, with the torn-up trees lying all round about.

The little tailor demanded the promised reward from the King; but the King had repented of his promise and was thinking again how he could get rid of the hero.

"Before you have my daughter and half my kingdom," he said to him, "you must perform one more heroic deed. In the forest there roams a unicorn that does a great deal of harm, and you must catch it first."

"I'm even less afraid of one unicorn than of two giants. Seven at one blow — that's my line of business."

He took a rope and an axe with him, went out into the forest, and again told those who were sent with him to wait outside. He did not have to look long. The unicorn soon came at him, and rushed straight for him as if it meant to spit him on its horn without further ceremony.

"Steady, steady," he said. "It can't be done as fast as that." And he stood still and waited until the animal was quite close, and then sprang nimbly behind the tree.

The unicorn ran at the tree with all its might and drove its horn so fast into the trunk that it had not the strength to pull it out again, and so it was caught.

"Now I've got the bird," said the tailor; and he came out from behind the tree, put the rope round its neck, then chopped the horn out of the trunk with his axe; and when everything was ready he led the beast away and brought it to the King.

Even so the King would not give him the promised reward, and made a third demand. Before the wedding the tailor was to catch him a wild boar that was doing great damage in the forest, and the huntsmen should help him.

"Willingly," said the tailor. "That's child's play."

He did not take the huntsmen into the forest with him, and they were well pleased about it, because the wild boar had received them several times in a way that gave them no wish to lie in wait for him again.

When the boar caught sight of the tailor it ran at him with foaming mouth and gnashing tusks and was about to throw him to the ground; but the nimble hero sprang into a chapel that stood nearby, and up to the window in a moment, and out again at a single bound. The boar ran in after him; but the tailor ran round outside and shut the door behind it, and so the raging beast, which was far too heavy and clumsy to leap out of the window, was caught.

The little tailor called the huntsmen over, so that they could see the prisoner with their own eyes; and the hero went to the King, who now, whether he liked it or not, had to keep his promise, and gave him his daughter and half his kingdom. If he had known that it was no warrior hero but a little tailor standing in front of him, it would have gone to his heart even more than it did.

So the wedding was held with great magnificence and small joy, and out of a tailor a king was made.

Some time afterwards the young Queen heard her husband saying in his sleep at night, "Boy, make me that doublet and patch those trousers, or I'll rap the yardstick over your ears." Then she found out what sort of family the young lord had been born into; and next morning she complained to her father and begged him to help her get rid of a husband who was nothing but a tailor.

The King comforted her and said, "Leave your bedroom door open tonight. My servants shall stand outside; and when he has fallen asleep they shall go in, tie him up, and put him aboard a ship that will carry him away into the wide world."

The woman was satisfied with this. But the King's armour-bearer, who had heard it all, was friendly with the young lord and told him the whole plot.

"I'll put a stop to that," said the little tailor.

That night he went to bed with his wife at the usual time; and when she thought he had fallen asleep she got up, opened the door, and lay down again. The little tailor, who was only pretending to sleep, began to call out in a clear voice, "Boy, make me that doublet and patch me those trousers, or I'll rap the yardstick over your ears! I struck seven at one blow, I killed two giants, I led away a unicorn and I caught a wild boar — and am I to be afraid of the men standing outside my room?"

When those men heard the tailor talking like that, a great terror came over them and they ran as if the wild huntsman were behind them, and not one of them would venture anything further against him.

So the little tailor was a king, and remained one to the end of his life.
