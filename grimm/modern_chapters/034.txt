The Twelve Huntsmen

There was once a King's son who was betrothed to a girl he loved very much. And once when he was sitting beside her, very happy, news came that his father lay sick to death and wanted to see him once more before the end.

So he said to his beloved, "I must go now and leave you. I give you this ring to remember me by. When I am King I'll come back and fetch you."

So he rode away; and when he reached his father, the old man was dangerously ill and near his death. He said to him, "Dear son, I wanted to see you once more before my end. Promise me to marry as I wish" — and he named a certain King's daughter who was to be his wife.

The son was in such distress that he did not think what he was doing, and said, "Yes, dear father, your will shall be done." And with that the King closed his eyes and died.

So when the son had been proclaimed King and the time of mourning was over, he was obliged to keep the promise he had given his father, and had the King's daughter asked for in marriage, and she was promised to him.

His first betrothed heard of it, and grieved so much over his faithlessness that she nearly died. Then her father said to her, "Dearest child, why are you so sad? You shall have whatever you want."

She thought for a moment and said, "Dear father, I want eleven girls exactly like me in face and figure and height."

The father said, "If it's possible, your wish shall be granted." And he had a search made through his whole kingdom until eleven young girls were found who were the exact likeness of his daughter in face and figure and height.

When they came to the King's daughter, she had twelve suits of huntsmen's clothes made, all alike; and the eleven girls had to put on the huntsmen's clothes, and she herself put on the twelfth suit. Then she took leave of her father and rode away with them, and rode to the court of the man she had been betrothed to and loved so dearly.

There she asked whether he needed any huntsmen, and whether he would take all of them into his service. The King looked at her and did not know her; but as they were such handsome fellows he said yes, he would gladly take them. And now they were the King's twelve huntsmen.

But the King had a lion that was a marvellous creature, for it knew everything hidden and secret. One evening it happened that the lion said to the King, "You think you have twelve huntsmen?"

"Yes," said the King, "they are twelve huntsmen."

The lion went on, "You're mistaken. They are twelve girls."

The King said, "That can't be true. How will you prove it to me?"

"Oh, just have some peas scattered in your antechamber," answered the lion, "and then you'll soon see. Men have a firm tread, and when they walk over peas not one of them stirs; but girls trip and skip and drag their feet, and the peas roll about."

The King was well pleased with the advice, and had the peas scattered.

But there was a servant of the King's who was fond of the huntsmen; and when he heard that they were to be put to this test he went to them and told them everything, and said, "The lion wants to make the King believe you're girls."

Then the King's daughter thanked him, and said to her girls, "Put some strength into it, and tread firmly on the peas."

So next morning, when the King had the twelve huntsmen called before him and they came into the antechamber where the peas were lying, they trod so firmly on them, and walked so strongly and surely, that not one pea rolled or stirred. Then they went out again, and the King said to the lion, "You've lied to me. They walk just like men."

The lion said, "They found out that they were going to be tested, and put on some strength. Just have twelve spinning wheels brought into the antechamber one day, and they'll go up to them and be pleased with them — and that's what no man would do."

The King liked this advice, and had the spinning wheels set out in the antechamber.

But the servant, who was well disposed to the huntsmen, went and told them the plan. Then, when they were alone, the King's daughter said to her eleven girls, "Keep a hold on yourselves, and don't look round at the spinning wheels."

And next morning, when the King had his twelve huntsmen summoned, they went through the antechamber and never once looked at the spinning wheels.

Then the King said to the lion again, "You've deceived me. They're men — they didn't look at the spinning wheels."

The lion replied, "They found out that they were going to be tested, and held themselves in."

But the King would not believe the lion any more.

The twelve huntsmen always went with the King to the chase, and his liking for them kept growing. Now it happened once, when they were out hunting, that news came that the King's betrothed was on her way. When the true bride heard it, it hurt her so much that her heart was nearly broken, and she fell fainting to the ground.

The King thought something had happened to his dear huntsman, and ran to him and tried to help him, and pulled off his glove. Then he saw the ring he had given his first bride; and when he looked in her face he knew her. Then his heart was so touched that he kissed her; and when she opened her eyes he said, "You are mine and I am yours, and nobody in the world can alter that."

He sent a messenger to the other bride and begged her to go back to her own kingdom, since he had a wife already, and a man who has just found an old dish has no need of a new one.

And with that the wedding was celebrated, and the lion was taken back into favour — because after all it had told the truth.

The Thief and his Master

Hans wanted to have his son taught a trade, so he went into the church and prayed to our Lord God to tell him which would do the boy most good. Then the clerk got behind the altar and said, "Thieving, thieving."

So Hans goes back to his son and tells him he is to learn thieving, and that the Lord God has said so. And he goes off with his son to look for a man who understands thieving.

They walk a long while and come into a great forest, where there stands a little house with an old woman in it. Hans says, "Do you know of a man who understands thieving?"

"You can learn that here well enough," says the woman. "My son is a master of it."

So he speaks with the son and asks whether he really knows thieving well.

The master thief says, "I'll teach him properly. Come back when a year is out; and then, if you recognise your son, I'll take no payment at all for teaching him; but if you don't know him, you must give me two hundred thalers."

The father goes home again, and the son learns witchcraft and thieving thoroughly.

When the year is out, the father is full of worry about how he is to manage to recognise his son. And as he is going about in his trouble, he meets a little dwarf, who says, "Man, what's the matter with you, that you're always in such distress?"

"Oh," says Hans, "a year ago I placed my son with a master thief, who told me to come back when the year was out; and that if I then didn't know my son when I saw him, I was to pay two hundred thalers, but if I did know him I was to pay nothing. And now I'm afraid I shan't know him, and I can't think where I'm to get the money."

Then the dwarf tells him to take a little basket of bread with him and stand under the chimney. "There on the crossbeam is a basket with a little bird peeping out of it, and that is your son."

Hans goes there and throws down a little basket full of black bread in front of the basket with the bird in it; and the little bird comes out and looks up.

"Hullo, my son, are you there?" says the father; and the son is delighted to see his father.

But the master thief says, "The devil must have prompted you — how else could you have known your son?"

"Father, let's go," said the boy.

Then father and son set out for home. On the way a carriage comes driving by, and the son says to his father, "I'll change myself into a big greyhound, and then you can make a lot of money out of me."

Then the gentleman calls out of the carriage, "My man, will you sell your dog?"

"Yes," says the father.

"How much do you want for him?"

"Thirty thalers."

"Well, man, that's a great deal; but as he's such a very fine dog I'll have him."

The gentleman takes him into the carriage; but when they have driven a little way, the dog springs out of the carriage window and goes back to his father, and is a greyhound no longer.

They go home together. Next day there is a fair in the next town, so the young man says to his father, "Now I'll change myself into a beautiful horse, and you can sell me. But when you've sold me, you must take off my bridle, or I can't become a man again."

So the father goes to the fair with the horse; and the master thief comes and buys the horse for a hundred thalers. But the father forgets, and does not take the bridle off.

So the man goes home with the horse and puts it in the stable. When the maid crosses the threshold, the horse says, "Take off my bridle, take off my bridle."

Then the maid stands still and says, "What — can you talk?" And she goes and takes the bridle off; and the horse turns into a sparrow and flies out at the door, and the wizard turns into a sparrow too and flies after him.

Then they come together and cast lots; but the master loses, and takes to the water and is a fish. Then the young man becomes a fish as well, and they cast lots again, and the master loses. So the master changes himself into a cock, and the young man becomes a fox and bites the master's head off; and he died, and has stayed dead to this day.

Jorinda and Joringel

There was once an old castle in the middle of a large, thick forest, and in it an old woman who was a witch lived all alone. By day she changed herself into a cat or a screech owl, but in the evening she took her proper human shape again. She could lure wild animals and birds to her, and then she killed them and boiled and roasted them.

If anyone came within a hundred paces of the castle he was forced to stand still and could not move from the spot until she gave him leave. But whenever an innocent girl came inside this circle, she changed her into a bird and shut her up in a wickerwork cage and carried the cage into a room in the castle. She had about seven thousand cages of rare birds in that castle.

Now there was once a girl called Jorinda, who was lovelier than all other girls. She and a handsome young man named Joringel had promised to marry each other. They were still in their days of betrothal, and their greatest happiness was to be together.

One day, so that they could talk quietly, they went for a walk in the forest. "Take care," said Joringel, "not to go too near the castle."

It was a beautiful evening; the sun shone brightly between the tree trunks into the dark green of the forest, and the turtle doves sang mournfully on the young boughs of the birches.

Jorinda wept now and then; she sat down in the sunshine and was sorrowful. Joringel was sorrowful too. They were as sad as if they were about to die. Then they looked about them and were quite at a loss, for they did not know which way they should go to get home. The sun was still half above the mountain and half set.

Joringel looked through the bushes and saw the old walls of the castle close at hand. He was horror-struck and filled with a deadly fear. Jorinda was singing:

	"My little bird, with the necklace red,
	sings sorrow, sorrow, sorrow;
	he sings that the dove must soon be dead,
	sings sorrow, sor — jug, jug, jug."

Joringel looked for Jorinda. She had been changed into a nightingale, and sang, "jug, jug, jug." A screech owl with glowing eyes flew three times round about her, and three times cried, "to-whoo, to-whoo, to-whoo!"

Joringel could not move. He stood there like a stone, and could not weep or speak or move hand or foot.

The sun had set now. The owl flew into the thicket, and directly afterwards a crooked old woman came out of it, yellow and lean, with great red eyes and a hooked nose whose tip reached her chin. She muttered to herself, caught the nightingale and carried it away in her hand.

Joringel could neither speak nor stir from the spot; the nightingale was gone. At last the woman came back and said in a hollow voice, "Greetings, Zachiel. When the moon shines on the cage, Zachiel, let him loose at once."

Then Joringel was free. He fell on his knees before the woman and begged her to give him back his Jorinda; but she said he should never have her again, and went away. He called, he wept, he lamented, but all in vain. "Ah, what is to become of me?"

Joringel went away and came at last to a strange village, where he kept sheep for a long time. He often walked round and round the castle, but never too close to it.

At last he dreamed one night that he found a blood-red flower with a beautiful large pearl in the middle of it; that he picked the flower and went with it to the castle, and that everything he touched with the flower was freed from enchantment. He dreamed too that by means of it he got his Jorinda back.

In the morning when he woke, he began to search over hill and dale to see whether he could find such a flower. He searched until the ninth day, and then, early in the morning, he found the blood-red flower. In the middle of it there was a great dewdrop, as big as the finest pearl.

Day and night he travelled with this flower to the castle. When he came within a hundred paces of it he was not held fast, but walked on to the door. Joringel was full of joy. He touched the door with the flower and it sprang open. He walked in across the courtyard and listened for the sound of the birds. At last he heard it. He went on and found the room it came from, and there the witch was, feeding the birds in the seven thousand cages.

When she saw Joringel she was angry — very angry — and scolded and spat poison and gall at him; but she could not come within two paces of him. He took no notice of her, but went and looked over the cages of birds. But there were many hundreds of nightingales in them. How was he to find his Jorinda again?

Just then he saw the old woman quietly taking away a cage with a bird in it, and going towards the door.

In a flash he sprang after her, touched the cage with the flower, and the old woman too. Now she could bewitch nobody any more; and Jorinda was standing there with her arms round his neck, and she was as beautiful as ever.

The Three Sons of Fortune

A father once called his three sons before him and gave the first a cock, the second a scythe and the third a cat.

"I'm an old man now," he said. "My death is near, and I wanted to make some provision for you before my end. Money I have none, and what I'm giving you now seems worth very little; but it all depends on your making sensible use of it. Only find a country where such things are still unknown, and your fortune is made."

After the father's death the eldest went off with his cock; but wherever he came the cock was known already. In the towns he could see one from a long way off, sitting on the steeples and turning round with the wind; and in the villages he heard more than one of them crowing. Nobody would show the least wonder at the creature, so it did not look as if he would make his fortune by it.

At last, though, it happened that he came to an island where the people knew nothing about cocks, and did not even understand how to divide up their time. They knew well enough when it was morning or evening; but at night, if they did not sleep through it, not one of them could tell the time.

"Look," said he, "what a proud creature this is! It has a ruby-red crown on its head and wears spurs like a knight; it calls you three times in the night, at fixed hours; and when it calls the last time the sun soon rises. But if it crows in broad daylight, take notice, for there'll certainly be a change in the weather."

The people were delighted. For a whole night they did not sleep, and listened with great pleasure as the cock proclaimed the time loudly and clearly at two, four and six o'clock. They asked whether the creature was for sale, and how much he wanted for it.

"About as much gold as a donkey can carry," he answered.

"A ridiculously small price for such a precious creature!" they all cried, and gladly gave him what he had asked.

When he came home with his wealth his brothers were astonished; and the second said, "Well, I'll go out and see whether I can't get rid of my scythe as profitably."

But it did not look as if he could, for he met labourers everywhere, and they had scythes on their shoulders as well as he.

At last, though, he happened on an island where the people knew nothing about scythes. When the corn was ripe there they took cannon out into the fields and shot it down. Now that was a rather uncertain business: many shot right over it, and others hit the ears instead of the stalks and shot them clean away, so that a great deal was lost; and on top of it all it made a terrible noise. So the man set to work and mowed the corn down so quietly and quickly that the people gaped with astonishment. They agreed to give him whatever he asked for the scythe, and he was given a horse loaded with as much gold as it could carry.

And now the third brother wanted to take his cat to the right man. It went with him just as it had with the others: as long as he stayed on the mainland there was nothing to be done. Every place had cats, and there were so many of them that newborn kittens were generally drowned in the ponds.

At last he sailed over to an island, and it luckily happened that no cats had ever been seen there, and that the mice had got the upper hand so completely that they danced on the tables and benches whether the master of the house was at home or not. The people complained bitterly of the plague. The King himself, in his palace, did not know how to protect himself from them; mice squeaked in every corner and gnawed whatever they could get their teeth into.

But now the cat began her hunting, and soon cleared a couple of rooms; and the people begged the King to buy the wonderful animal for the country. The King gladly gave what was asked, which was a mule loaded with gold; and the third brother came home with the greatest treasure of all.

The cat, meanwhile, was making merry with the mice in the royal palace, and killed so many that they could not be counted. At last she grew hot with the work and thirsty, so she stood still, lifted up her head and cried, "Mew! Mew!"

When they heard this strange cry, the King and all his people were frightened, and in their terror ran out of the palace all together. Then the King took counsel about what was best to be done; and at last it was decided to send a herald to the cat and demand that she leave the palace, and to warn her that if she did not, she must expect force to be used against her. The councillors said, "We'd rather be plagued with the mice — we're used to that misfortune — than give up our lives to a monster like this."

So a young nobleman was sent to ask the cat whether she would quit the castle peaceably. But the cat, whose thirst had grown greater still, only answered, "Mew! Mew!"

The young man understood her to say, "Most certainly not! Most certainly not!" — and took this answer back to the King.

"Then," said the councillors, "she shall yield to force."

Cannon were brought up, and the palace was soon in flames. When the fire reached the room where the cat was sitting, she sprang safely out of the window; but the besiegers did not leave off until the whole palace had been shot to the ground.
