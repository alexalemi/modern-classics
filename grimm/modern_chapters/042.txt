The Young Giant

Once upon a time a countryman had a son who was no bigger than a thumb, and did not get any bigger, and for several years did not grow so much as a hair's breadth.

Once when the father was going out to plough, the little one said, "Father, I'll come out with you."

"You'd come out with me?" said his father. "Stay here. You'll be no use out there — and besides, you might get lost."

Then Thumbling began to cry; and for the sake of peace his father put him in his pocket and took him along. When he got out into the field he took him out again and set him down in a freshly cut furrow.

While he was sitting there, a great giant came over the hill. "Do you see that big bogeyman?" said his father, wanting to frighten the little fellow into being good. "He's coming to fetch you."

But the giant had hardly taken two steps with his long legs before he was at the furrow. He picked little Thumbling up carefully between two fingers, looked him over, and went off with him without saying a word. His father stood there, but could not utter a sound for terror, and thought nothing else but that his child was lost, and that as long as he lived he would never set eyes on him again.

The giant, though, carried him home and nursed him; and Thumbling grew tall and strong after the fashion of giants.

When two years had gone by, the old giant took him into the forest to try him out, and said, "Pull yourself up a stick."

By then the boy was already so strong that he tore a young tree out of the earth by the roots. But the giant thought, "We must do better than that" — and took him back and nursed him another two years.

When he tried him then, his strength had grown so much that he could tear an old tree out of the ground. That was still not enough for the giant; he nursed him for two years more, and then when he took him into the forest and said, "Now tear me up a proper stick," the boy tore the strongest oak tree out of the earth so that it split — and it was a mere trifle to him.

"That will do," said the giant. "You're finished now" — and took him back to the field he had brought him from.

His father was there, following the plough. The young giant went up to him and said, "Does my father see what a fine man his son has grown into?"

The farmer was alarmed and said, "No, you're not my son. I don't want you — get away from me!"

"But I truly am your son. Let me do your work; I can plough as well as you — better, in fact."

"No, no, you're not my son, and you can't plough. Go away!"

But as he was afraid of this huge man, he let go of the plough and stepped back and stood at the edge of the field. Then the young man took the plough and just pressed on it with one hand; but his grip was so strong that the plough went deep into the earth.

The farmer could not bear to see that, and called out to him, "If you're set on ploughing, you mustn't press so hard. That makes bad work."

But the young man unharnessed the horses and pulled the plough himself, saying, "Just go home, father, and tell my mother to get ready a large dish of food; and meanwhile I'll go over the field."

So the farmer went home and told his wife to prepare the food, while the young man ploughed the field, which was two acres, entirely on his own; and then he harnessed himself to the harrow and harrowed the whole of the land, using two harrows at once.

When he had finished he went into the forest and pulled up two oak trees and laid them across his shoulders, and hung one harrow on them behind and one in front, and one horse behind and one in front too, and carried the lot to his parents' house as if it were a bundle of straw.

When he came into the yard his mother did not know him, and asked, "Who is that horrible tall man?"

The farmer said, "That's our son."

She said, "No, that can't be our son. We never had a tall one; ours was a little thing." And she called out to him, "Go away — we don't want you!"

The young man said nothing, but led his horses to the stable and gave them oats and hay and everything they needed. When he had done that, he went into the parlour, sat down on the bench and said, "Mother, now I should like something to eat. Will it be ready soon?"

Then she said yes, and brought in two enormous dishes full of food, which would have been enough for her and her husband for a week. But the young man ate the whole of it himself, and asked whether she had nothing more to set in front of him.

"No," she replied, "that's all we have."

"But that was only a taste. I must have more."

She did not dare oppose him, and went and put a huge cauldron of food on the fire; and when it was ready she carried it in.

"At last, a few crumbs," said he, and ate everything there was — and it still was not enough to satisfy his hunger.

Then he said, "Father, I can see plainly that with you I shall never get enough to eat. If you'll get me an iron staff that's strong, one I can't break across my knees, I'll go out into the world."

The farmer was glad, and put his two horses in his cart and fetched from the smith a staff so large and thick that the two horses could only just bring it away. The young man laid it across his knees and — snap! — broke it in two in the middle like a beanstalk, and threw it away.

Then his father harnessed four horses and brought a bar so long and thick that the four horses could only just drag it. His son snapped that across his knees too, threw it away, and said, "Father, this is no use to me. You must harness more horses and bring a stronger staff."

So the father harnessed eight horses and brought one so long and thick that the eight horses could only just carry it. But when his son took it in his hand he broke a bit off the top of it as well, and said, "Father, I can see that you won't be able to get me the sort of staff I want. I'll stay with you no longer."

So he went away and gave out that he was a smith's apprentice. He came to a village where there lived a smith who was a greedy fellow and never did anybody a kindness, but wanted everything for himself. The young man went into the smithy and asked whether he needed a journeyman.

"Yes," said the smith, and looked him over, and thought, "There's a strong fellow who will strike out well and earn his bread." So he asked, "How much wages do you want?"

"I don't want any at all," he replied. "Only, every fortnight, when the other journeymen are paid, I'll give you two blows, and you must bear them."

The miser was heartily satisfied, and thought he would save a lot of money that way.

Next morning the new journeyman was to start work; but when the master brought the glowing bar and the young man struck his first blow, the iron flew apart and the anvil sank so deep into the earth that there was no getting it out again.

Then the miser grew angry and said, "Oh, but I can make no use of you. You strike far too hard. What will you have for the one blow?"

Then he said, "I'll only give you quite a small blow, that's all." And he raised his foot and gave him such a kick that he flew away over four loads of hay. Then he picked out the thickest iron bar in the smithy for himself, took it in his hand for a walking stick, and went on his way.

When he had walked some time he came to a small farm, and asked the bailiff whether he did not need a head servant.

"Yes," said the bailiff, "I can make use of one. You look a strong fellow who can do something. How much a year do you want in wages?"

He answered again that he wanted no wages at all, but that every year he would give him three blows, which he must bear. The bailiff was satisfied with that, for he too was a covetous man.

Next morning all the servants were to go into the wood; the others were already up, but the head servant was still in bed. Then one of them called to him, "Get up, it's time. We're going into the wood and you must come with us."

"Ah," said he, quite roughly and surlily, "you go on, then. I'll be back again before any of you."

So the others went to the bailiff and told him the head man was still lying in bed and would not go into the wood with them. The bailiff said they were to wake him again and tell him to harness the horses. But the head man said as before, "Just go on, I'll be back again before any of you." And then he stayed in bed two hours longer.

At length he got out of the feathers; but first he fetched himself two bushels of peas from the loft and made himself some broth with them, and ate it at his leisure; and when that was done he went and harnessed the horses and drove into the wood.

Not far from the wood there was a ravine he had to pass through; so he first drove the horses on through it, and then stopped them and went round behind the cart and took trees and brushwood and made a great barricade, so that no horse could get through.

As he was going into the wood, the others were just driving out of it with their loaded carts to go home; so he said to them, "Drive on. I shall still get home before you do."

He did not drive far into the wood, but immediately tore up two of the very largest trees of all and threw them on his cart and turned round. When he came to the barricade the others were still standing there, unable to get through.

"Don't you see," said he, "that if you'd stayed with me you'd have got home just as fast, and had another hour's sleep besides?"

Now he wanted to drive on, but his horses could not work their way through; so he unharnessed them and laid them on top of the cart, took the shafts in his own hands and pulled the whole lot through — and did it as easily as if it had been loaded with feathers.

When he was over he said to the others, "There, you see — I've got through quicker than you." And he drove on, and the others had to stay where they were.

In the yard he took one of the trees in his hand and showed it to the bailiff and said, "Isn't that a fine bundle of firewood?"

Then the bailiff said to his wife, "That servant is a good one. Even if he does sleep late, he's still home before the others."

So he served the bailiff for a year; and when the year was up and the other servants were getting their wages, he said it was time for him to take his too.

But the bailiff was afraid of the blows he was to be given, and earnestly begged to be let off them; sooner than that, he said, he would be head servant himself and the young man should be bailiff.

"No," said he. "I won't be a bailiff. I'm head servant and I'll stay head servant; but I'll deliver what we agreed on."

The bailiff was willing to give him anything he asked, but it was no use; the head servant said no to everything. Then the bailiff did not know what to do, and begged for a fortnight's grace, for he wanted to find some way out. The head servant agreed to the delay.

The bailiff called all his clerks together, and they were to think it over and give him their advice. The clerks pondered a long time, but at last they said that nobody's life was safe with the head servant, since he could kill a man as easily as a midge; and that the bailiff ought to make him go down the well and clean it, and when he was down at the bottom they would roll up one of the millstones lying there and drop it on his head, and then he would never come up to daylight again.

The advice pleased the bailiff, and the head servant was perfectly willing to go down the well. But when he was standing at the bottom, and they had rolled the biggest millstone down and thought they had broken his skull, he called up, "Chase those hens away from the well! They're scratching in the sand up there and throwing the grains into my eyes, so that I can't see."

So the bailiff cried, "Shoo, shoo!" and pretended to be frightening the hens away.

When the head servant had finished his work he climbed up and said, "Just look what a beautiful necktie I've got on" — and behold, it was the millstone he was wearing round his neck.

Now the head servant wanted to take his reward; but the bailiff begged for another fortnight's grace. The clerks met again and advised him to send the head servant to the haunted mill to grind corn by night, since from there no man had ever yet come back alive in the morning.

The proposal pleased the bailiff. He called the head servant that very evening and told him to take eight bushels of corn to the mill and grind it that night, because it was wanted.

So the head servant went to the loft and put two bushels in his right pocket and two in his left, and took four in a bag, half on his back and half on his chest, and went off to the haunted mill loaded like that.

The miller told him that he could grind there very well by day, but not by night, because the mill was haunted, and that up to now whoever went into it by night had been found lying dead inside in the morning.

He said, "I'll manage it. Just go off to bed."

Then he went into the mill and poured out the corn. About eleven o'clock he went into the miller's room and sat down on the bench. When he had sat there a while, a door suddenly opened and a great table came in; and wine and roast meats set themselves out on the table, and plenty of other good food besides — but everything came by itself, for there was nobody there to carry it. After that the chairs pushed themselves up, but no people came; until all at once he saw fingers, which handled knives and forks and laid food on the plates, but apart from that he could see nothing.

As he was hungry and saw the food, he sat down at the table too, and ate with those who were eating, and enjoyed it. When he had had enough, and the others had emptied their dishes too, he distinctly heard all the candles being suddenly snuffed out; and as it was now pitch dark, he felt something like a box on the ear.

Then he said, "If anything of that sort comes again, I shall hit back."

And when he got a second box on the ear, he hit back too. And so it went on the whole night through. He took nothing without returning it, but paid everything back with interest, and did not lay about him for nothing. At daybreak it all stopped.

When the miller got up he went to look for him and wondered whether he was still alive. Then the young man said, "I've eaten my fill, and I got a few boxes on the ears — but I gave some back."

The miller rejoiced, and said that the mill was released from its spell now, and wanted to give him a great deal of money as a reward. But he said, "Money I won't have. I have enough of it."

So he took his meal on his back and went home, and told the bailiff that he had done what he had been told to do, and would now have the reward they had agreed on.

When the bailiff heard that, he was seriously alarmed and quite beside himself. He walked up and down the room, and the sweat ran down his forehead in drops. Then he opened the window to get some fresh air; but before he knew what was happening, the head servant had given him such a kick that he flew out through the window into the air, and so far away that nobody ever saw him again.

Then the head servant said to the bailiff's wife, "If he doesn't come back, you must take the other blow."

She cried, "No, no, I can't bear it!" — and opened the other window, because the sweat was running down her forehead in drops. Then he gave her such a kick that she flew out too; and as she was lighter, she went much higher than her husband.

Her husband called, "Do come over to me!" — but she answered, "You come to me. I can't come to you."

And they hovered about up there in the air and could not get to each other; and whether they are still hovering about or not, I don't know. But the young giant picked up his iron bar and went on his way.
