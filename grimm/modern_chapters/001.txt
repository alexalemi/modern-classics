The Story of the Youth Who Went Forth to Learn What Fear Was

A certain father had two sons. The elder was sharp and sensible and could do anything, but the younger was slow and could neither learn nor understand a thing; and when people saw him they said, "There's a lad who'll give his father some trouble."

Whenever there was something to be done, it was always the elder who had to do it. But if his father sent him to fetch something late in the evening, or at night, and the way led past the churchyard or any other gloomy place, he would say, "Oh no, father, I'm not going there — it gives me the shudders." Because he was afraid.

Or in the evening, when stories were told by the fire that made your flesh creep, the listeners would sometimes say, "Oh, that gives me the shudders!" The younger boy sat in his corner and listened with the rest, and could not imagine what they meant. "They're always saying it gives them the shudders, it gives them the shudders. It doesn't give me the shudders," he thought. "That must be another one of these skills I know nothing about."

Now one day his father said to him, "Listen to me, you there in the corner. You're growing tall and strong, and it's time you learned something to earn your living by. Look how your brother works — you don't even earn your salt."

"Well, father," he answered, "I'm quite willing to learn something. In fact, if it could be managed, I'd like to learn how to shudder. I don't understand that at all yet."

The elder brother laughed when he heard it, and thought to himself, "Good God, what a blockhead my brother is. He'll never be any good as long as he lives. If you're going to be a sickle, you have to start bending early."

The father sighed and answered, "You'll soon enough learn what it is to shudder, but you won't earn your bread by it."

Soon afterwards the sexton came to the house on a visit, and the father poured out his troubles and told him how backward his younger son was in every way — he knew nothing and learned nothing. "Just think," he said, "when I asked him how he was going to earn his bread, he actually said he wanted to learn how to shudder."

"If that's all," said the sexton, "he can learn that with me. Send him over and I'll soon polish him up."

The father was glad to agree, because he thought, "It will lick the boy into shape a little." So the sexton took him into his house, and his job was to ring the bell.

After a day or two the sexton woke him at midnight and told him to get up and go to the church tower and ring the bell. "You'll soon find out what shuddering is," he thought; and he went up secretly ahead of him. And when the boy was at the top of the tower and turned round and was just reaching for the bell rope, he saw a white figure standing on the stairs opposite the sound hole.

"Who's there?" he called. But the figure gave no answer and did not move or stir.

"Answer me," called the boy, "or clear off. You've no business here at night."

But the sexton stood there without moving, so that the boy would take him for a ghost. The boy called a second time: "What do you want here? Speak up, if you're an honest man, or I'll throw you down the steps."

The sexton thought, "He can't really mean it," and made no sound and stood as if he were made of stone. So the boy called out to him a third time; and when that did no good either, he ran at him and pushed the ghost down the stairs, so that it fell ten steps and lay there in a corner.

Then he rang the bell, went home, got into bed without a word and fell asleep.

The sexton's wife waited a long time for her husband, but he did not come back. At last she grew uneasy, woke the boy, and asked, "Do you know where my husband is? He climbed up the tower before you did."

"No, I don't," said the boy. "But there was somebody standing by the sound hole on the other side of the steps, and as he wouldn't answer and wouldn't go away, I took him for a scoundrel and threw him down the stairs. Go and see whether it was him. I should be sorry if it was."

The woman ran off and found her husband lying in the corner, groaning, with a broken leg.

She carried him down, and then, screaming at the top of her voice, she rushed to the boy's father. "That boy of yours," she cried, "has caused a great misfortune! He threw my husband down the steps and broke his leg. Take the good-for-nothing out of our house."

The father was horrified. He ran over and scolded the boy. "What wicked tricks are these? The devil himself must have put it into your head."

"Father," he answered, "do listen to me. I am completely innocent. He was standing there in the night like a man who means some harm. I didn't know who it was, and I begged him three times to speak or go away."

"Ah," said the father, "you bring me nothing but misery. Get out of my sight. I don't want to see you again."

"Gladly, father — only wait till daylight. Then I'll go out and learn how to shudder, and at least I'll have one skill to keep me."

"Learn what you like," said the father. "It's all the same to me. Here are fifty thalers. Take them and go out into the wide world, and tell nobody where you come from or who your father is, because I've reason to be ashamed of you."

"Yes, father, just as you wish. If that's all you want of me, it's easy enough to remember."

So when the day dawned the boy put his fifty thalers in his pocket and set off along the highway, saying to himself over and over, "If only I could shudder! If only I could shudder!"

Then a man came up who heard him talking to himself; and when they had walked a little further, to where they could see the gallows, the man said, "Look, there's the tree where seven men have married the ropemaker's daughter and are now learning to fly. Sit down underneath it and wait till night comes, and you'll soon learn how to shudder."

"If that's all it takes," said the boy, "it's easily done. And if I learn to shudder as fast as that, you shall have my fifty thalers. Just come back to me first thing in the morning."

So the boy went to the gallows, sat down underneath and waited for evening. And as he was cold he lit himself a fire; but at midnight the wind blew so sharply that even with his fire he could not get warm. And when the wind knocked the hanged men against one another so that they swung backwards and forwards, he thought, "Here am I shivering by the fire — how those poor fellows up there must be freezing."

And because he felt sorry for them, he set up the ladder, climbed up, untied them one after another and brought all seven down. Then he stirred the fire and blew it up and sat them round it to warm themselves. But they sat there without moving, and the fire caught their clothes.

So he said, "Careful, or I'll hang you up again." But the dead men did not hear, and stayed silent, and let their rags go on burning.

At that he grew angry and said, "If you won't take care, I can't help you — and I'm not going to be burnt along with you." And he hung them all up again, one by one. Then he sat down by his fire and fell asleep.

Next morning the man came for his fifty thalers and said, "Well, do you know what it is to shudder now?"

"No," he said. "How was I supposed to find out? Those fellows up there never opened their mouths, and they were so stupid they let the few old rags they had on their bodies get burnt."

Then the man saw he was not going to get the fifty thalers that day, and went off saying, "I never met anybody like that before."

The boy went on his way too, and began muttering to himself again, "Ah, if only I could shudder! Ah, if only I could shudder!"

A carter walking behind him heard it and asked, "Who are you?"

"I don't know."

"Where do you come from?"

"I don't know."

"Who is your father?"

"That I mustn't tell you."

"What is it you keep muttering through your teeth?"

"Ah," said the boy, "I do so wish I could shudder, and nobody can teach me how."

"Give up that foolish talk," said the carter. "Come along with me and I'll see about finding you a place."

So the boy went with the carter, and in the evening they came to an inn where they meant to spend the night. And at the door of the room the boy said again, quite loudly, "If only I could shudder! If only I could shudder!"

The landlord heard him and laughed and said, "If that's what you want, there should be a good opportunity for you here."

"Oh, be quiet," said the landlady. "Too many inquisitive people have lost their lives already. It would be a pity and a shame if such fine eyes as those never saw daylight again."

But the boy said, "However hard it may be, I mean to learn it. That's what I came out for."

He gave the landlord no peace until the man told him that not far off there stood a haunted castle, where anybody could easily enough learn what shuddering was, if he would only keep watch in it for three nights. The King had promised that whoever dared to do it should have his daughter for a wife, and she was the most beautiful girl the sun ever shone on. There were great treasures in the castle too, guarded by evil spirits, and those treasures would then be set free and would make a poor man rich. Plenty of men had gone into the castle, but not one had ever come out again.

So next morning the boy went to the King and said that, if he were allowed, he would keep watch three nights in the haunted castle. The King looked him over, and as he liked the look of him, he said, "You may ask for three things to take into the castle with you, but they must be things that have no life in them."

He answered, "Then I ask for a fire, a wood-turner's lathe, and a cutting-board with the knife."

The King had these things carried into the castle for him during the day.

When night was drawing on, the boy went up, made himself a good bright fire in one of the rooms, set the cutting-board and knife beside it, and sat down by the lathe. "Ah, if only I could shudder," he said. "But I shan't learn it here either."

Towards midnight he was about to poke up his fire; and as he was blowing at it, something suddenly cried out from a corner, "Ow, miaow! How cold we are!"

"You fools," he called, "what are you howling about? If you're cold, come and sit by the fire and warm yourselves."

And when he had said it, two great black cats came with one tremendous leap and sat down on either side of him, staring at him savagely with their fiery eyes. After a while, when they had warmed themselves, they said, "Friend, shall we have a game of cards?"

"Why not?" he said. "But show me your paws first."

So they stretched out their claws.

"Well!" he said. "What long nails you have. Wait, I'll have to cut them for you first."

With that he took them by the throat, laid them on the cutting-board and screwed their feet down fast. "I've had a look at your fingers," he said, "and I've gone off the idea of card-playing." And he struck them dead and threw them out into the water.

But when he had got rid of those two and was about to sit down by his fire again, out of every hole and corner came black cats and black dogs with red-hot chains, more and more of them, until he could not move; and they howled horribly, and got on his fire and pulled it apart and tried to put it out.

He watched them quietly for a while; but at last, when they went too far, he took hold of his cutting-knife and cried, "Away with you, vermin!" and set about cutting them down. Some of them ran off, the rest he killed and threw out into the fish-pond.

When he came back he fanned the embers of his fire up again and warmed himself. And as he sat there his eyes would not stay open any longer, and he felt like sleeping. So he looked round and saw a great bed in the corner. "That's just the thing for me," he said, and got into it.

But just as he was going to shut his eyes, the bed began to move by itself and went all over the castle. "That's the way," he said. "Only go faster."

Then the bed rolled on as if six horses were harnessed to it, up and down, over thresholds and stairs — and then suddenly, hop, hop, it turned upside down and lay on top of him like a mountain. But he threw the quilts and pillows up in the air, climbed out and said, "Now anybody who likes can drive"; and he lay down by his fire and slept till daylight.

In the morning the King came; and when he saw him lying there on the floor, he thought the evil spirits had killed him and he was dead. So he said, "It's a pity, after all — he was a fine-looking man."

The boy heard it, sat up and said, "It hasn't come to that yet."

The King was astonished, but very glad, and asked how he had got on.

"Very well indeed," he said. "One night's over, and I'll get through the other two as well."

Then he went to the innkeeper, who opened his eyes very wide and said, "I never expected to see you alive again. Have you learnt to shudder yet?"

"No," he said. "It's all no use. If only somebody would tell me how."

The second night he went up into the old castle again, sat down by the fire and began his old song once more: "If only I could shudder."

When midnight came there was an uproar and a noise of tumbling about; faint at first, then louder and louder. Then it was quiet for a while, and at last, with a loud scream, half a man came down the chimney and fell in front of him.

"Hey!" he called. "There's another half belongs to this. This isn't enough."

Then the uproar began again, with roaring and howling, and the other half fell down too.

"Wait," he said, "I'll blow the fire up a bit for you first."

And when he had done that and looked round again, the two pieces had joined together and a frightful man was sitting in his place.

"That wasn't the bargain," said the boy. "That bench is mine."

The man tried to push him away, but the boy would not have it, shoved him off with all his strength and sat down in his own place again.

Then more men came falling down, one after another; they fetched nine dead men's leg-bones and two skulls, set them up and played skittles with them. The boy wanted to play too, and said, "Here — can I join in?"

"Yes, if you have any money."

"Money enough," he said, "but your balls aren't quite round."

So he took the skulls, put them in the lathe and turned them till they were round. "There — now they'll roll better," he said. "Hurrah! Now it's going nicely."

He played with them and lost some of his money; but when the clock struck twelve, everything vanished before his eyes. He lay down and quietly went to sleep.

Next morning the King came to ask after him. "How did you get on this time?"

"I've been playing skittles," he answered, "and I lost a couple of farthings."

"And you didn't shudder?"

"What, me?" he said. "I had a very good time. If only I knew what it was to shudder!"

The third night he sat down on his bench again and said, quite miserably, "If only I could shudder."

When it grew late, six tall men came in carrying a coffin. So he said, "Aha — that will be my little cousin, who died only a few days ago," and he beckoned with his finger and called, "Come along, little cousin, come along."

They set the coffin down on the ground and he went over and took the lid off; and there lay a dead man inside. He felt his face, and it was as cold as ice.

"Wait," he said, "I'll warm you a little." And he went to the fire, warmed his own hand and laid it on the dead man's face; but he stayed cold. So he lifted him out, sat down by the fire with him, laid him against his chest and rubbed his arms to get the blood moving again. When that did no good either, he thought, "When two people lie in bed together they keep each other warm"; and he carried him to the bed, covered him up and lay down beside him.

After a while the dead man grew warm too, and began to move.

Then the boy said, "There, little cousin — didn't I warm you?"

But the dead man got up and cried, "Now I am going to strangle you."

"What!" said he. "Is that how you thank me? Back into your coffin with you." And he picked him up, threw him in and shut the lid. Then the six men came and carried him away again.

"I can't manage to shudder," he said. "I shall never learn it here as long as I live."

Then a man came in who was taller than all the others and looked terrible. But he was old, and had a long white beard.

"You wretch," he cried, "you'll soon learn what it is to shudder, because you are going to die."

"Not so fast," said the boy. "If I'm to die, I'll have some say in it."

"I'll soon have hold of you," said the fiend.

"Gently, gently, don't talk so big. I'm as strong as you are — stronger, perhaps."

"We'll see about that," said the old man. "If you're stronger, I'll let you go. Come, we'll try."

Then he led him through dark passages to a smith's forge, took an axe and with one blow drove an anvil into the ground.

"I can do better than that," said the boy, and went to the other anvil. The old man stood close by to watch, with his white beard hanging down. Then the boy took hold of the axe, split the anvil at one blow, and wedged the old man's beard into the split.

"Now I have you," said the boy. "Now it's your turn to die."

And he took an iron bar and beat the old man until he groaned and begged him to stop, and promised him great riches. The boy pulled out the axe and let him go.

The old man led him back into the castle and showed him three chests full of gold in a cellar. "One share of this is for the poor," he said, "one for the King, and the third is yours."

Just then it struck twelve and the spirit vanished, leaving the boy in the dark. "I'll find my way out all the same," he said; and he felt about, found his way back to the room, and slept there by his fire.

Next morning the King came and said, "Now you must have learnt what shuddering is."

"No," he said. "What can it be? My dead cousin was here, and a bearded man came and showed me a great deal of money down below, but nobody told me what it is to shudder."

"Well," said the King, "you have set the castle free, and you shall marry my daughter."

"That's all very fine," he said, "but I still don't know what it is to shudder."

Then the gold was brought up and the wedding was celebrated; but however much the young king loved his wife, and however happy he was, he still kept saying, "If only I could shudder — if only I could shudder."

And at last she grew annoyed with it. Her waiting-maid said, "I'll find a cure for him. He shall soon learn what it is to shudder."

She went out to the stream that ran through the garden and had a whole bucketful of little fish brought to her. At night, when the young king was asleep, his wife was to pull the covers off him and empty the bucket of cold water and fish all over him, so that the little fish would go flapping about him.

And when it was done he woke up and cried, "Oh, what is making me shudder so? What is making me shudder so, dear wife? Ah — now I know what it is to shudder!"
