The Wishing-Table, the Gold-Ass, and the Cudgel in the Sack

Once upon a time there was a tailor who had three sons and only one goat. But as the goat kept the whole of them with her milk, she had to have good food and be taken out to pasture every day. So the sons did this, each in his turn.

Once the eldest took her to the churchyard, where the finest herbs were to be found, and let her eat and run about there. At night, when it was time to go home, he asked, "Goat, have you had enough?"

The goat answered:

	"I have eaten so much,
	not a leaf more I'll touch — meh! meh!"

"Come home, then," said the young man, and took hold of the cord round her neck, led her into the stable and tied her up securely.

"Well," said the old tailor, "has the goat had as much food as she ought?"

"Oh," said his son, "she has eaten so much, not a leaf more she'll touch."

But the father wanted to satisfy himself, and went down to the stable, stroked the dear animal and asked, "Goat, are you satisfied?"

The goat answered:

	"How should I be satisfied?
	Among the graves I leapt about,
	and found no leaf, so went without — meh! meh!"

"What's this I hear?" cried the tailor; and he ran upstairs and said to the young man, "You liar! You said the goat had had enough, and you let her go hungry!" And in his anger he took the yardstick down from the wall and drove him out with blows.

Next day it was the second son's turn. He looked out a place by the garden fence where nothing but good herbs grew, and the goat cleared them all off. At night, when he wanted to go home, he asked, "Goat, are you satisfied?"

The goat answered:

	"I have eaten so much,
	not a leaf more I'll touch — meh! meh!"

"Come home, then," said the young man, and led her home and tied her up in the stable.

"Well," said the old tailor, "has the goat had as much food as she ought?"

"Oh," said his son, "she has eaten so much, not a leaf more she'll touch."

The tailor would not rely on that, but went down to the stable and said, "Goat, have you had enough?"

The goat answered:

	"How should I be satisfied?
	Among the graves I leapt about,
	and found no leaf, so went without — meh! meh!"

"The godless wretch!" cried the tailor. "To let such a good creature go hungry!" And he ran up and drove the young man out of doors with the yardstick.

Now it was the third son's turn. He wanted to do the thing properly, and sought out some bushes with the finest leaves and let the goat eat them all. In the evening, when he wanted to go home, he asked, "Goat, have you had enough?"

The goat answered:

	"I have eaten so much,
	not a leaf more I'll touch — meh! meh!"

"Come home, then," said the young man, and led her into the stable and tied her up.

"Well," said the old tailor, "has the goat had a proper amount of food?"

"She has eaten so much, not a leaf more she'll touch."

The tailor did not trust that either, but went down and asked, "Goat, have you had enough?"

The wicked beast answered:

	"How should I be satisfied?
	Among the graves I leapt about,
	and found no leaf, so went without — meh! meh!"

"Oh, the brood of liars!" cried the tailor. "Each as wicked and as careless of his duty as the last! You shall make a fool of me no longer." And, quite beside himself with rage, he ran upstairs and belaboured the poor young fellow so hard with the yardstick that he leapt out of the house.

Now the old tailor was alone with his goat. Next morning he went down into the stable, made much of the goat and said, "Come, my dear little creature, I'll take you out to feed myself."

He took her by the rope and led her to green hedges, and among yarrow and whatever else goats like to eat. "There, for once you can eat to your heart's content," he said to her, and let her browse till evening. Then he asked, "Goat, are you satisfied?"

She answered:

	"I have eaten so much,
	not a leaf more I'll touch — meh! meh!"

"Come home, then," said the tailor, and led her into the stable and tied her fast. As he was going away he turned round again and said, "Well, are you satisfied for once?"

But the goat behaved no better to him, and cried:

	"How should I be satisfied?
	Among the graves I leapt about,
	and found no leaf, so went without — meh! meh!"

When the tailor heard that he was appalled, and saw clearly that he had driven his three sons away for nothing.

"Wait, you ungrateful creature," he cried. "It isn't enough to drive you out — I'll mark you so that you'll never dare show your face among honest tailors again."

In great haste he ran upstairs, fetched his razor, lathered the goat's head and shaved her as bare as the palm of his hand. And as the yardstick would have been too good for her, he fetched the horsewhip and gave her such cuts with it that she ran off in a great hurry.

Left all alone in his house like this, the tailor fell into great grief and would gladly have had his sons back again; but nobody knew where they had gone.

The eldest had apprenticed himself to a joiner, and learned hard and tirelessly; and when the time came for him to go travelling, his master gave him a little table which was nothing much to look at and was made of common wood, but which had one good quality. If anybody set it down and said, "Little table, spread yourself," the good little table was at once covered with a clean little cloth, and there was a plate on it and a knife and fork beside it, and dishes of boiled and roast meat, as many as there was room for, and a great glass of red wine shining so that it made the heart glad.

The young journeyman thought, "With this you have enough for your whole life" — and went about the world in high spirits, and never troubled himself whether an inn was good or bad or whether there was anything to be had in it. When it suited him he did not go into an inn at all, but out on the plain, or in a wood, or in a meadow, or wherever he fancied, he took the little table off his back, set it down in front of him and said, "Spread yourself" — and everything his heart desired appeared.

At length he took it into his head to go back to his father, whose anger would have cooled by now and who would gladly take him in with his wishing-table.

It happened that on the way home he came one evening to an inn that was full of guests. They made him welcome and invited him to sit and eat with them, since otherwise he would have trouble getting anything.

"No," said the joiner, "I won't take the few bites out of your mouths. You shall be my guests instead."

They laughed and thought he was joking with them; but he set his wooden table down in the middle of the room and said, "Little table, spread yourself."

Instantly it was covered with food, so good that the landlord could never have got hold of it, and the smell of it went up pleasantly into the guests' nostrils.

"Fall to, dear friends," said the joiner. And when the guests saw that he meant it, they did not need asking twice, but drew up, pulled out their knives and set to work valiantly. And what surprised them most was that when a dish became empty, a full one at once took its place of its own accord.

The innkeeper stood in a corner and watched it all. He had no idea what to say, but thought, "You could find a use for a cook like that in your kitchen."

The joiner and his companions made merry until late into the night; at length they lay down to sleep, and the young journeyman went to bed too, and stood his magic table against the wall.

But the landlord's thoughts gave him no rest. It occurred to him that there was a little old table in his lumber room that looked just like the journeyman's, and he brought it out very softly and exchanged it for the wishing-table.

Next morning the joiner paid for his bed, took up his table without a thought that he had got a false one, and went on his way. At midday he reached his father, who received him with great joy.

"Well, my dear son, what have you learned?" he said.

"Father, I have become a joiner."

"A good trade," said the old man. "But what have you brought back from your apprenticeship?"

"Father, the best thing I've brought back is this little table."

The tailor looked it over on all sides and said, "You didn't make a masterpiece when you made that. It's a bad old table."

"But it's a table that spreads itself," said his son. "When I set it down and tell it to spread itself, the most beautiful dishes appear on it, and wine too, that gladdens the heart. Just invite all our relations and friends — they shall refresh themselves and enjoy themselves for once, because the table will give them everything they want."

When the company was assembled, he put his table in the middle of the room and said, "Little table, spread yourself." But the little table did not stir, and stayed as bare as any other table that does not understand language.

Then the poor journeyman saw that his table had been changed, and was ashamed at having to stand there like a liar. The relations laughed at him and had to go home without having eaten or drunk anything. The father brought out his patches again and went back to his tailoring, and the son went to work for a master in the trade.

The second son had gone to a miller and apprenticed himself to him. When his years were up, the master said, "As you have behaved so well, I'll give you a donkey of a peculiar kind. He won't pull a cart and he won't carry a sack."

"What use is he, then?" asked the young journeyman.

"He drops gold out of his mouth," said the miller. "If you stand him on a cloth and say 'Bricklebrit,' the good animal will drop gold pieces for you."

"That's a fine thing," said the journeyman; and he thanked his master and went out into the world. Whenever he needed gold he had only to say "Bricklebrit" to his donkey, and it rained gold pieces, and he had nothing to do but pick them up off the ground. Wherever he went, only the best was good enough for him, and the dearer the better, because his purse was always full.

When he had seen something of the world he thought, "You must go and find your father. If you turn up with the gold-donkey he'll forget his anger and take you in gladly."

It happened that he came to the very same inn where his brother's table had been exchanged. He was leading his donkey by the bridle, and the landlord was about to take the animal from him and tie it up; but the young journeyman said, "Don't trouble yourself, I'll take my grey horse into the stable and tie him up myself, because I have to know where he is."

That struck the landlord as odd, and he thought a man who had to look after his own donkey could not have much to spend. But when the stranger put his hand in his pocket, brought out two gold pieces and told him to get him something good, the landlord's eyes opened wide, and he ran and hunted out the best he could find.

After dinner the guest asked what he owed. The landlord saw no reason not to double the reckoning, and said the journeyman must give him two more gold pieces. He felt in his pocket, but his gold had just run out.

"Wait a moment, landlord," he said, "I'll go and fetch some money" — and he took the tablecloth with him.

The landlord could not imagine what that could mean; and being curious, he stole after him; and as the guest bolted the stable door, he peeped through a knot hole in the wood. The stranger spread the cloth out under the animal and cried, "Bricklebrit" — and immediately the beast began to let gold pieces fall, so that it fairly rained money on the ground.

"Well, upon my word," said the landlord, "ducats are quickly minted there. A purse like that is not to be sneezed at."

The guest paid his bill and went to bed; but in the night the landlord crept down into the stable, led away the master of the mint and tied up another donkey in his place.

Early next morning the journeyman travelled off with his donkey, thinking he had his gold-donkey. At midday he reached his father, who was delighted to see him again and took him in gladly.

"What have you made of yourself, my son?" asked the old man.

"A miller, dear father."

"What have you brought back from your travels?"

"Nothing but a donkey."

"There are donkeys enough here," said his father. "I'd rather have had a good goat."

"Yes," said his son, "but this is no ordinary donkey — it's a gold-donkey. When I say 'Bricklebrit,' the good beast opens its mouth and drops out a whole sheetful of gold pieces. Just call all our relations here, and I'll make them rich."

"That suits me," said the tailor, "because then I shan't have to torment myself with the needle any longer" — and he ran out himself and called the relations together.

As soon as they were assembled, the miller told them to make room, spread out his cloth and led the donkey into the room. "Now watch," he said, and cried, "Bricklebrit."

But no gold pieces fell, and it was plain that the animal knew nothing of the art — for not every donkey reaches such perfection.

Then the poor miller pulled a long face, saw that he had been cheated, and begged his relations' pardon; and they went home as poor as they had come. There was no help for it: the old man had to take to his needle once more, and the young man hired himself out to a miller.

The third brother had apprenticed himself to a turner; and as that is skilled work, he was the longest in learning it. His brothers, though, wrote and told him how badly things had gone with them, and how the innkeeper had cheated them of their beautiful magic gifts on the last evening before they got home.

When the turner had served his time and had to set out on his travels, his master, because he had behaved so well, gave him a sack and said, "There's a cudgel inside it."

"I can carry the sack," he said, "and it may be useful to me. But why should the cudgel be in it? It only makes it heavy."

"I'll tell you why," said his master. "If anybody does you an injury, you have only to say, 'Cudgel, out of the sack!' and the cudgel will leap out among them and dance such a dance on their backs that they won't be able to stir or move for a week; and it won't leave off until you say, 'Cudgel, into the sack!'"

The journeyman thanked him and put the sack on his back; and whenever anybody came too near him and meant to attack him, he said, "Cudgel, out of the sack!" — and instantly the cudgel sprang out and dusted one coat or jacket after another on their backs, and never stopped until it had beaten it off them; and it was done so quickly that before anybody knew it, it was already his turn.

In the evening the young turner reached the inn where his brothers had been cheated. He laid his sack on the table in front of him and began to talk about all the wonderful things he had seen in the world.

"Yes," he said, "people can easily enough come across a table that spreads itself, or a gold-donkey, or things of that kind — excellent things, and I don't despise them in the least. But they're nothing compared with the treasure I've won for myself and carry about with me in that sack there."

The innkeeper pricked up his ears. "What in the world can that be?" he thought. "The sack must be filled with nothing but jewels. I ought to get hold of those cheap too — all good things come in threes."

When it was time for sleep, the guest stretched himself out on the bench and put his sack under him for a pillow. When the innkeeper thought his guest was sound asleep, he went to him and pushed and pulled very gently and carefully at the sack, to see whether he could possibly draw it out and lay another in its place.

But the turner had been waiting for this a long while; and just as the innkeeper was about to give a hearty tug, he cried, "Cudgel, out of the sack!"

Instantly the little cudgel came out and fell on the innkeeper and gave him a sound thrashing. The landlord cried for mercy; but the louder he cried, the harder the cudgel beat time on his back, until at last he fell to the floor exhausted.

Then the turner said, "If you don't give back the table that spreads itself and the gold-donkey, the dance shall begin again."

"Oh no," cried the landlord, quite humbly, "I'll gladly hand everything over — only make that accursed goblin creep back into the sack."

Then the journeyman said, "I'll let mercy take the place of justice — but beware of getting into mischief again." And he cried, "Cudgel, into the sack!" and let him have some peace.

Next morning the turner went home to his father with the wishing-table and the gold-donkey. The tailor rejoiced when he saw him once more, and asked him too what he had learned in foreign parts.

"Dear father," he said, "I have become a turner."

"A skilled trade," said his father. "What have you brought back from your travels?"

"A precious thing, dear father," said his son. "A cudgel in a sack."

"What!" cried his father. "A cudgel! That was worth the trouble, I must say. You can cut yourself one off any tree."

"But not one like this, dear father. If I say, 'Cudgel, out of the sack!' the cudgel springs out and leads anybody who means me harm a weary dance, and never stops until he is lying on the ground praying for fair weather. Look — with this cudgel I've got back the wishing-table and the gold-donkey that the thieving innkeeper took from my brothers. Now send for them both, and invite all our kinsfolk. I'll give them food and drink and fill their pockets with gold into the bargain."

The old tailor did not quite believe it, but all the same he got the relations together. Then the turner spread a cloth in the room and led in the gold-donkey, and said to his brother, "Now, dear brother, speak to him."

The miller said, "Bricklebrit" — and instantly the gold pieces came down on the cloth like a thunder shower, and the donkey did not stop until every one of them had as much as he could carry. (I can see by your face that you would have liked to be there too.)

Then the turner brought out the little table and said, "Now, dear brother, speak to it."

And hardly had the joiner said, "Little table, spread yourself," before it was laid and amply covered with the most exquisite dishes.

Then there was such a meal as the good tailor had never known in his house; and the whole party of relations stayed together far into the night, and were all merry and glad. The tailor locked needle and thread and yardstick and pressing iron away in a cupboard, and lived with his three sons in joy and splendour.

(And what became of the goat, whose fault it was that the tailor drove out his three sons? I'll tell you. She was ashamed of having a bald head, and ran to a fox's hole and crept into it. When the fox came home, two great eyes shone at him out of the darkness, and he was terrified and ran away.

A bear met him; and as the fox looked thoroughly upset, he said, "What's the matter with you, brother fox? Why are you looking like that?"

"Ah," said Redskin, "there's a fierce beast in my den, and it glared at me with its fiery eyes."

"We'll soon drive him out," said the bear, and went with him to the den and looked in. But when he saw the fiery eyes, fear took hold of him too; he would have nothing to do with the raging beast, and took to his heels.

A bee met him; and as she saw he was ill at ease, she said, "Bear, you're really pulling a very sorry face. What's become of all your good cheer?"

"It's all very well for you to talk," said the bear. "There's a raging beast with staring eyes in Redskin's house, and we can't drive it out."

The bee said, "Bear, I'm sorry for you. I'm a poor weak creature that you wouldn't turn your head to look at; but all the same, I think I can help you."

She flew into the fox's den, settled on the goat's smoothly shaven head and stung her so violently that she sprang up crying "Meh, meh!" and ran off into the world like a mad thing; and to this hour nobody knows where she has got to.)
