Old Hildebrand

Once upon a time there lived a peasant and his wife; and the parson of the village had taken a fancy to the wife, and had wished for a long time to spend a whole day pleasantly with her. The peasant woman was quite willing too.

So one day he said to her, "Listen, my dear friend, I've thought of a way for the two of us to spend a whole day happily together. I'll tell you what. On Wednesday you must take to your bed and tell your husband you're ill; and if you'll only complain and act the invalid properly, and go on doing it until Sunday, when I have to preach, then I'll say in my sermon that whoever has at home a sick child, a sick husband, a sick wife, a sick father, a sick mother, a sick brother — or whoever else it may be — and makes a pilgrimage to the Göckerli hill in Italy, where you can get a peck of laurel leaves for a kreuzer, the sick child, the sick husband, the sick wife, the sick father, the sick mother, the sick sister, or whoever else it may be, will be restored to health immediately."

"I'll manage it," said the woman promptly.

So on the Wednesday the peasant woman took to her bed and complained and lamented as they had agreed; and her husband did everything for her that he could think of, but nothing did her any good.

And when Sunday came the woman said, "I feel as ill as if I were going to die this minute; but there's one thing I should like to do before my end — I should like to hear the sermon the parson is going to preach today."

At that the peasant said, "Ah, my child, don't do it. You might make yourself worse if you got up. Look, I'll go to the sermon, and I'll pay very close attention and tell you everything the parson says."

"Well," said the woman, "go then, and pay great attention, and repeat to me everything you hear."

So the peasant went to the sermon; and the parson began to preach, and said that if anyone had at home a sick child, a sick husband, a sick wife, a sick father, a sick mother, a sick sister or brother or anyone else, and would make a pilgrimage to the Göckerli hill in Italy, where a peck of laurel leaves costs a kreuzer, the sick child, sick husband, sick wife, sick father, sick mother, sick sister or brother, or whoever else it might be, would be restored to health instantly; and that whoever wished to undertake the journey was to come to him after the service was over, and he would give him the sack for the laurel leaves and the kreuzer.

Nobody was more delighted than the peasant; and after the service was over he went straight to the parson, who gave him the bag for the laurel leaves and the kreuzer.

After that he went home, and even at the front door he shouted, "Hurrah, dear wife! It's now almost the same as if you were well! The parson preached today that whoever had at home a sick child, a sick husband, a sick wife, a sick father, a sick mother, a sick sister or brother or whoever it might be, and would make a pilgrimage to the Göckerli hill in Italy, where a peck of laurel leaves costs a kreuzer, the sick child, sick husband, sick wife, sick father, sick mother, sick sister or brother, or whoever else it was, would be cured immediately. And now I've already got the bag and the kreuzer from the parson, and I'll set off at once, so that you get well the sooner." And with that he went off.

But he was hardly gone before the woman got up, and the parson was there directly.

But we'll leave the two of them for a while and follow the peasant, who walked on quickly without stopping so as to get to the Göckerli hill the sooner. And on the way he met his old friend, who was an egg merchant and was just coming back from market, where he had sold his eggs.

"Bless you," said his friend. "Where are you off to in such a hurry?"

"To all eternity, my friend," said the peasant. "My wife is ill, and today I went to hear the parson's sermon, and he preached that if anybody had in his house a sick child, a sick husband, a sick wife, a sick father, a sick mother, a sick sister or brother or anyone else, and made a pilgrimage to the Göckerli hill in Italy, where a peck of laurel leaves costs a kreuzer, the sick child, the sick husband, the sick wife, the sick father, the sick mother, the sick sister or brother, or whoever else it was, would be cured immediately. And so I've got the bag for the laurel leaves and the kreuzer from the parson, and now I'm starting on my pilgrimage."

"But listen, my friend," said the egg merchant to the peasant, "are you really stupid enough to believe a thing like that? Don't you know what it means? The parson wants to spend a whole day alone with your wife in peace, so he's given you this job to get you out of the way."

"My word," said the peasant, "how I'd like to know whether that's true!"

"Come along, then," said his friend. "I'll tell you what to do. Get into my egg basket and I'll carry you home, and then you can see for yourself."

So that was settled, and his friend put the peasant into his egg basket and carried him home.

When they got to the house — hurrah, but things were going merrily there! The woman had already had nearly everything in the farmyard killed, and had made pancakes; and the parson was there, and had brought his fiddle with him.

His friend knocked at the door, and the woman asked who was there.

"It's me, my dear," said the egg merchant. "Give me shelter for the night. I didn't sell my eggs at the market, so now I have to carry them home again, and they're so heavy that I'll never manage it — it's dark already."

"Well, my friend," said the woman, "you come at a very inconvenient time for me; but as you're here it can't be helped. Come in and take a seat there on the bench by the stove."

So she put the man, and the basket he carried on his back, on the bench by the stove. The parson and the woman, meanwhile, were as merry as could be.

At length the parson said, "Listen, my dear friend, you sing beautifully. Sing something for me."

"Oh," said the woman, "I can't sing now. In my young days I could sing well enough, but that's all over now."

"Come," said the parson again, "do sing some little song."

At that the woman began, and sang:

	"I've sent my husband away from me
	to the Göckerli hill in Italy."

Then the parson sang:

	"I wish it were a year before he came back —
	I'd never ask him for the laurel-leaf sack.
	Hallelujah."

Then the friend in the background began to sing (but I ought to tell you that the peasant was called Hildebrand), so he sang:

	"What are you doing, my Hildebrand dear,
	there on the bench by the stove so near?
	Hallelujah."

And then the peasant sang from inside his basket:

	"All singing I ever shall hate from this day,
	and here in this basket no longer I'll stay.
	Hallelujah."

And he got out of the basket and cudgelled the parson out of the house.

The Three Little Birds

About a thousand years ago or more, there were nothing but small kings in this country; and one of them, who lived on the Keuterberg, was very fond of hunting.

Once, when he was riding out from his castle with his huntsmen, three girls were watching their cows on the mountain; and when they saw the King with all his followers, the eldest girl pointed at him and called to the other two, "If I don't get that one, I'll have none."

Then the second girl answered from the other side of the hill, and pointed at the man on the King's right hand, "Hey there! Hey there! If I don't get him, I'll have nobody." And the third pointed at the one on his left. Now these two were the King's ministers.

The King heard all this; and when he came back from the hunt he had the three girls brought to him and asked them what they had said the day before on the mountain. They would not tell him; so the King asked the eldest whether she would really take him for her husband. Then she said yes, and the two ministers married the two sisters — for all three were fair and beautiful of face, and especially the Queen, who had hair like flax.

But the two sisters had no children; and once when the King had to go away from home, he invited them to come and stay with the Queen to cheer her, for she was about to bear a child. She had a little boy, who brought a bright red star into the world with him.

Then the two sisters said to each other that they would throw the beautiful boy into the water. And when they had thrown him in — I believe it was into the Weser — a little bird flew up into the air, singing:

	"To your death you are sped
	till God's own word be said.
	In the white lily's bloom,
	brave boy, is your tomb."

When the two heard that they were frightened to death, and ran away in great haste. When the King came home they told him that the Queen had given birth to a dog. Then the King said, "What God does is well done."

But a fisherman who lived by the water fished the little boy out again while he was still alive; and as his wife had no children, they brought him up.

When a year had gone by, the King went away again, and the Queen had another little boy, whom the false sisters likewise took and threw into the water. Then a little bird flew up again and sang:

	"To your death you are sped
	till God's own word be said.
	In the white lily's bloom,
	brave boy, is your tomb."

And when the King came back they told him that the Queen had given birth to a dog again; and again he said, "What God does is well done." But the fisherman fished this one out of the water too, and brought him up.

Then the King went off on his travels again, and the Queen had a little girl, whom the false sisters also threw into the water. Then again a little bird flew up on high and sang:

	"To your death you are sped
	till God's own word be said.
	In the white lily's bloom,
	bonny girl, is your tomb."

And when the King came home they told him that the Queen had given birth to a cat. Then the King grew angry and ordered his wife to be thrown into prison, and she was shut up there for many long years.

Meanwhile the children had grown up. The eldest went out one day with some other boys to fish; but the other boys would not have him with them, and said, "Go your own way, foundling."

He was much troubled at this, and asked the old fisherman whether it was true. The fisherman told him that once, when he was fishing, he had drawn him out of the water.

So the boy said he would go out and look for his father. The fisherman begged him to stay; but he would not be held back, and at last the fisherman consented.

Then the boy went on his way and walked for many days, and at last he came to a great stretch of water, beside which an old woman was standing, fishing.

"Good day, mother," said the boy.

"Many thanks," said she.

"You'll fish a long while before you catch anything."

"And you'll search a long while before you find your father. How will you get over the water?" said the woman.

"God knows."

Then the old woman took him up on her back and carried him across; and he searched a long time but could not find his father.

When a year had gone by, the second boy set out to look for his brother. He came to the water, and it went with him exactly as it had with his brother.

And now there was nobody left at home but the daughter, and she mourned for her brothers so much that at last she too begged the fisherman to let her go, for she wanted to search for them. So she came to the great stretch of water too, and said to the old woman, "Good day, mother."

"Many thanks," replied the old woman.

"May God help you with your fishing," said the girl.

When the old woman heard that she became quite friendly, and carried her over the water, and gave her a wand, and said to her, "Go on and on along this road, my daughter; and when you come to a great black dog, you must pass it silently and boldly, without laughing or looking at it. Then you'll come to a great high castle, and you must let the wand fall on the threshold and go straight through the castle and out again on the other side. There you'll see an old fountain with a great tree growing out of it, and a bird hanging on the tree in a cage, which you must take down. Take a glass of water out of the fountain as well, and go back the same way with those two things. Pick the wand up again from the threshold and take it with you; and when you pass the dog again, strike him in the face with it — but be sure you hit him — and then come back here to me."

The girl found everything exactly as the old woman had said; and on the way back she found her two brothers, who had been searching for each other over half the world. They went together to the place where the black dog was lying in the road; she struck it in the face, and it turned into a handsome prince, who went with them to the river.

There the old woman was still standing. She rejoiced greatly to see them again, and carried them all over the water; and then she went away too, for now she was free.

The others went to the old fisherman, and everyone was glad that they had found each other again; and they hung the bird up on the wall.

But the second son could not settle down at home, and took his crossbow and went out hunting. When he was tired he took out his flute and made music. The King was out hunting too, and heard it and went over; and when he met the young man he said, "Who gave you leave to hunt here?"

"Oh, nobody."

"Whose son are you, then?"

"I'm the fisherman's son."

"But he has no children."

"If you won't believe me, come with me."

The King did; and he questioned the fisherman, who told him the whole story; and the little bird on the wall began to sing:

	"The mother sits alone
	there in the prison small.
	O King of royal blood,
	these are your children all.
	The sisters twain so false,
	they wrought the children woe,
	there in the waters deep
	where the fishermen come and go."

Then they were all horror-struck; and the King took the bird, the fisherman and the three children back with him to the castle, and ordered the prison to be opened and brought his wife out again. But she had grown quite ill and weak. Then the daughter gave her some of the water from the fountain to drink, and she became strong and healthy.

But the two false sisters were burnt; and the daughter married the prince.
