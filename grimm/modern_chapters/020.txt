Thumbling as Journeyman

A certain tailor had a son who happened to be small — no bigger than a thumb — and so he was always called Thumbling. He had some courage in him, though, and said to his father, "Father, I must and will go out into the world."

"That's right, my son," said the old man; and he took a long darning needle and made a knob of sealing wax on it at the candle. "And there's a sword for you to take with you on the way."

Then the little tailor wanted to have one more meal at home, and hopped into the kitchen to see what his lady mother had cooked for the last time. It had just been dished up, and the dish was standing on the hearth.

"Mother," he said, "what is there to eat today?"

"See for yourself," said his mother.

So Thumbling jumped onto the hearth and peeped into the dish; but he stretched his neck in too far, and the steam from the food caught hold of him and carried him up the chimney. He rode about in the air on the steam for a while, until at length he sank down to the ground again.

So now the little tailor was out in the wide world; and he travelled about and took work with a master in his trade, but the food was not good enough for him.

"Mistress," said Thumbling, "if you don't give us better food, I'll go away, and early tomorrow morning I'll write on your front door in chalk: Too many potatoes, too little meat. Farewell, Mr. Potato-King."

"What would you have, then, grasshopper?" said the mistress, and grew angry and snatched up a dishcloth and was going to hit him. But my little tailor crept nimbly under a thimble, peeped out from under it and put his tongue out at the mistress. She picked up the thimble and tried to get hold of him, but little Thumbling hopped into the dishcloth; and while the mistress was shaking it out and looking for him, he got into a crack in the table.

"Ho, ho, my lady mistress," he cried, sticking his head out; and when she started hitting at him he leapt down into the drawer. At last, though, she caught him and drove him out of the house.

The little tailor journeyed on and came to a great forest, and there he fell in with a band of robbers who had a plan to steal the King's treasure. When they saw the little tailor they thought, "A little fellow like that can creep through a keyhole and be a picklock for us."

"Hey there," one of them cried, "you giant Goliath, will you come to the treasure chamber with us? You can slip yourself inside and throw the money out."

Thumbling thought about it for a while, and at length he said yes, and went with them to the treasure chamber. There he looked at the doors, top and bottom, to see whether there was any crack in them; and it was not long before he spied one that was wide enough to let him in.

He was about to get in at once, but one of the two sentries standing in front of the door noticed him and said to the other, "What an ugly spider that is, crawling there. I'll kill it."

"Leave the poor creature alone," said the other. "It's done you no harm."

So Thumbling got safely through the crack into the treasure chamber, opened the window under which the robbers were standing, and threw out one thaler after another to them.

When the little tailor was in the full swing of the work, he heard the King coming to inspect his treasure chamber, and crept hastily into a hiding place. The King noticed that a number of solid thalers were missing, but could not imagine who could have stolen them, since the locks and bolts were in good order and everything seemed well guarded. So he went away again and said to the sentries, "Keep watch — somebody is after the money."

So when Thumbling started work again, they heard the money moving and a sound of clink, clink, clink. They ran in quickly to catch the thief; but the little tailor, who heard them coming, was quicker still, and leapt into a corner and covered himself with a thaler so that nothing could be seen of him — and at the same time he mocked the sentries and called, "Here I am!"

The sentries ran over; but by the time they got there he had already hopped into another corner under a thaler, and was calling, "Ho, ho, here I am!" The watchmen sprang over there in a hurry, but Thumbling had long since got into a third corner and was calling, "Ho, ho, here I am!"

And so he made fools of them and chased them round and round the treasure chamber until they were worn out and went away. Then he threw all the thalers out, one by one, sending the last one out with all his strength, and then hopped nimbly onto it and flew down with it through the window.

The robbers paid him great compliments. "You're a valiant hero," they said. "Will you be our captain?"

Thumbling declined, though, and said he wanted to see the world first. They divided up the booty, but the little tailor asked only for a farthing, because he could not carry more.

Then he buckled on his sword again, said goodbye to the robbers and took to the road. First he went to work for some masters, but he had no liking for that; and at last he hired himself out as a manservant at an inn.

The maids could not endure him, though, because he saw everything they did in secret without their seeing him, and told the master and mistress what they had taken off the plates and carried out of the cellar for themselves.

"Wait," they said, "and we'll pay you out" — and arranged between them to play him a trick.

Soon afterwards, when one of the maids was mowing in the garden and saw Thumbling jumping about and creeping up and down the plants, she mowed him up quickly along with the grass, tied it all in a great cloth and secretly threw it to the cows. Now among them there was a great black one, who swallowed him down without hurting him.

Down there, though, he liked it very ill, because it was quite dark and there was no candle burning. When the cow was being milked he called out:

	"Strip, strap, strull,
	will the pail soon be full?"

But the noise of the milking stopped anybody understanding him.

After this the master of the house came into the cowshed and said, "That cow shall be killed tomorrow."

Then Thumbling was so alarmed that he cried out in a clear voice, "Let me out first — I'm shut up inside her!"

The master heard that quite well, but did not know where the voice was coming from. "Where are you?" he asked.

"In the black one," answered Thumbling; but the master did not understand what that meant, and went out.

Next morning the cow was killed. Luckily Thumbling did not take a single blow in the cutting up and chopping; he got in among the sausage meat. And when the butcher came in and started his work, he cried out with all his might, "Don't chop too deep, don't chop too deep, I'm in among it!"

Nobody heard it, because of the noise of the chopping knife. Now poor Thumbling was in trouble; but trouble sharpens the wits, and he sprang about so nimbly between the blows that none of them touched him and he escaped with a whole skin.

But still he could not get away; there was nothing for it but to let himself be stuffed into a black pudding along with the bits of bacon. His quarters there were rather cramped; and besides that he was hung up in the chimney to be smoked, and there the time hung terribly heavy on his hands.

At length, in the winter, he was taken down again, because the black pudding was to be set before a guest. When the landlady was cutting it into slices he took care not to stretch his head out too far, in case a bit of it should be cut off; and at last he saw his chance, cleared a passage for himself and jumped out.

The little tailor would not stay any longer, though, in a house where he had fared so badly, and set out on his travels again at once. But his liberty did not last long. Out in the open country he met a fox, who snapped him up without thinking.

"Hey there, Mr. Fox," cried the little tailor, "it's me sticking in your throat. Set me free again."

"You're right," said the fox. "You're next to nothing to me. But if you'll promise me the fowls in your father's yard, I'll let you go."

"With all my heart," said Thumbling. "You shall have all the cocks and hens, I promise you."

So the fox let him go, and even carried him home himself. And when his father saw his dear son again, he willingly gave the fox all the fowls he had.

"And here I bring you a handsome bit of money too," said Thumbling, and gave his father the farthing he had earned on his travels.

"But why did the fox get the poor chickens to eat?"

"Oh, you goose — surely your father would love his child far more than the fowls in the yard."


Fitcher’s Bird

There was once a wizard who used to take the form of a poor man and go to houses and beg, and catch pretty girls. Nobody knew where he carried them, because they were never seen again.

One day he appeared at the door of a man who had three pretty daughters. He looked like a poor, weak beggar and carried a basket on his back, as though he meant to collect gifts of charity in it. He begged for a little food; and when the eldest daughter came out and was just handing him a piece of bread, he only touched her — and she was forced to jump into his basket.

Then he hurried away with long strides and carried her off into a dark forest, to his house that stood in the middle of it.

Everything in the house was magnificent. He gave her whatever she could possibly want and said, "My darling, you will certainly be happy with me, because you have everything your heart can wish for."

This lasted a few days, and then he said, "I have to go on a journey and leave you alone for a short time. Here are the keys of the house. You may go everywhere and look at everything, except into one room, which this little key opens; and that I forbid you on pain of death."

He also gave her an egg, and said, "Take good care of this egg for me and carry it about with you all the time, because great misfortune would come of losing it."

She took the keys and the egg and promised to obey him in everything. When he had gone she went all round the house from bottom to top and examined everything. The rooms shone with silver and gold, and she thought she had never seen such splendour.

At length she came to the forbidden door. She meant to pass it by, but curiosity gave her no peace. She looked at the key; it looked just like any other. She put it in the keyhole and turned it a little, and the door sprang open.

But what did she see when she went in? A great bloody basin stood in the middle of the room, and in it lay human beings, dead and hacked to pieces; and close by stood a block of wood with a gleaming axe lying on it.

She was so terrified that the egg she was holding fell into the basin. She got it out and washed the blood off, but it was no use — it appeared again in a moment. She washed and scrubbed, but she could not get it off.

It was not long before the man came back from his journey, and the first things he asked for were the key and the egg. She gave them to him, but she trembled as she did it; and he saw at once by the red spots that she had been in the bloody chamber.

"Since you went into that room against my will," he said, "you shall go back into it against your own. Your life is over."

He threw her down, dragged her there by the hair, cut her head off on the block and hacked her to pieces so that her blood ran on the floor. Then he threw her into the basin with the rest.

"Now I'll fetch myself the second," said the wizard; and he went to the house again in the shape of a poor man and begged. Then the second daughter brought him a piece of bread; and he caught her like the first, simply by touching her, and carried her off.

She fared no better than her sister. She let her curiosity get the better of her, opened the door of the bloody chamber, looked in, and had to pay for it with her life when the wizard came back.

Then he went and fetched the third sister; but she was clever and crafty. When he had given her the keys and the egg and had gone, she first put the egg away with great care, and then explored the house, and last of all went into the forbidden room.

Oh, what she saw there! Both her sisters lay in the basin, cruelly murdered and cut to pieces. But she began to gather their limbs together and put them in order — head, body, arms and legs. And when nothing more was missing, the limbs began to move and join themselves together, and both the girls opened their eyes and were alive again. Then they rejoiced and kissed and hugged one another.

When the man came home he demanded the keys and the egg at once; and as he could see no trace of blood on it, he said, "You have stood the test. You shall be my bride."

Now he had no power over her any more, and had to do whatever she wished.

"Very well," she said, "but first you shall take a basketful of gold to my father and mother, and carry it yourself on your back. Meanwhile I'll get ready for the wedding."

Then she ran to her sisters, whom she had hidden in a little room, and said, "The moment has come when I can save you. The wretch shall carry you home himself. But as soon as you're at home, send help to me."

She put them both in a basket and covered them right over with gold, so that nothing of them could be seen; and then she called the wizard in and said, "Now carry the basket away. But I shall be looking out of my little window and watching, to see whether you stop on the road to stand still or to rest."

The wizard lifted the basket onto his back and set off with it; but it weighed him down so heavily that the sweat ran off his face. So he sat down and wanted to rest a while; but immediately one of the girls in the basket cried, "I'm looking out of my little window and I can see you resting. Will you get on at once?"

He thought it was his bride calling to him, and got to his feet again. Once more he was going to sit down, and instantly she cried, "I'm looking out of my little window and I can see you resting. Will you get on this minute?"

And whenever he stood still she cried it, and then he had to go on; until at last, groaning and out of breath, he brought the basket with the gold and the two girls into their parents' house.

At home, meanwhile, the bride was preparing the wedding feast and sending out invitations to the wizard's friends. Then she took a skull with grinning teeth, put some ornaments on it and a wreath of flowers, carried it up to the attic window and set it looking out.

When everything was ready, she got into a barrel of honey, and then cut the feather bed open and rolled herself in it until she looked like some wonderful bird and nobody could have recognised her.

Then she went out of the house; and on the way she met some of the wedding guests, who asked:

	"Oh, Fitcher's bird, how come you here?"
	"I come from Fitcher's house, quite near."
	"And what may the young bride be doing?"
	"From cellar to attic she's swept all clean,
	and now from the window she's peeping, I ween."

At last she met the bridegroom, who was coming slowly back. He asked her, like the others:

	"Oh, Fitcher's bird, how come you here?"
	"I come from Fitcher's house, quite near."
	"And what may the young bride be doing?"
	"From cellar to attic she's swept all clean,
	and now from the window she's peeping, I ween."

The bridegroom looked up, saw the decked-out skull, thought it was his bride and nodded to her, greeting her kindly.

But when he and his guests had all gone into the house, the bride's brothers and kinsmen arrived, who had been sent to rescue her. They locked all the doors of the house so that nobody could escape, and set fire to it; and the wizard and all his crew had to burn.
