Cinderella

The wife of a rich man fell ill; and when she felt her end coming near she called her only daughter to her bedside and said, "Dear child, be good and pious, and then the good God will always protect you, and I will look down on you from heaven and be near you."

With that she closed her eyes and died. Every day the girl went out to her mother's grave and wept, and she stayed pious and good. When winter came the snow spread a white sheet over the grave; and when the spring sun had drawn it off again, the man had taken another wife.

The woman had brought two daughters into the house with her, who were beautiful and fair of face but vile and black-hearted. Now a bad time began for the poor stepchild.

"Is the stupid goose to sit in the parlour with us?" they said. "Whoever wants to eat bread must earn it. Out with the kitchen maid."

They took away her pretty clothes, put an old grey smock on her and gave her wooden shoes. "Just look at the proud princess, all dressed up," they cried, and laughed, and led her into the kitchen.

There she had to do heavy work from morning till night: get up before daybreak, carry water, light the fires, cook and wash. And on top of that the sisters did her every injury they could think of — they mocked her, and tipped her peas and lentils into the ashes so that she had to sit and pick them out again. In the evening, when she had worked until she was worn out, she had no bed to go to but had to sleep by the hearth in the ashes. And because that made her always look dusty and dirty, they called her Cinderella.

It happened once that the father was going to the fair, and he asked his two stepdaughters what he should bring back for them.

"Beautiful dresses," said one.

"Pearls and jewels," said the second.

"And you, Cinderella," he said, "what will you have?"

"Father, break off for me the first branch that knocks against your hat on the way home."

So he bought beautiful dresses and pearls and jewels for his two stepdaughters; and on the way home, as he was riding through a green thicket, a hazel twig brushed against him and knocked his hat off. So he broke off the branch and took it with him.

When he got home he gave his stepdaughters what they had asked for, and he gave Cinderella the branch from the hazel bush. Cinderella thanked him, went to her mother's grave and planted the branch on it, and wept so much that her tears fell on it and watered it. And it grew and became a fine tree. Three times a day Cinderella went and sat under it and wept and prayed; and a little white bird always came to the tree, and if Cinderella wished for anything, the bird threw down to her what she had wished for.

Now it happened that the King proclaimed a festival, to last three days, to which all the beautiful young girls in the country were invited, so that his son might choose a bride. When the two stepsisters heard that they were to appear among them, they were delighted, and called Cinderella and said, "Comb our hair for us, brush our shoes and fasten our buckles. We are going to the festival at the King's palace."

Cinderella did as she was told, but she wept, because she too would have liked to go to the dance; and she begged her stepmother to let her.

"You, Cinderella!" she said. "You're dusty and dirty, and you want to go to the festival? You have no clothes and no shoes, and you want to dance?"

But as Cinderella kept on asking, the stepmother said at last, "I have tipped a dish of lentils into the ashes for you. If you have picked them out again in two hours, you shall go with us."

The girl went out through the back door into the garden and called, "You tame pigeons, you turtle doves, and all you birds under the sky, come and help me to pick —

	the good ones into the pot,
	the bad ones into your crop."

Then two white pigeons came in at the kitchen window, and after them the turtle doves; and at last all the birds under the sky came whirring and crowding in and settled among the ashes. And the pigeons nodded their heads and began, pick, pick, pick, pick; and the rest began too, pick, pick, pick, pick, and gathered all the good grains into the dish. Hardly an hour had gone by before they had finished and all flew out again.

Then the girl took the dish to her stepmother, glad at heart, believing that now she would be allowed to go to the festival. But the stepmother said, "No, Cinderella. You have no clothes and you can't dance. You would only be laughed at."

And as Cinderella wept at this, the stepmother said, "If you can pick two dishes of lentils out of the ashes for me in one hour, you shall come with us" — and thought to herself, "She certainly can't do that."

When the stepmother had tipped the two dishes of lentils into the ashes, the girl went out through the back door into the garden and called, "You tame pigeons, you turtle doves, and all you birds under the sky, come and help me to pick —

	the good ones into the pot,
	the bad ones into your crop."

Then two white pigeons came in at the kitchen window, and after them the turtle doves; and at last all the birds under the sky came whirring and crowding in and settled among the ashes. And the doves nodded their heads and began, pick, pick, pick, pick; and the others began too, pick, pick, pick, pick, and gathered all the good seeds into the dishes. And before half an hour was over they had finished and all flew out again.

Then the girl carried the dishes to her stepmother, delighted, believing that now she might go to the festival. But the stepmother said, "None of this will help you. You are not coming with us, because you have no clothes and you can't dance. We should be ashamed of you."

And with that she turned her back on Cinderella and hurried off with her two proud daughters.

Now that nobody was at home, Cinderella went to her mother's grave under the hazel tree and called:

	"Shiver and quiver, little tree,
	throw gold and silver down on me."

Then the bird threw down a dress of gold and silver, and slippers embroidered with silk and silver. She put the dress on as fast as she could and went to the festival. Her stepsisters and her stepmother did not know her, and thought she must be some foreign princess, she looked so beautiful in the golden dress. They never once thought of Cinderella, and supposed she was sitting at home in the dirt picking lentils out of the ashes.

The prince came to meet her, took her by the hand and danced with her. He would dance with no other girl and never let go of her hand; and if anybody else came to ask her, he said, "This is my partner."

She danced until it was evening, and then she wanted to go home. But the King's son said, "I'll come with you and see you home" — because he wanted to see whose daughter the beautiful girl was. She got away from him, though, and sprang into the pigeon house.

The King's son waited until her father came, and then told him that the strange girl had jumped into the pigeon house. The old man thought, "Can it be Cinderella?" — and they had to bring him an axe and a pick so that he could chop the pigeon house to pieces; but there was nobody inside. And when they came home, Cinderella was lying in her dirty clothes among the ashes, with a dim little oil lamp burning on the mantelpiece; for Cinderella had jumped down quickly at the back of the pigeon house and run to the little hazel tree, and there she had taken off her beautiful clothes and laid them on the grave, and the bird had taken them away again, and then she had set herself down in the kitchen among the ashes in her grey gown.

Next day, when the festival began again and her parents and her stepsisters had gone, Cinderella went to the hazel tree and said:

	"Shiver and quiver, little tree,
	throw gold and silver down on me."

Then the bird threw down a dress far more beautiful than the day before. And when Cinderella appeared at the festival in this dress, everybody was astonished at her beauty. The King's son had been waiting until she came, and took her hand at once and danced with nobody but her. When others came and asked her, he said, "She is my partner."

When evening came she wanted to leave, and the King's son followed her, meaning to see which house she went into. But she sprang away from him and into the garden behind the house. In it stood a beautiful tall tree hung with the most magnificent pears. She climbed up among the branches as nimbly as a squirrel, and the King's son did not know where she had gone.

He waited until her father came and said to him, "The strange girl has escaped me, and I think she has climbed the pear tree."

The father thought, "Can it be Cinderella?" — and had an axe brought and cut the tree down; but there was nobody in it. And when they came into the kitchen, Cinderella was lying among the ashes as usual, for she had jumped down on the other side of the tree, taken the beautiful dress back to the bird on the little hazel tree, and put on her grey gown.

On the third day, when her parents and sisters had gone, Cinderella went once more to her mother's grave and said to the little tree:

	"Shiver and quiver, little tree,
	throw gold and silver down on me."

And now the bird threw down a dress more splendid and magnificent than any she had had yet, and the slippers were of gold. And when she came to the festival in that dress, nobody could find words for their astonishment. The King's son danced with her alone; and if anybody asked her to dance, he said, "She is my partner."

When evening came Cinderella wanted to leave, and the King's son was eager to go with her; but she got away from him so quickly that he could not follow. The King's son had used a trick, though: he had had the whole staircase smeared with pitch, and as she ran down, the girl's left slipper stuck fast in it. The King's son picked it up, and it was small and dainty and all of gold.

Next morning he went with it to the father and said, "Nobody shall be my wife but the one whose foot this golden slipper fits."

Then the two sisters were glad, because they had pretty feet. The eldest went into her room with the shoe to try it on, and her mother stood by. But she could not get her big toe in, and the shoe was too small for her. So her mother handed her a knife and said, "Cut the toe off. When you are Queen you won't need to go on foot any more."

The girl cut off her toe, forced her foot into the shoe, swallowed the pain and went out to the King's son. He took her up on his horse as his bride and rode away with her. But they had to pass the grave; and there on the hazel tree sat the two pigeons, crying:

	"Turn and peep, turn and peep,
	there's blood inside the shoe.
	The shoe is far too small for her;
	the true bride waits for you."

Then he looked at her foot and saw the blood streaming from it. He turned his horse round and took the false bride home again and said she was not the right one, and that the other sister must put the shoe on.

So she went into her room and got her toes safely into the shoe, but her heel was too big. Her mother handed her a knife and said, "Cut a piece off your heel. When you are Queen you won't need to go on foot any more."

The girl cut a piece off her heel, forced her foot into the shoe, swallowed the pain and went out to the King's son. He took her up on his horse as his bride and rode away with her; but as they passed the hazel tree the two little pigeons sat on it and cried:

	"Turn and peep, turn and peep,
	there's blood inside the shoe.
	The shoe is far too small for her;
	the true bride waits for you."

He looked down at her foot and saw the blood running out of the shoe and staining her white stocking red. Then he turned his horse and took the false bride home again.

"This isn't the right one either," he said. "Have you no other daughter?"

"No," said the man. "There's only a stunted little kitchen drudge my late wife left behind — but she can't possibly be the bride."

The King's son said he was to send her up to him; but the mother answered, "Oh no, she is much too dirty, she can't let herself be seen."

He absolutely insisted, though, and Cinderella had to be called. First she washed her hands and face clean, and then she came and bowed before the King's son, who handed her the golden shoe. She sat down on a stool, drew her foot out of the heavy wooden shoe and put it into the slipper — and it fitted like a glove.

And when she stood up and the King's son looked at her face, he knew her for the beautiful girl who had danced with him, and cried, "This is the true bride!"

The stepmother and the two sisters were horrified and turned pale with rage; but he took Cinderella up on his horse and rode away with her. As they passed the hazel tree the two white doves cried:

	"Turn and peep, turn and peep,
	no blood is in the shoe.
	The shoe is not too small for her;
	the true bride rides with you."

And when they had said it, the two came flying down and settled on Cinderella's shoulders, one on the right and the other on the left, and stayed sitting there.

When the wedding with the King's son was to be celebrated, the two false sisters came and wanted to get into Cinderella's good graces and share her good fortune. As the betrothed couple went to church, the elder was on the right and the younger on the left, and the pigeons pecked out one eye from each of them. Afterwards, as they came back, the elder was on the left and the younger on the right, and then the pigeons pecked out the other eye from each. And so, for their wickedness and their lying, they were punished with blindness as long as they lived.


The Riddle

There was once a King's son who was seized with a longing to travel about the world, and he took nobody with him but one faithful servant.

One day he came to a great forest; and when darkness came on he could find no shelter and did not know where to spend the night. Then he saw a girl going towards a little house; and when he came nearer he saw that she was young and beautiful. He spoke to her and said, "Dear child, can my servant and I find shelter for the night in that little house?"

"Oh yes," said the girl in a sad voice, "you certainly can, but I don't advise you to risk it. Don't go in."

"Why not?" asked the King's son.

The girl sighed and said, "My stepmother practises wicked arts. She means no good to strangers."

Then he saw plainly that he had come to a witch's house. But as it was dark and he could go no further, and he was not afraid either, he went in.

The old woman was sitting in an armchair by the fire, and looked at the stranger with her red eyes. "Good evening," she growled, and pretended to be quite friendly. "Sit down and rest yourselves." And she blew up the fire, on which she was cooking something in a little pot.

The daughter warned the two of them to be careful and to eat nothing and drink nothing, because the old woman brewed evil drinks. They slept quietly until early morning.

When they were making ready to leave, and the King's son was already on his horse, the old woman said, "Wait a moment, I'll fetch you a parting drink." And while she went to get it, the King's son rode off; so that the servant, who had stayed to buckle his saddle tight, was the only one there when the wicked witch came with the drink.

"Take that to your master," she said. But at that moment the glass broke and the poison spurted over the horse, and it was so strong that the animal dropped down dead on the spot.

The servant ran after his master and told him what had happened; but he did not want to leave his saddle behind, and ran back to fetch it. When he came to the dead horse, though, a raven was already sitting on it, eating.

"Who knows whether we shall find anything better today?" said the servant. So he killed the raven and took it with him.

And now they journeyed on into the forest all day long, but could not get out of it. By nightfall they came to an inn and went in. The servant gave the raven to the innkeeper to get ready for supper.

But they had stumbled on a den of murderers; and in the dark twelve of them came in, meaning to kill the strangers and rob them. Before they set about the work they sat down to supper, and the innkeeper and the witch sat down with them, and together they ate a dish of soup with the flesh of the raven cut up in it.

They had hardly swallowed a couple of mouthfuls, though, before they all fell down dead; for the raven had passed on to them the poison from the horseflesh.

There was nobody left in the house but the innkeeper's daughter, who was honest and had taken no part in their godless doings. She opened all the doors to the stranger and showed him the heaped-up treasures. But the King's son said she might keep the lot; he would have none of it; and he rode on with his servant.

After they had travelled about for a long time, they came to a town where there was a beautiful but proud princess, who had had it proclaimed that whoever could set her a riddle she could not solve should be her husband; but if she solved it, his head must be cut off. She had three days to guess it in; but she was so clever that she always found the answer to any riddle she was given before the time was up. Nine suitors had died in this way already, when the King's son arrived and, blinded by her great beauty, was willing to stake his life on it.

So he went to her and set her his riddle. "What is this?" he said. "One killed none, and yet killed twelve."

She did not know what it could be. She thought and thought, and could not find it out. She opened her riddle books, and it was not in them. In short, her wisdom was at an end.

As she could not help herself, she ordered her maid to creep into the gentleman's bedroom and listen to his dreams, thinking that perhaps he would talk in his sleep and give the riddle away. But the clever servant had put himself in the bed instead of his master; and when the maid came in he tore off the cloak she had wrapped herself in and chased her out with a stick.

The second night the King's daughter sent her lady-in-waiting, to see whether she would do better at listening; but the servant took her cloak away too and chased her out with a stick.

Now the master thought himself safe for the third night, and lay down in his own bed. And then the princess herself came, wrapped in a misty-grey cloak, and sat down beside him. And when she thought he was asleep and dreaming, she spoke to him and hoped he would answer in his sleep, as many people do. But he was awake, and understood and heard everything perfectly well.

Then she asked, "One killed none — what is that?"

He answered, "A raven, which ate of a dead and poisoned horse and died of it."

She asked further, "And yet killed twelve — what is that?"

He answered, "That means twelve murderers, who ate the raven and died of it."

When she knew the answer to the riddle she tried to steal away; but he held her cloak so fast that she had to leave it behind her.

Next morning the King's daughter announced that she had guessed the riddle, and sent for the twelve judges and explained it in front of them. But the young man asked for a hearing and said, "She crept into my room in the night and questioned me; otherwise she could never have found it out."

The judges said, "Bring us proof of that."

Then the servant brought in the three cloaks; and when the judges saw the misty-grey one that the King's daughter usually wore, they said, "Let that cloak be embroidered with gold and silver, and then it shall be your wedding cloak."
