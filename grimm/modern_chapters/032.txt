The Little Peasant

There was once a village where nobody lived but really rich farmers, and just one poor one, whom they called the little peasant. He did not own so much as a cow, still less the money to buy one; and he and his wife did so long to have one.

One day he said to her, "Listen, I've had a good idea. There's our friend the carpenter — he shall make us a wooden calf and paint it brown, so that it looks like any other; and in time it will surely grow big and be a cow."

The woman liked the idea too; and their friend the carpenter cut and planed the calf and painted it as it ought to be, and made it with its head hanging down, as if it were eating.

Next morning, when the cows were being driven out, the little peasant called the cowherd and said, "Look, I've a little calf here; but it's still small and has to be carried."

"All right," said the cowherd — and he took it in his arms and carried it to the pasture and set it down in the grass. The little calf went on standing there like one that was eating, and the cowherd said, "It will soon be running about on its own. Just look how it eats already!"

In the evening, when he was going to drive the herd home again, he said to the calf, "If you can stand there and eat your fill, you can go on your own four legs as well. I don't care to be dragging you home again in my arms."

But the little peasant stood at his door waiting for his little calf; and when the cowherd drove the cows through the village and the calf was missing, he asked where it was.

The cowherd answered, "It's still standing out there eating. It wouldn't stop and come along with us."

But the little peasant said, "Oh no — I must have my beast back again."

So they went back to the meadow together; but somebody had stolen the calf and it was gone.

The cowherd said, "It must have run away."

The peasant, though, said, "Don't tell me that" — and brought the cowherd before the mayor, who condemned him for his carelessness to give the peasant a cow for the calf that had run away.

And now the little peasant and his wife had the cow they had wished for so long, and they were heartily glad. But they had no fodder for it and could give it nothing to eat, so it soon had to be slaughtered. They salted the meat, and the peasant went to town meaning to sell the hide there, so that he could buy a new calf with the money.

On the way he passed a mill, and there sat a raven with broken wings; and out of pity he picked it up and wrapped it in the hide. But the weather turned so bad, and there was such a storm of wind and rain, that he could go no further; so he turned back to the mill and asked for shelter.

The miller's wife was alone in the house, and said to the peasant, "Lie down there on the straw" — and gave him a slice of bread with cheese on it.

The peasant ate it and lay down with his hide beside him, and the woman thought, "He's tired and has gone to sleep."

Meanwhile the parson arrived. The miller's wife made him welcome and said, "My husband's out, so we'll have a feast."

The peasant listened; and when he heard about feasting he was vexed that he had had to make do with a slice of bread and cheese. Then the woman brought out four different things: roast meat, salad, cakes and wine.

Just as they were about to sit down and eat there was a knocking outside.

"Oh, heavens!" said the woman. "It's my husband!"

She quickly hid the roast meat inside the tiled stove, the wine under the pillow, the salad on the bed, the cakes under it, and the parson in the cupboard in the passage. Then she opened the door to her husband and said, "Thank heaven you're back! There's such a storm, it looks as if the world were coming to an end."

The miller saw the peasant lying on the straw and asked, "What's that fellow doing there?"

"Ah," said his wife, "the poor man came in the wind and rain and begged for shelter, so I gave him a bit of bread and cheese and showed him where the straw was."

The man said, "I've no objection. But be quick and get me something to eat."

The woman said, "But I've nothing except bread and cheese."

"I'm content with anything," her husband answered. "As far as I'm concerned, bread and cheese will do." And he looked at the peasant and said, "Come and eat some more with me."

The peasant did not need to be asked twice, but got up and ate.

After this the miller noticed the hide lying on the ground with the raven in it, and asked, "What have you got there?"

The peasant answered, "There's a soothsayer inside it."

"Can he foretell anything for me?" said the miller.

"Why not?" answered the peasant. "But he only says four things, and the fifth he keeps to himself."

The miller was curious and said, "Let him foretell something for once."

So the peasant pinched the raven's head, and it croaked and made a noise like krr, krr.

The miller said, "What did he say?"

The peasant answered, "In the first place, he says there's some wine hidden under the pillow."

"Bless me!" cried the miller, and went and found the wine. "Now go on."

The peasant made the raven croak again, and said, "In the second place, he says there's roast meat in the tiled stove."

"Upon my word!" cried the miller, and went there and found the roast meat.

The peasant made the raven prophesy still more, and said, "Thirdly, he says there's salad on the bed."

"That would be a fine thing!" cried the miller, and went and found the salad.

At last the peasant pinched the raven once more till it croaked, and said, "Fourthly, he says there are cakes under the bed."

"That would be a fine thing!" cried the miller, and looked there and found the cakes.

And now the two of them sat down at the table together; but the miller's wife was frightened to death, and went to bed and took all the keys with her.

The miller would very much have liked to know the fifth thing; but the little peasant said, "First we'll eat up the four quickly, for the fifth is something bad."

So they ate; and after that they bargained over how much the miller was to give for the fifth prophecy, until they settled on three hundred thalers.

Then the peasant pinched the raven's head once more till it croaked loudly.

The miller asked, "What did he say?"

The peasant replied, "He says the Devil is hiding out there in the cupboard in the passage."

The miller said, "The Devil must go out" — and opened the house door. Then the woman was forced to hand over the keys, and the peasant unlocked the cupboard. The parson ran out as fast as he could, and the miller said, "It was true. I saw the black rascal with my own eyes."

The peasant, though, made off at daybreak next morning with the three hundred thalers.

At home the little peasant gradually began to spread himself. He built a beautiful house, and the other farmers said, "The little peasant has certainly been where the golden snow falls, and people carry the gold home by the shovelful."

Then the little peasant was brought before the mayor and told to say where his wealth came from. He answered, "I sold my cow's hide in the town for three hundred thalers."

When the farmers heard that, they wanted a share of this great profit too, and ran home, killed all their cows and stripped off the hides, so as to sell them in town to the best advantage.

The mayor, however, said, "But my servant must go first."

When she came to the merchant in the town he gave her no more than two thalers for a hide; and when the others came he did not give them even that much, and said, "What am I to do with all these hides?"

Then the farmers were furious that the little peasant should have taken them in like that, and wanted revenge, and accused him of this trickery before the mayor. The innocent little peasant was sentenced to death by common consent, and was to be rolled into the water in a barrel pierced full of holes.

He was led out, and a priest was fetched to say a mass for his soul. The rest all had to withdraw to a distance; and when the peasant looked at the priest he recognised the man who had been with the miller's wife.

He said to him, "I set you free from the cupboard — set me free from the barrel."

At that very moment who should come by with a flock of sheep but the shepherd, who the peasant knew had long been wanting to be mayor. So he cried out with all his might, "No, I won't do it! If the whole world insists on it, I won't do it!"

The shepherd heard him, came up and asked, "What are you about? What is it you won't do?"

The peasant said, "They want to make me mayor if I'll only get into the barrel; but I won't do it."

The shepherd said, "If that's all that's needed to be mayor, I'd get into the barrel this minute."

The peasant said, "If you'll get in, you'll be mayor."

The shepherd was willing, and got in, and the peasant shut the lid down on him; then he took the shepherd's flock for himself and drove it away.

The parson went over to the crowd and announced that the mass had been said. Then they came and rolled the barrel towards the water. When it began to roll, the shepherd called out, "I'm quite willing to be mayor!"

They supposed it was nobody but the peasant saying it, and answered, "That's what we intend. But first you shall have a look round down below there" — and they rolled the barrel into the water.

After that the farmers went home; and as they were coming into the village the little peasant came quietly in too, driving a flock of sheep and looking perfectly content.

Then the farmers were astonished, and said, "Peasant, where have you come from? Have you come out of the water?"

"Yes, indeed," replied the peasant. "I sank down deep, deep, until at last I got to the bottom; and I pushed the bottom out of the barrel and crept out, and there were beautiful meadows with any number of lambs feeding on them, and I brought this flock away with me."

Said the farmers, "Are there any more there?"

"Oh, yes," said he, "more than I knew what to do with."

Then the farmers made up their minds to fetch some sheep for themselves, a flock apiece; but the mayor said, "I come first."

So they went to the water together. And just then there were some of the small fleecy clouds in the blue sky that people call little lambs, and they were reflected in the water; whereupon the farmers cried, "We can see the sheep down there already!"

The mayor pressed forward and said, "I'll go down first and look round, and if it all looks promising I'll call you."

So he jumped in — splash went the water — and he made a sound as if he were calling them, and the whole crowd plunged in after him as one man.

Then the entire village was dead, and the little peasant, as sole heir, became a rich man.

The Queen Bee

Two king's sons once went out in search of adventures and fell into a wild, disorderly way of living, so that they never came home again. The youngest, who was called Simpleton, set out to look for his brothers; but when he did at last find them, they mocked him for supposing that he, with his simplicity, could make his way in the world, when the two of them could not manage it and were so much cleverer.

The three of them travelled on together and came to an ant hill. The two elder ones wanted to knock it down, to see the little ants scurrying about in their terror and carrying off their eggs; but Simpleton said, "Leave the creatures in peace. I won't let you disturb them."

Then they went on and came to a lake with a great many ducks swimming on it. The two brothers wanted to catch a couple and roast them, but Simpleton would not have it, and said, "Leave the creatures in peace. I won't let you kill them."

At last they came to a bees' nest with so much honey in it that it was running down the trunk of the tree. The two wanted to light a fire under the tree and smoke the bees to death so as to take the honey; but Simpleton stopped them again and said, "Leave the creatures in peace. I won't let you burn them."

At length the three brothers arrived at a castle where stone horses were standing in the stables and not a single human being was to be seen. They went through all the halls, until right at the end they came to a door with three locks in it. In the middle of the door, though, there was a little pane through which they could see into the room; and there they saw a little grey man sitting at a table. They called to him once, and twice, but he did not hear; and at the third call he got up, opened the locks and came out.

He said nothing, but led them to a handsomely laid table; and when they had eaten and drunk he took each of them to a bedroom.

Next morning the little grey man came to the eldest, beckoned to him and led him to a stone table on which three tasks were carved, by the doing of which the castle could be delivered.

The first was that the princess's pearls, a thousand of them, lay in the forest under the moss and must be picked up; and if a single pearl were missing at sunset, whoever had been looking for them would be turned to stone.

The eldest went there and searched the whole day; but when it came to an end he had found only a hundred, and what was written on the table came to pass, and he was changed into stone.

Next day the second brother undertook the adventure; but it went not much better with him than with the eldest. He did not find more than two hundred pearls, and was changed to stone.

At last it was Simpleton's turn as well, and he searched in the moss. But the pearls were so hard to find and he got on so slowly that he sat down on a stone and wept. And while he was sitting there, the King of the ants, whose life he had once saved, came with five thousand ants; and before long the little creatures had all the pearls collected and laid in a heap.

The second task, though, was to fetch the key of the King's daughter's bedchamber out of the lake. When Simpleton came to the lake, the ducks he had saved swam up to him, dived down and brought the key up out of the water.

But the third task was the hardest of all: the youngest and dearest of the King's three sleeping daughters had to be picked out from among them. They looked exactly alike, and there was nothing to tell them apart except that they had each eaten a different sweet thing before falling asleep — the eldest a lump of sugar, the second a little syrup, and the youngest a spoonful of honey.

Then the Queen of the bees, which Simpleton had saved from the fire, came and tasted the lips of all three; and at last she stayed sitting on the mouth that had eaten honey, and so the King's son knew the right princess.

Then the enchantment was at an end. Everything was released from its sleep, and all those who had been turned to stone got their own shapes back again. Simpleton married the youngest and sweetest princess, and became King after her father's death; and his two brothers got the other two sisters.

The Three Feathers

There was once a King who had three sons. Two of them were clever and quick-witted; but the third did not say much, and was simple, and was called the Simpleton.

When the King had grown old and weak and was thinking about his end, he did not know which of his sons should inherit the kingdom after him. So he said to them, "Go out into the world, and whichever of you brings me the most beautiful carpet shall be King after my death."

And so that there should be no quarrelling among them, he took them outside his castle, blew three feathers into the air, and said, "You shall go where they fly."

One feather flew east and one flew west; but the third flew straight up and did not fly far, and soon dropped to the ground.

And now one brother went to the right and the other to the left, and they mocked Simpleton, who had to stay where the third feather had fallen.

He sat down and was sad; and then all at once he saw that there was a trapdoor close by the feather. He lifted it, found some steps and went down them, and then came to another door and knocked at it, and heard somebody inside calling:

	"Little green maiden, hopping so free,
	hop to the door and see who it may be."

The door opened, and he saw a great fat toad sitting there with a crowd of little toads around her.

The fat toad asked what he wanted. He answered, "I should like to have the prettiest and finest carpet in the world."

Then she called one of the young ones and said:

	"Little green maiden, hop away quick,
	the great box yonder is what I pick."

The young toad brought the box, and the fat toad opened it and gave Simpleton a carpet out of it, so beautiful and so finely made that up on the earth above nobody could have woven the like of it. So he thanked her and climbed up again.

The other two had thought their youngest brother so stupid that they were sure he would find and bring nothing at all.

"Why should we give ourselves any trouble to search?" they said — and they got a couple of coarse handkerchiefs from the first shepherds' wives they met, and carried them home to the King.

At the same moment Simpleton came back too, and brought his beautiful carpet; and when the King saw it he was astonished and said, "If justice is done, the kingdom belongs to the youngest."

But the other two gave their father no peace, and said it was impossible that Simpleton, who had no sense in anything, should be King, and begged him to make a new bargain with them.

So the father said, "Whoever brings me the most beautiful ring shall inherit the kingdom" — and he led the three brothers out and blew three feathers into the air for them to follow.

The two eldest ones' feathers went east and west again, and Simpleton's flew straight up and dropped down beside the door into the earth. So he went down again to the fat toad and told her that he wanted the most beautiful ring in the world. She at once ordered her great box to be brought, and gave him a ring out of it that sparkled with jewels, and was so beautiful that no goldsmith on earth could have made it.

The two eldest laughed at Simpleton for going to look for a golden ring. They gave themselves no trouble at all, but knocked the nails out of an old cartwheel-ring and took that to the King. But when Simpleton produced his golden ring, his father said again, "The kingdom belongs to him."

The two eldest did not stop tormenting the King until he made a third condition, and declared that whoever brought home the most beautiful woman should have the kingdom. He blew the three feathers into the air again, and they flew as they had before.

Then Simpleton went straight down to the fat toad and said, "I'm to take home the most beautiful woman!"

"Oho," answered the toad, "the most beautiful woman! She isn't to hand at the moment, but you shall have her all the same."

She gave him a hollowed-out yellow turnip with six mice harnessed to it. Then Simpleton said quite miserably, "What am I to do with that?"

The toad answered, "Just put one of my little toads inside it."

So he took one at random out of the ring of them and set her in the yellow carriage; and hardly was she seated in it before she turned into a wonderfully beautiful girl, and the turnip into a carriage, and the six mice into horses. So he kissed her and drove off at a good pace and brought her to the King.

His brothers came along afterwards. They had given themselves no trouble at all to look for beautiful girls, but had brought the first peasant women they happened to meet.

When the King saw them he said, "After my death the kingdom belongs to my youngest son."

But the two eldest deafened the King's ears again with their outcry — "We can't consent to Simpleton's being King" — and demanded that the one whose wife could jump through a ring hanging in the middle of the hall should have the preference. They thought, "The peasant women can do that easily, they're strong enough; but the delicate girl will jump herself to death."

The old King agreed to this as well. So the two peasant women jumped, and jumped through the ring, but were so heavy that they fell and broke their coarse arms and legs. And then the pretty girl Simpleton had brought with him sprang, and sprang through as lightly as a deer, and all opposition had to come to an end.

So he was given the crown, and has ruled wisely for a long time now.
