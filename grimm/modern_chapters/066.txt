The Turnip

There were once two brothers who both served as soldiers; one of them was rich and the other poor. Then the poor one, to escape from his poverty, took off his soldier's coat and turned farmer. He dug and hoed his bit of land and sowed it with turnip seed.

The seed came up, and one turnip grew there that became large and strong, and visibly grew bigger and bigger and seemed as if it would never stop growing, so that it might have been called the princess of turnips; for such a one was never seen before and never will be seen again.

At length it was so enormous that by itself it filled a whole cart, and two oxen were needed to draw it; and the farmer had not the least idea what he was to do with the turnip, or whether it would be a fortune to him or a misfortune.

At last he thought, "If you sell it, what will you get for it that's worth anything? And if you eat it yourself, the small turnips would do you just as much good. It would be better to take it to the King and make him a present of it."

So he put it on a cart and harnessed two oxen and took it to the palace and presented it to the King.

"What strange thing is this?" said the King. "Many wonderful things have come before my eyes, but never such a monster as this. What seed can it have sprung from? Or are you a luck-child, and came upon it by chance?"

"Ah, no," said the farmer, "I'm no luck-child. I'm a poor soldier who, because he could no longer support himself, hung his soldier's coat on a nail and took to farming. I have a brother who is rich and well known to you, my lord King; but I, because I have nothing, am forgotten by everybody."

Then the King felt pity for him, and said, "You shall be raised out of your poverty, and shall have such gifts from me that you'll be your rich brother's equal." And he bestowed on him a great deal of gold, and lands and meadows and herds, and made him immensely rich, so that his brother's wealth could not be compared with it.

When the rich brother heard what the poor one had got for himself with one single turnip, he envied him, and thought in every way he could how he too might get hold of a similar piece of luck. He would set about it in a much cleverer way, though; and he took gold and horses and carried them to the King, quite certain that the King would give him a far larger present in return. If his brother had got so much for one turnip, what would he not carry away in return for such beautiful things as these?

The King accepted his present, and said that he had nothing to give him in return that was more rare and excellent than the great turnip. So the rich man had to put his brother's turnip on a cart and have it taken home.

Once there, he did not know whom to vent his rage and anger on, until bad thoughts came to him and he resolved to kill his brother. He hired murderers, who were to lie in ambush; and then he went to his brother and said, "Dear brother, I know of a hidden treasure. We'll dig it up together and divide it between us."

The other agreed to this and went with him without suspicion. But while they were on their way, the murderers fell on him and bound him and would have hanged him from a tree.

But just as they were doing it, loud singing and the sound of a horse's hoofs were heard in the distance. At this their hearts were filled with terror, and they pushed their prisoner head first into a sack and hung it on a branch and took to flight.

He, though, worked away up there until he had made a hole in the sack he could put his head through. The man who was coming along was none other than a travelling student, a young fellow who was riding on his way through the wood, joyfully singing his song.

When the one up aloft saw that somebody was passing below him, he cried, "Good day! You've come at a lucky time."

The student looked round on every side, but did not know where the voice came from. At last he said, "Who's calling me?"

Then an answer came from the top of the tree. "Raise your eyes; here I sit aloft in the Sack of Wisdom. In a short time I've learned great things; compared with this, all schools are a joke. In a very short while I shall have learned everything, and shall come down wiser than all other men. I understand the stars and the signs of the Zodiac and the tracks of the winds, the sand of the sea, the healing of illness and the virtues of all herbs and birds and stones. If you were once inside it, you'd feel what noble things come out of the Sack of Knowledge."

When the student heard all this he was astonished, and said, "Blessed be the hour in which I found you! May I not get into the sack for a while too?"

The one above replied, as if unwillingly, "For a short time I'll let you get into it, if you reward me and give me good words. But you must wait an hour longer, for there's one thing left that I must learn before I do it."

When the student had waited a while he grew impatient and begged to be let in at once; his thirst for knowledge was so very great. So the one above pretended to give way at last, and said, "So that I can come out of the house of knowledge, you must let it down by the rope, and then you shall get in."

So the student let the sack down and untied it and set him free, and then cried, "Now draw me up at once" — and was about to get into the sack.

"Stop!" said the other. "That won't do." And he took him by the head and put him upside down into the sack and fastened it, and drew the disciple of wisdom up the tree by the rope. Then he swung him about in the air and said, "How goes it with you, my dear fellow? Look — you can feel the wisdom coming already, and you're gaining valuable experience. Keep perfectly quiet until you grow wiser."

And with that he mounted the student's horse and rode away — but in an hour's time he sent somebody to let the student out again.

The Old Man Made Young Again

In the days when our Lord still walked this earth, he and St. Peter stopped one evening at a smith's and were given free lodging. Then it happened that a poor beggar, hard pressed by age and infirmity, came to this house and begged alms of the smith.

St. Peter had pity on him and said, "Lord and master, if it please you, cure his troubles so that he can earn his own bread."

The Lord said kindly, "Smith, lend me your forge and put some coals on for me, and then I'll make this ailing old man young again."

The smith was quite willing, and St. Peter blew the bellows; and when the coal fire sparked up large and high, our Lord took the little old man and pushed him into the forge in the middle of the red-hot fire, so that he glowed like a rose bush and praised God in a loud voice.

After that the Lord went to the quenching tub and put the glowing little man into it, so that the water closed over him; and when he had carefully cooled him he gave him his blessing — and behold, the little man sprang nimbly out, looking fresh and straight and healthy, and as if he were only twenty.

The smith, who had watched everything closely and attentively, invited them all to supper. But he had an old, half-blind, crooked mother-in-law, who went to the young man and asked him very earnestly whether the fire had burned him much. He answered that he had never felt more comfortable, and that he had sat in the red heat as if he were sitting in cool dew.

The young man's words echoed in the old woman's ears all night long; and early next morning, when the Lord had gone on his way again and been heartily thanked by the smith, the smith thought that he might make his old mother-in-law young again in the same way, since he had watched everything so carefully and it lay within his own trade.

So he called to ask her whether she too would like to go bounding about like a girl of eighteen.

She said, "With all my heart, since the young man came out of it so well."

So the smith made a great fire and thrust the old woman into it; and she writhed about this way and that and uttered terrible cries of murder.

"Sit still! Why are you screaming and jumping about like that?" cried he. And as he spoke he blew the bellows again, until all her rags were burnt.

The old woman cried without stopping, and the smith thought to himself, "I haven't quite got the right art" — and took her out and threw her into the cooling tub. Then she screamed so loudly that the smith's wife upstairs and her daughter-in-law heard her, and both of them came running downstairs and saw the old woman lying in a heap in the quenching tub, howling and screaming, with her face wrinkled and shrivelled and all out of shape.

At this the two of them, who were both with child, were so terrified that that very night two boys were born who were not made like men but like apes; and they ran off into the woods, and from them sprang the race of apes.

The Lord’s Animals and the Devil’s

The Lord God had created all the animals, and had chosen the wolf to be his dog; but he had forgotten the goat.

Then the Devil made ready and began to create as well, and created goats with fine long tails. Now when they went out to pasture they generally got caught in the hedges by their tails, and then the Devil had to go and disentangle them, with a great deal of trouble. This enraged him at last, and he went and bit off the tail of every goat, as may be seen to this day by the stump.

Then he let them go out to pasture alone; but it came about that the Lord God saw how at one time they gnawed away at a fruit tree, at another damaged the noble vines, or destroyed other tender plants. This distressed him, so that in his goodness and mercy he summoned his wolves, who soon tore to pieces the goats that went there.

When the Devil saw this he went before the Lord and said, "Your creatures have destroyed mine."

The Lord answered, "Why did you create things that do harm?"

The Devil said, "I was forced to it. Since my thoughts run on evil, what I create can have no other nature; and you must pay me heavy damages."

"I'll pay you as soon as the oak leaves fall. Come then, and your money will be counted out ready."

When the oak leaves had fallen the Devil came and demanded what was due to him. But the Lord said, "In the church of Constantinople stands a tall oak tree that still has all its leaves."

With raging and cursing the Devil went off, and went to look for the oak; and he wandered in the wilderness for six months before he found it — and when he came back, all the other oaks had covered themselves with green leaves again in the meantime.

So he had to forfeit his compensation; and in his rage he put out the eyes of all the remaining goats and put his own in instead.

That is why all goats have devil's eyes and their tails bitten off, and why he likes to take on their shape.

The Beam

There was once an enchanter who was standing in the middle of a great crowd of people performing his wonders. He had a cock brought in, which lifted a heavy beam and carried it as if it were as light as a feather.

But a girl was there who had just found a four-leaved clover, and had become so wise by it that no deception could stand up against her; and she saw that the beam was nothing but a straw. So she cried out, "You people, don't you see that what the cock is carrying is a straw and no beam?"

Immediately the enchantment vanished, and the people saw what it was, and drove the magician away in shame and disgrace. But he, full of inward anger, said, "I'll soon have my revenge."

After some time the girl's wedding day came, and she was dressed up and went in a great procession over the fields to the place where the church was. All at once she came to a stream that was very much swollen, and there was no bridge and no plank to cross by. So the bride nimbly took her clothes up and meant to wade through it.

And just as she was standing in the water like this, a man — and it was the enchanter — cried mockingly close beside her, "Aha! Where are your eyes, that you take that for water?"

Then her eyes were opened, and she saw that she was standing with her clothes lifted up in the middle of a field that was blue with the flowers of blue flax. And all the people saw it too, and chased her away with jeers and laughter.

The Old Beggar-Woman

There was once an old woman — but you've surely seen an old woman go begging before now? This woman begged too, and when she was given anything she said, "May God reward you."

The beggar-woman came to a door, and there by the fire a friendly rogue of a boy was standing warming himself. The boy said kindly to the poor old woman, as she stood shivering there by the door, "Come in, old mother, and warm yourself."

She came in, but stood too close to the fire, so that her old rags began to burn, and she did not notice it. The boy stood and saw it — but he ought to have put the flames out. Isn't it true that he ought to have put them out? And if he had no water, then he should have wept all the water in his body out of his eyes, and that would have made two pretty streams to put them out with.

The Three Sluggards

A certain King had three sons who were all equally dear to him, and he did not know which of them to appoint as his successor after his death.

When the time came that he was about to die, he called them to his bedside and said, "Dear children, I've been thinking of something that I'll now tell you. Whichever of you is the laziest shall have the kingdom."

The eldest said, "Then, father, the kingdom is mine; for I'm so idle that if I lie down to rest and a drop falls in my eye, I won't open it, so that I can go on sleeping."

The second said, "Father, the kingdom belongs to me; for I'm so idle that when I'm sitting by the fire warming myself, I'd sooner let my heel burn off than draw my leg back."

The third said, "Father, the kingdom is mine; for I'm so idle that if I were going to be hanged and had the rope round my neck already, and somebody put a sharp knife into my hand to cut the rope with, I'd sooner let myself be hanged than raise my hand to the rope."

When their father heard that he said, "You've carried it furthest, and you shall be King."

The Twelve Idle Servants

Twelve servants who had done nothing all day would not exert themselves at night either, but laid themselves down on the grass and boasted about their idleness.

The first said, "What's your laziness to me? I have my own to think about. The care of my body is my chief work: I eat not a little and drink still more. When I've had four meals I fast a short while until I feel hungry again, and that suits me best. Getting up early isn't for me; when it's getting on towards midday I'm already looking out a resting place for myself. If the master calls, I behave exactly as if I hadn't heard him; and if he calls a second time, I wait a while before I get up, and go to him very slowly. Life is bearable that way."

The second said, "I have a horse to look after, but I leave the bit in his mouth; and if I don't feel like it, I give him no food and say he's had it already. But I lay myself down in the oat chest and sleep for four hours. After that I stretch out one foot and move it a couple of times over the horse's body, and then he's combed and cleaned. Who's going to make a great business of that? All the same, service is too toilsome for me."

The third said, "Why plague yourself with work? Nothing comes of it. I laid myself down in the sun and fell asleep. It began to rain a little — but why should I get up? I let it rain on, in God's name. At last a splashing shower came, so heavy that it pulled the hair out of my head and washed it away, and I got a hole in my skull. I put a plaster on it, and then it was all right. I've had several injuries of that kind already."

The fourth said, "If I'm to undertake a piece of work, I first loiter about for an hour to save up my strength. After that I begin quite slowly, and ask whether there's nobody about who could help me. Then I let him do the chief part of the work, and really only look on — but even that's still too much for me."

The fifth said, "What does that matter? Just think — I'm supposed to take the manure out of the horse's stable and load the cart with it. I let it go on slowly; and if I've got anything on the fork, I only half raise it, and then I rest for a good quarter of an hour before I throw it in. It's enough and to spare if I take out one cartload in the day. I've no fancy for killing myself with work."

The sixth said, "Shame on you. I'm afraid of no work — but I lie down for three weeks at a time and never once take my clothes off. What's the use of buckling your shoes on? For all I care they can fall off my feet, it doesn't matter. If I'm going up some steps, I drag one foot slowly after the other onto the first step, and then I count the rest of them so that I know where I have to rest."

The seventh said, "That won't do for me; my master keeps an eye on my work — only he's not at home all day. But I neglect nothing; I run as fast as it's possible to run when you're crawling. If I'm to get on at all, four sturdy men have to push me with all their might. I came where six men were lying asleep on a bed side by side. I lay down beside them and slept too. There was no waking me again, and when they wanted me home they had to carry me."

The eighth said, "I can see plainly that I'm the only active fellow here. If a stone lies in front of me, I don't give myself the trouble of raising my legs and stepping over it. I lay myself down on the ground; and if I'm wet and covered with mud and dirt, I stay lying there until the sun has dried me again. At the very most I only turn myself over so that it can shine on me."

The ninth said, "That's the right way. Today the bread was in front of me, but I was too idle to take it, and nearly died of hunger. There was a jug standing by it too, but it was so big and heavy that I didn't care to lift it, and preferred to bear the thirst. Just turning myself over was too much for me; I stayed lying like a log the whole day."

The tenth said, "Laziness has brought misfortune on me — a broken leg and a swollen calf. Three of us were lying in the road and I had my legs stretched out. Somebody came along with a cart, and the wheels went over me. I could have drawn my legs back, of course, but I didn't hear the cart coming, because the midges were humming about my ears and creeping in at my nose and out again at my mouth. Who can take the trouble to drive the vermin away?"

The eleventh said, "I gave up my place yesterday. I'd no fancy for carrying the heavy books to my master any longer, or fetching them away again. There was no end to it all day long. But to tell the truth, he gave me my notice and wouldn't keep me any longer, because his clothes, which I'd left lying in the dust, were all moth-eaten. And I'm very glad of it."

The twelfth said, "Today I had to drive the cart out into the country, and I made myself a bed of straw on it and had a good sleep. The reins slipped out of my hand; and when I woke, the horse had nearly torn itself loose, the harness was gone, the strap that fastens the horse to the shafts was gone, and so were the collar and the bridle and bit. Somebody had come by and carried it all off. On top of that the cart had got into a bog and stuck fast. I left it standing and stretched myself out on the straw again. At last the master came himself and pushed the cart out; and if he hadn't come, I shouldn't be lying here but there, and sleeping in perfect peace."

The Shepherd Boy

There was once a shepherd boy whose fame spread far and wide because of the wise answers he gave to every question. The King of the country heard of it too, but did not believe it, and sent for the boy.

Then he said to him, "If you can give me an answer to three questions I'll ask you, I'll look on you as my own child, and you shall live with me in my royal palace."

The boy said, "What are the three questions?"

The King said, "The first is: how many drops of water are there in the ocean?"

The shepherd boy answered, "My lord King, if you'll have all the rivers on earth dammed up so that not a single drop runs from them into the sea until I've counted it, I'll tell you how many drops there are in the sea."

The King said, "The next question is: how many stars are there in the sky?"

The shepherd boy said, "Give me a great sheet of white paper." And then he made so many fine points on it with a pen that they could hardly be seen, and it was all but impossible to count them; anybody who looked at them would have lost his sight. Then he said, "There are as many stars in the sky as there are points on this paper. Just count them." But nobody was able to do it.

The King said, "The third question is: how many seconds of time are there in eternity?"

Then the shepherd boy said, "In Lower Pomerania is the Diamond Mountain, which is two and a half miles high, two and a half miles wide and two and a half miles deep. Every hundred years a little bird comes and sharpens its beak on it; and when the whole mountain has been worn away by this, then the first second of eternity will be over."

The King said, "You've answered the three questions like a wise man, and from now on you shall live with me in my royal palace, and I'll regard you as my own child."
