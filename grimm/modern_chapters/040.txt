The Singing, Springing Lark

There was once a man who was about to set out on a long journey; and as he was leaving he asked his three daughters what he should bring back for them. The eldest wished for pearls, and the second for diamonds; but the third said, "Dear father, I should like a singing, soaring lark."

The father said, "Yes, if I can get it, you shall have it" — and kissed all three and set out.

Now when the time came for him to be on his way home again, he had bought pearls and diamonds for the two eldest; but he had searched everywhere in vain for a singing, soaring lark for the youngest, and he was very unhappy about it, for she was his favourite child.

Then his road led him through a forest, and in the middle of it stood a splendid castle; and near the castle stood a tree, and right at the top of the tree he saw a singing, soaring lark.

"Aha, you've come just at the right moment," he said, quite delighted, and called to his servant to climb up and catch the little creature. But as he came up to the tree, a lion sprang out from under it, shook himself, and roared until the leaves on the trees trembled.

"Whoever tries to steal my singing, soaring lark," he cried, "I'll devour."

Then the man said, "I didn't know the bird belonged to you. I'll make amends for the wrong I've done and buy myself off with a large sum of money — only spare my life."

The lion said, "Nothing can save you unless you'll promise to give me for my own whatever first meets you when you get home. And if you'll do that, I'll grant you your life, and you shall have the bird for your daughter into the bargain."

But the man hesitated and said, "That might be my youngest daughter. She loves me best and always runs out to meet me when I come home."

The servant, though, was terrified, and said, "Why should it be your daughter who meets you? It could just as easily be a cat or a dog."

So the man let himself be talked round, and took the singing, soaring lark, and promised to give the lion whatever first met him when he got home.

When he reached home and went into his house, the first to meet him was none other than his youngest and dearest daughter, who came running up and kissed and hugged him; and when she saw that he had brought a singing, soaring lark with him she was beside herself with joy.

But her father could not rejoice, and began to weep, and said, "My dearest child, I've bought that little bird dear. To get it, I had to promise you to a savage lion; and when he has you he'll tear you to pieces and devour you." And he told her the whole thing just as it had happened, and begged her not to go, come what might.

But she comforted him and said, "Dearest father, your promise must be kept. I'll go there and soften the lion, so that I come back to you safe."

Next morning she had the road pointed out to her, took her leave, and went fearlessly out into the forest.

The lion, however, was an enchanted prince: by day he was a lion, and all his people were lions with him, but at night they took their natural human shapes again. When she arrived she was kindly received and led into the castle. When night came the lion turned into a handsome man, and their wedding was celebrated with great magnificence. They lived happily together, staying awake at night and sleeping through the day.

One day he came and said, "Tomorrow there's a feast at your father's house, because your eldest sister is to be married. If you'd like to go, my lions shall take you there."

She said, "Yes, I should very much like to see my father again" — and off she went, with the lions for company.

There was great rejoicing when she arrived, for they had all believed that she had been torn to pieces by the lion and had long since stopped living. But she told them what a handsome husband she had and how well off she was, and stayed with them as long as the wedding feast lasted, and then went back to the forest.

When the second daughter was about to be married and she was invited to that wedding too, she said to the lion, "This time I won't go alone. You must come with me."

But the lion said it was too dangerous for him; for if a ray of light from a burning candle fell on him there, he would be changed into a dove and would have to fly about with the doves for seven years.

She said, "Ah, but do come with me. I'll take great care of you and keep you from all light."

So they went off together and took their little child with them as well. She had a room built there, so strong and thick that no ray of light could get through it, and he was to shut himself up in it when the candles were lit for the wedding feast. But the door was made of green wood, which warped and left a little crack that nobody noticed.

The wedding was celebrated with great splendour; but when the procession came back from church with all its candles and torches and passed this room, a ray about the width of a hair fell on the King's son; and the moment it touched him he was transformed. When she came in and looked for him she did not see him, but a white dove was sitting there.

The dove said to her, "For seven years I must fly about the world; but at every seventh step you take, I'll let fall a drop of red blood and a white feather, and those will show you the way; and if you follow the trail you can set me free."

With that the dove flew out at the door, and she followed him; and at every seventh step a red drop of blood and a little white feather fell down and showed her the way.

So she went on and on, further and further into the wide world, never looking about her and never resting; and the seven years were almost up. Then she rejoiced, and thought that they would soon be free — and yet they were far from it.

Once, as they were going on like this, no little feather and no drop of red blood fell; and when she raised her eyes the dove had vanished. And as she thought to herself, "No man can help you in this," she climbed up to the sun and said to him, "You shine into every crevice and over every peak. Haven't you seen a white dove flying?"

"No," said the sun, "I've seen none; but I'll make you a present of this casket. Open it when you're in your greatest need."

Then she thanked the sun and went on until evening came and the moon appeared, and she asked her, "You shine the whole night through, over every field and forest. Haven't you seen a white dove flying?"

"No," said the moon, "I've seen no dove; but here, I'll give you this egg. Break it when you're in great need."

She thanked the moon and went on until the night wind got up and blew on her; and then she said to it, "You blow over every tree and under every leaf. Haven't you seen a white dove flying?"

"No," said the night wind, "I've seen none; but I'll ask the three other winds — perhaps they've seen it."

The east wind and the west wind came, and had seen nothing; but the south wind said, "I've seen the white dove. It has flown to the Red Sea, where it has become a lion again, because the seven years are over; and the lion is there fighting with a dragon — and the dragon is an enchanted princess."

Then the night wind said to her, "I'll give you my advice. Go to the Red Sea; on the right-hand bank there are some tall reeds. Count them, break off the eleventh, and strike the dragon with it; then the lion will be able to overcome it, and the two of them will get their human shapes back. After that, look round and you'll see the griffin that lives by the Red Sea. Swing yourself up onto his back with your beloved, and the bird will carry you home across the sea. And here's a nut for you: when you're above the middle of the sea, let the nut fall. It will shoot up at once, and a tall nut tree will grow out of the water for the griffin to rest on; for if he can't rest, he won't be strong enough to carry you across — and if you forget to throw the nut down, he'll let you fall into the sea."

So she went there and found everything as the night wind had said. She counted the reeds by the sea and cut off the eleventh and struck the dragon with it, whereupon the lion overcame it; and immediately both of them got their human shapes back. But when the princess, who had been the dragon, was released from the enchantment, she took the young man by the arm, seated herself on the griffin, and carried him off with her.

And there stood the poor girl who had wandered so far, deserted all over again. She sat down and cried; but at last she took courage and said, "Still, I'll go as far as the wind blows and as long as the cock crows, until I find him." And she went off along long, long roads, until at last she came to the castle where the two of them were living together.

There she heard that a feast was soon to be held at which they would celebrate their wedding; but she said, "God helps me still" — and opened the casket the sun had given her. Inside lay a dress as brilliant as the sun itself. So she took it out and put it on and went up into the castle, and everybody, even the bride herself, looked at her in astonishment.

The dress pleased the bride so much that she thought it might do for her wedding dress, and asked whether it was for sale.

"Not for money or land," she answered, "but for flesh and blood."

The bride asked her what she meant by that, and she said, "Let me sleep one night in the room where the bridegroom sleeps."

The bride did not want to; but she wanted the dress very much, and at last she consented — but the page was to give the prince a sleeping draught.

So when it was night and the young man was already asleep, she was led into the room. She sat down on the bed and said, "I've followed you for seven years. I've been to the sun and the moon and the four winds asking after you, and I helped you against the dragon. Will you forget me altogether now?"

But the prince slept so soundly that it only seemed to him as if the wind were whistling outside in the fir trees.

So when day broke she was led out again, and had to give up the golden dress. And as that had been no use either, she was sad, and went out into a meadow and sat down and wept.

While she was sitting there she thought of the egg the moon had given her. She opened it, and out came a clucking hen with twelve chicks all of gold, running about and cheeping and creeping back under the old hen's wings; nothing more beautiful was ever seen in the world.

Then she got up and drove them across the meadow in front of her until the bride looked out of the window. The little chicks pleased her so much that she came straight down and asked whether they were for sale.

"Not for money or land, but for flesh and blood. Let me sleep another night in the room where the bridegroom sleeps."

The bride said yes, meaning to cheat her as she had the evening before. But when the prince went to bed he asked the page what the murmuring and rustling in the night had been. At that the page told him everything: that he had been made to give him a sleeping draught because a poor girl had slept secretly in the room, and that he was to give him another one that night.

The prince said, "Pour the draught out beside the bed."

At night she was led in again; and when she began to tell how badly everything had gone with her, he knew his own dear wife at once by her voice, and sprang up and cried, "Now I really am set free! I've been as if in a dream, because that strange princess bewitched me so that I had to forget you; but God has delivered me from the spell in good time."

Then the two of them left the castle secretly in the night, for they were afraid of the princess's father, who was a sorcerer; and they seated themselves on the griffin, which carried them across the Red Sea; and when they were in the middle of it she let the nut fall. At once a tall nut tree grew up, and the bird rested on it, and then carried them home, where they found their child, who had grown tall and beautiful; and from that time on they lived happily until their deaths.
