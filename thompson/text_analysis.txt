TEXT ANALYSIS — Silvanus P. Thompson, LIGHT VISIBLE AND INVISIBLE (1897)
The Royal Institution Christmas Lectures of 1896

READ FIRST: soap-bubbles/text_analysis.txt, candle/text_analysis.txt and
fleming/text_analysis.txt. This is the FIFTH Royal Institution Christmas
Lecture volume in the collection, after Boys (1890), Faraday (1861 and
1860) and Fleming (1902). The register carries over almost entirely — a
scientist demonstrating, live, to an audience largely of children, in the
continuous present tense. Everything those three documents say about
keeping the demonstration a demonstration applies here without change.

What is new here is the news. Röntgen announced his rays on 28 December
1895. Thompson gave this course a year later — the first lecture is dated
in the text to "29th December 1896, 3 o'clock" — and devoted the whole of
Lecture Six to X-rays, quoting Röntgen's own account of the discovery from
an interview published only months before. Nothing else in the collection
is this close to the event it describes.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHO THOMPSON IS, AND HOW HE DIFFERS FROM THE OTHER FOUR
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Silvanus Phillips Thompson (1851-1916): Professor of Physics at Finsbury
Technical College, FRS, biographer of Faraday and of Kelvin, author of the
textbook every engineering student of the period owned. A Quaker, a
teacher by vocation, and a reformer with a grievance.

His voice against the others:

  - BOYS is delighted. Everything is a lovely thing that happens to a
    bubble.
  - FARADAY is affectionate. "My boys and girls", constant self-
    interruption to admire something.
  - FLEMING is an engineer. Everything is a thing on a table.
  - THOMPSON IS A REFORMER. He believes optics is taught wrongly, and he
    has come to say so. The first lecture opens by throwing out the whole
    apparatus of rays and angles of incidence and replacing it with
    waves — and he says plainly that the orthodox method is
    "fundamentally wrong". He believes that hard words, not hard ideas,
    are what make science look difficult, and he says so in the middle of
    Lecture Three: scientific men "often fall into the habit of using long
    and difficult words to express very simple and easy ideas."

That last sentence is the thesis of this whole PROJECT, written by the
author himself in 1897. It stays, and it stays prominent.

His grievance surfaces once more, at the end of Lecture Six, in an
extraordinary aside comparing the funding of German universities with the
neglect of scientific education in England — Würzburg, a town of 61,000,
supporting a university on £41,000 a year while London, with five million
people, has "a mere Examining Board". Render this straight and complete.
It is the one place where the lecturer stops lecturing and argues.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE ARC OF THE SIX LECTURES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

  I.   Lights and Shadows — light is waves; the ripple-tank; shadows;
       photometry; reflexion; lenses; the eye; the magic mirror of Japan
  II.  The Visible Spectrum and the Eye — the prism; colour; the three
       primary sensations; complementary tints; colour photography
  III. The Polarisation of Light — the plain-language attack on a word;
       Nicol prisms; crystals; testing false gems; Newton's rings
  IV.  The Invisible Spectrum, the Ultra-Violet Part — photography beyond
       the violet; fluorescence; phosphorescence; luminescence
  V.   The Invisible Spectrum, the Infra-Red Part — radiant heat; the
       thermopile; the bolometer; Hertz's waves; the electromagnetic
       theory of light
  VI.  Röntgen Light — vacuum tubes; kathode rays; Crookes; Röntgen's
       discovery in his own words; what X-rays do and do not do

Each lecture has an appendix. They are NOT optional matter: Thompson says
in his Introduction that he added them "for the sake of students and
teachers of optics", and Appendix I is a complete reworking of geometrical
optics on wave principles that he had been teaching for fifteen years.
Translate all six.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE GOVERNING PRINCIPLE: LIGHT IS WAVES, AND SO IS EVERYTHING
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Thompson's method is to teach light entirely through the behaviour of
water waves in a ripple-tank, and only afterwards to say that light does
the same. Two consequences for the translation:

  - The RIPPLE-TANK is the spine of the book. Every optical idea is first
    shown in water. Keep the water experiments in the same detail as the
    light ones; do not compress them as "preliminaries".
  - His four propositions about waves — they march at right angles to
    their own front; a wave-front bends round obstacles smaller than a
    wave-length; curvature is what a lens imprints; the sagitta measures
    the curvature — are stated once and used for two hundred pages. Keep
    the wording consistent every time they recur (see running_notes.txt).

Above all: WAVE-FRONT, not ray. Thompson has abolished the ray on purpose.
Where he uses the word "ray" he almost always says, in the same breath,
that there really are no rays. Never reintroduce ray-talk to smooth a
sentence.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE VERNE RULE: DATED CLAIMS ARE KEPT, DATED WORDS ARE MODERNIZED
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

This book is a photograph of physics in the twelve months after the
discovery of X-rays, and the interest of it is that nobody yet knew what
they were.

KEEP AS HIS CLAIMS, unhedged, with no editorial wink:

  - THE AETHER. Light is a wave in the aether; the aether is real and its
    rigidity and density set the speed of light. Stated as fact, kept as
    fact. (Same rule as fleming/, where it is the whole book.)
  - X-RAYS ARE NOT LIGHT. Thompson reports Röntgen's own "No" to the
    question "Is it light?", and his own speculation that they may be
    longitudinal waves in the aether, or a train of single independent
    pulses. Both are wrong. Both stay, in his voice, as his reasoning.
  - The three-colour theory of vision, the "physiological" account of
    complementary tints, the velocity of light as 186,400 miles a second,
    the frequency of the D line as 508.8 billions per second: all as
    printed.
  - Do NOT annotate with modern physics. No square-bracket corrections,
    no "we now know". The reader is being shown 1897 and should see it.

MODERNIZE SILENTLY, because they are vocabulary and not claims:

  - kathode -> cathode; kathode rays -> cathode rays (electrons, but he
    does not know that, so do not say so)
  - Röntgen light / Röntgen rays / X-rays: Thompson uses all three. Keep
    "Röntgen rays" as his primary term — it is the point of the lecture
    that the discovery is one year old and still carries its discoverer's
    name — and let "X-rays" stand where he uses it.
  - reflexion -> reflection; connexion -> connection; inflexion ->
    inflection
  - dioptrie -> dioptre (the modern British spelling of his unit); note
    the scan gives both "dioptrie" and "dioptries"
  - "electric pressure" -> keep, he glosses it himself as voltage
  - vibratory, luminiferous, calorific: modernize where they are simply
    old words for a plain thing
  - "billions" means 10^12 throughout (the British long scale). Keep the
    NUMBER as printed and the word "billions"; he defines it himself.
  - Wurzburg -> Würzburg; Rontgen -> Röntgen (prep.py has already done
    most of this)

KEEP UNCHANGED because they are load-bearing terms he reasons with:

  - "velocity-constant" (his own coinage, the reciprocal of the refractive
    index, and the whole of Appendix I turns on it)
  - "sagitta" (he explains why he prefers it to "versed sine")
  - "luminescence", "fluorescence", "phosphorescence" — his taxonomy in
    Lecture Four is the modern one and the terms are exact
  - "calc-spar", "tourmaline", "selenite", "Iceland spar" — real
    materials, still called that
  - "Geissler tube", "Crookes's tube", "Nicol prism", "bolometer",
    "thermopile" — the instruments by name

5. THE PLATES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

127 plates, recovered from the page scans — see FIGURES.md, which is a
study in itself. They ride as `[Figure N]` markers and MUST come back as
`[Figure N: caption]`, exactly the same set, in the same order.

CAPTIONS ARE NEW WRITING and they are the single biggest improvement over
the original, which captions every plate "FIG. 84." and nothing more.
Write a descriptive one-liner for each from the adjacent prose. Look at
what the prose says the plate shows before writing the caption.

TWENTY-ONE FIGURES HAVE NO PLATE. Their numbers are

    8, 13, 22, 36, 64, 65, 73, 96, 103, 108, 123, 131, 134, 135,
    150, 151, 152, 153, 156, 157

(Fig. 63, the refractive-index chart, WAS recovered late and does have a
plate.) Where the text says "see Fig. 8", DESCRIBE what the reader is
meant to see instead of sending them to a picture that is not there.
Never invent a `[Figure]` marker for a number in that list.

Compound plates take hyphenated ids — `[Figure 121-122]` — and their
caption must name every figure on the block, in order, saying which is
which (left/right/above/below). The prose refers to them separately and
the reader has only one image in which to find them.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. WHAT THIS SOURCE IS, AND WHY IT MATTERS TO THE TRANSLATOR
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

THIS IS THE PROJECT'S FIRST OCR SOURCE. There is no Gutenberg edition and
no Standard Ebooks edition of this book; the text comes from a scan of a
copy in the University of California library, and prep.py has spent a long
time repairing it: running heads pulled out of mid-sentence, ~330
paragraphs mended where the page break cut them in half, 78 footnotes
lifted from where the page-bottom dropped them, every stacked fraction
read back off the page image.

For the translator this means one thing: THE TEXT YOU ARE GIVEN IS CLEAN
BUT NOT PERFECT. If a sentence does not parse, it is more likely a scanner
artifact than a difficult 1897 construction. Read through it to the sense
and render the sense. If a NUMBER looks wrong, flag it in your notes
rather than silently repairing it — the numbers have all been checked
against the page images and a surprise there is worth knowing about.

Two known survivals: occasional single-letter debris from figures set INTO
the text (a stray "O|" or "B" where a diagram's labels landed in the
prose), and a very occasional dropped word. Drop the debris; supply the
obvious word.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. THE FOOTNOTES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

78 of them, all Thompson's own, and they arrive as paragraphs beginning
"Footnote: ". They are real content — the invention of the Joly
photometer, the Board of Trade standard candle, references to Tait and to
Wiedemann's Annalen — and they are translated in the same voice as the
body, because they are the same man.

Render each as its own paragraph beginning "Footnote: " and keep it where
it is. Do not fold a footnote into the sentence that cites it; do not drop
one because it is only a reference.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. THE MATHEMATICS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Appendices I, II, III and V carry real mathematics, and every displayed
formula was transcribed from the page image and set as an indented block:

    F = (R₁ − R₂)(1 − h)/h                      [10]

KEEP EVERY SUCH BLOCK EXACTLY AS IT STANDS, indentation and all, and keep
the bracketed formula numbers — the prose refers to them by number ("the
general formula [10] for the power of any lens"). Do not re-derive, do not
simplify, do not convert to modern notation. Thompson's whole argument in
Appendix I is that HIS notation is better than the Cambridge one, and he
prints both side by side to prove it.

The same goes for the notation table in Appendix I and the wave-length
table in Appendix IV, both of which are indented blocks. Reproduce them as
tables, with the columns aligned. You may modernize the column HEADINGS
into plain modern English; do not touch a single figure inside.

ONE CORRECTION HAS BEEN MADE AND IS FLAGGED HERE: in the wave-length
table, the A line's wave-length in millionths of an inch is printed 29.28,
where 75.94 micro-centimetres divided by 2.54 gives 29.90. Every other one
of the 55 rows checks out to within a rounding step. The frequency cell
(395.2) confirms which of the two is the misprint. The table as you receive
it reads 29.90.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. HANDLING NOTES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

THE INTERVIEW. Lecture Six opens with a long verbatim quotation of an
interview with Röntgen from McClure's Magazine — question and answer,
several pages of it. Set it as dialogue, keep the quotation marks, keep
every exchange. It is the best thing in the book: a reporter asking the
discoverer "Is it light?" and being told "No."

THE MAGIC MIRROR. Lecture One ends with Japanese magic mirrors and English
imitations of them. Thompson is admiring, not exoticising; keep it that
way, and keep his explanation of how the trick works.

THE BUTTERFLY, THE SPECTRE AND THE HAND. The demonstrations are theatre —
a skeleton hand photographed through flesh, a ghost thrown on a screen, a
butterfly in three colours. Let them be theatre.

X-RAY SAFETY. Nobody in 1896 knew that X-rays were dangerous, and
Thompson cheerfully photographs his own hand and invites the audience to
look into the fluoroscope. Do NOT add a warning and do NOT soften it. This
is a historical document; the reader can be trusted with the date.

CHILDREN. The audience is children, and Thompson addresses them directly
and without condescension. Keep every "you can try this at home", every
"I want you to notice", every direct question. Where he says "you boys" or
similar, keep the address but there is no need to preserve the assumption
that the audience is only boys — "you" alone does the work.
