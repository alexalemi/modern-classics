Wilhelm Röntgen announced the discovery of X-rays on 28 December 1895. Silvanus P. Thompson began this course of six Christmas lectures at the Royal Institution on 29 December 1896, thirteen months later, and gave the whole of the last one to the new rays, quoting Röntgen's own account of the discovery from an interview published a few months before. A reporter asks whether the rays are light. No, says Röntgen. Is it electricity? Not in any known form. What is it? I do not know. Nothing else in this collection is this close to the event it describes, and Thompson's own guess about what the rays might be is left standing as he made it.

Thompson was professor of physics at Finsbury Technical College, a Quaker, a teacher by vocation, and the author of the textbook every engineering student of the period owned. Among the Royal Institution lecturers in this collection he is the reformer. He thought optics was taught wrongly, and he throws out rays and angles of incidence on the first page in favour of waves in a tank of water, saying plainly that the orthodox method is fundamentally wrong. He also thought that hard words, not hard ideas, are what make science look difficult, and he says so in the middle of the third lecture: scientific men often fall into the habit of using long and difficult words to express very simple and easy ideas.

So light is taught here as something you can watch. Ripples spreading in a tank. Shadows with fringed edges. A lens of air that magnifies when you drop it into oil. A Japanese bronze mirror that throws the pattern cast on its back onto the wall. A white swan that turns black when a crystal is rotated. Then he goes past both ends of the spectrum: the ultra-violet that fogs a photographic plate in the dark, the infra-red that lights a cigar at the focus of a mirror, Hertz's waves ten feet long, and at last the light that shows you the bones of your own hand. Near the end he stops lecturing to complain that Würzburg, a town of 61,000 people, supports a university on £41,000 a year while London, with five million, has a mere examining board. That passage is here in full.

No transcription of this book existed, so the text was read off page scans of the 1897 edition, and the work of repairing the scan was most of the work of the edition. All 127 surviving plates have been recut from those scans and cleaned, and every one carries a descriptive caption written for this edition. One genuine error in Thompson's printing — a single wrong figure in his table of wave-lengths, which the table's own arithmetic identifies — is corrected with an editor's note. The aether, which he treats as real and obvious throughout, is not corrected anywhere.
