Ovid was born in 43 BC, a year after Julius Caesar was killed, and was the most successful poet in Rome by his thirties. He was finishing the Metamorphoses around AD 8 when Augustus banished him to Tomis, a garrison town on the Black Sea at the edge of the empire. He never came back and died there. He said afterwards that the causes were a poem and a mistake, and he never said what the mistake was. He also said that he burned his own copy of this poem before he left; other copies were already in circulation, which is why it exists.

It is fifteen books and some two hundred and fifty stories, running from the creation of the world to the death of Julius Caesar, and every one of them turns on somebody changing shape. Girls become trees, birds, springs and stones; men become wolves, stags and statues; gods become bulls and showers of gold in order to get at women. Ovid called it one continuous song and wrote it as one: the tales hand off to each other, characters tell stories inside stories, and it is meant to be read forwards rather than looked up.

This is where most of what English readers know as Greek myth actually comes from, at second and third hand. Arthur Golding's English version of 1567 is the one Shakespeare used. A copy of Ovid is carried onto the stage in Titus Andronicus and the plot turns on the story of Philomela; Pyramus and Thisbe is the play the workmen perform in A Midsummer Night's Dream. Narcissus at the pool, Midas and the golden touch, Icarus flying too near the sun, Pygmalion falling in love with his own statue, Daphne turning into a laurel as Apollo's hands close on her — the versions that got passed down are his, and so is most of what is hanging in the painting galleries. The picture on the cover of this edition is Apollo and Daphne.

One thing is worth knowing before you start. A great many of the transformations begin with a god pursuing a woman who does not want him, and the poem contains a great deal of rape. Ovid tells some of it with real horror and terror, and some of it briskly and in passing, and the difference between those two is part of what the poem is. It is translated here with gravity, at full length, neither sensationalised nor cut.

The translation is from the Latin, complete, and it is prose: Latin hexameter has no English equivalent that does not end in padding.
