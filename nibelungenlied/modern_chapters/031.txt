Chapter 32: How Blödel Was Killed

Blödel's warriors were all ready. A thousand coats of mail
strong, they made their way to where Dankwart sat at table with the
squires. There the deadliest hatred of all broke out between heroes.
When Lord Blödel walked up to the tables, Dankwart the marshal
greeted him with every courtesy: "Welcome to this house, my lord
Blödel. Truly, your coming surprises me. What is this about?"

"You need not greet me," said Blödel, "for my coming here means
your end. Because of your brother Hagen, who killed Siegfried, you
will pay for it here among the Huns, and plenty of other warriors
with you."

"No, Lord Blödel," said Dankwart. "If that is so, we may soon
regret this journey to court. I was a small child when Siegfried lost
his life. I do not know what King Etzel's wife holds against me."

"I have nothing more to tell you about it. Your kinsmen Gunther
and Hagen did the deed. Now defend yourselves, you men far from home;
you cannot survive. Your lives are the forfeit Kriemhild demands."

"Since you will not turn back," said Dankwart, "I am sorry I
pleaded with you. I should have saved my breath." The swift, bold
warrior sprang up from the table and drew a sharp weapon, big and
long. He struck Blödel a swift sword-stroke, and in a moment
Blödel's head lay at his feet. "Let that be your morning gift," said
Dankwart the warrior, "for Nudung's bride, the one you wanted to take
in love. Tomorrow they can marry her to some other man, and if
he wants the bride-price, he will get the same." A very loyal Hun had
told him what great wrongs the queen was plotting against them.

When Blödel's men saw their lord lying dead, they would not
stand for it from the guests any longer. With raised swords they rushed
at the young men in a grim fury. Many of them came to regret it later.

Dankwart shouted to the whole company: "You can see, noble
squires, how this is going to go. Now defend yourselves, you men far
from home! Truly, we have no choice, however kindly noble Kriemhild
invited us here."

Those who had no swords reached down in front of the benches and
lifted many a long footstool by its legs. The Burgundian squires were
not going to put up with it, and with the heavy stools they beat many
a dent through a helmet. How fiercely those young strangers
defended themselves! At last they drove the armed men out of the
house, but five hundred of them or more stayed behind inside, dead. The
company was red and wet with blood.

The grim news was carried to Etzel's warriors. It was bitter
grief to them that Blödel and his men had been killed, and that Hagen's
brother and the squires had done it. Before the queen even heard
of it, the Huns in their hatred armed two thousand men or more. They
went for the squires — there was no stopping it now — and left not one
of that company alive.

The treacherous Huns brought a great army up before the house.
The squires, far from home, stood their ground well; but what good was
their quick courage? They had to lie dead. And within a short while
after that, a terrible calamity began. Here you can hear
wonders told of a monstrous thing: nine thousand squires lay dead, and
with them twelve knights, Dankwart's own men. He was seen standing all
alone among the enemy.

The uproar had died down, the din had gone quiet, when Dankwart
the warrior looked back over his shoulder. "Alas for the friends I have
lost," he said. "Now, to my grief, I must stand alone among my
enemies."

The swords fell thick and fast on his one body; later many a
hero's wife would weep for it. He raised his shield higher and lowered
the strap, and he made many a mail-ring run wet with blood. "Oh,
the sorrow of it," said Aldrian's son. "Now give way, you Hunnish
warriors. Let me out into the wind, so the air can cool a man worn out
with fighting." And then they saw the warrior walk out, every inch a
lord.

As the battle-weary man sprang out of the house, how many fresh
swords rang on his helmet! Those who had not seen what wonders his hand
could do came rushing at the man from Burgundy. "I wish to
God," said Dankwart, "that I had a messenger who could let my
brother Hagen know that I am standing in such peril in front of these
warriors. He would get me out of here, or lie dead beside me."

The Hunnish warriors said: "You will have to be the messenger
yourself, when we carry you dead to your brother. Then Gunther's vassal
will know grief for the first time. You have done King Etzel great harm
here."

"Stop your threatening," he said, "and stand farther back. I
will make the mail wet on a few more of you yet. I will carry the news
to court myself, and I will tell my lords my great trouble."

He made himself so feared among Etzel's men that they dared not
face him with swords. Instead they shot so many spears into his shield
that for sheer weight he had to let it fall from his hand. Now
that he carried no shield, they thought they would overpower him. Hey,
what deep wounds he struck through their helmets! Many a bold man had
to stagger before him, and strong Dankwart won great praise for it.

They sprang at him from both sides, and truly, every one of them
came into that fight too soon. He went before his enemies like a wild boar
heading for the woods in front of the hounds. How could a man be
braver? His path was wet all over again with hot blood. No
single warrior could ever have fought better against his enemies than
he did. And so they saw Hagen's brother walk to court like a lord.

The stewards and cupbearers heard the clang of swords. Many of
them flung down the drink from their hands, and the dishes they were
carrying to court. In front of the stairs he met plenty of strong
enemies. "What is this, you stewards?" said the weary warrior.
"You should be looking after the guests, and carrying good food to the
lords, and letting me tell my lords the news as it really is."

Whoever was brave enough to spring at him on the stairs, he
dealt some of them such a grim sword-swing that out of fear they had to
back up higher. His great courage had worked great wonders that day.
