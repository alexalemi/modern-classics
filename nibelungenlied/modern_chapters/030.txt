Chapter 31: How the Lords Went to Church

"My coat of mail is growing cold," said Volker. "I think the night
is almost over. I can feel it in the air: it will soon be day." Then
they woke the many men who still lay sleeping.

The bright morning shone into the hall on the guests. Hagen went
about waking the knights, asking whether they wanted to go to the
cathedral for Mass. The bells were already ringing, in the Christian
way. Christians and pagans sang differently there, and that
showed clearly enough: they were not of one mind. Gunther's men wanted
to go to church, and they had all risen from their beds together.

The warriors laced themselves into such fine clothes that no
heroes ever brought better ones into any king's land. That troubled
Hagen. He said: "Heroes, you ought to be wearing other clothes here.
You know the news well enough by now. Instead of roses, carry
weapons in your hands; instead of jeweled garlands, your bright good
helmets, since we know only too well what wicked Kriemhild has in mind.
We will have to fight today, I tell you. Instead of silk shirts,
wear your coats of mail; instead of rich cloaks, your good broad
shields, so that if anyone turns on you in anger, you are fully armed.

"My dear lords, and my kinsmen and vassals, go to church of your
own free will, and lay your worry and your peril before almighty God;
and know for certain that death is drawing near us. Do not forget
whatever you have done, either, and stand before God with all your
heart. I warn you of this, noble warriors: unless God in heaven wills
otherwise, you will never hear Mass again."

So the princes and their men went to the cathedral. In the holy
churchyard bold Hagen made them halt, so that they would not get
separated. He said: "No one knows yet what will happen to us at the
hands of the Huns. Set your shields down in front of your feet,
my friends, and if anyone offers you a cold greeting, pay him back with
deep and deadly wounds. That is Hagen's advice: that you are found
standing as your honor demands."

Volker and Hagen, the two of them, went and stood in front of
the wide cathedral door. They did it so that the king's wife would have
to push past them; truly, she was in a grim mood. Then the lord
of the land came, and his beautiful wife with him, her body decked in
rich clothes, and many swift warriors could be seen walking with her.
You could see the dust rising high from Kriemhild's company.

When the mighty king saw the kings and their followers armed
like that, how quickly he spoke: "Why do I see my friends going about
in helmets? On my loyalty, it grieves me if anyone has done them any
harm. I will gladly make it good, however they think best. If
anyone has weighed down their hearts or their spirits, I will show them
plainly that it grieves me very much. Whatever they ask of me, I am
ready to do it all."

Hagen answered: "No one has done us any harm. It is my lords'
custom to go armed at every festival for three full days. If anyone did
anything to us here, we would tell Etzel."

Kriemhild heard very well what Hagen said. How full of hate was
the look she gave him from under her brows! But she would not reveal
the custom of her own land, though she had known it long enough in
Burgundy. However grim and strong her hatred of them, if anyone
had told Etzel the truth of the matter, he would surely have prevented
what happened there later. Out of their great arrogance, not one of
them told him.

A great crowd went past with the queen, but those two would not
step aside for them by so much as two hands' breadth. That annoyed the
Huns: they had to shove their way past the proud heroes. Etzel's
chamberlains did not like it at all. They would have liked to pick a
quarrel with the warriors, but they did not dare in front of the noble
king. So there was a great deal of shoving, and nothing more.

When they had served God and were ready to leave, many a Hun
hurried to his horse. Many beautiful girls stood beside Kriemhild, and
a good seven thousand warriors rode with the queen. Kriemhild
sat down at the windows with her ladies, beside mighty Etzel; he was
glad of that. They wanted to watch the proud heroes ride. Hey, what a
crowd of foreign warriors rode in the courtyard before them!

The marshal had come too, with the squires: bold Dankwart had
brought his lord's household with him from the land of Burgundy. The
horses were found well saddled for the bold Nibelungs. When the
kings and their men had reached their horses, strong Volker advised
them to ride a buhurt after the custom of their own land. Later the
heroes rode it magnificently. They did not mind at all what the
hero had advised. The buhurt and the noise of it both grew loud; many a
man came out into the wide courtyard, and Etzel and Kriemhild watched
it themselves.

Six hundred of Dietrich's warriors rode into the buhurt to face
the guests. They wanted some sport with the Burgundians, and if he had
allowed it they would gladly have had it. Hey, what good warriors
came riding after them! But Lord Dietrich was told of it, and he forbade
them the game with Gunther's men. He was afraid for his men, and he had
good reason to be.

When the men of Bern had ridden off, Rüdiger's men came from
Pöchlarn, five hundred of them under their shields, riding in front of
the hall. The margrave would have been glad if they had let it be.
He rode to them wisely through the throng and told his warriors
to be aware that Gunther's men were in a dangerous temper; if they left
the buhurt alone, he would take it kindly.

When those proud heroes had parted from them, the men of
Thuringia came, as we have been told, and the men of Denmark, a good
thousand bold men. You could see the splinters of broken lances flying
from the thrusts. Irnfried and Hawart rode into the buhurt. The
men from the Rhine were waiting for them proudly, and they offered the
Thuringians many a joust. Many a lordly shield-rim was pierced through
by their thrusts.

Then Lord Blödel came with three thousand. Etzel and Kriemhild
watched him closely, for the knightly sport was taking place right in
front of them both. The queen watched it gladly, hoping for harm to the
Burgundians. Schrutan and Gibeche, Ramung and Hornboge rode into
the buhurt in the Hunnish style. They held their ground against the
heroes from the land of Burgundy, and the lance-shafts whirled high
over the wall of the king's hall.

Whatever anyone did there, it was nothing but noise. You could
hear the great hall and the palace ringing loudly with the shields
Gunther's men struck. His household won praise there with great honor.
Their sport was so hard and so long that the white sweat ran out
from under the trappings of the good horses the heroes rode. They tried
their luck against the Huns in a very high-handed way.

Then the bold warrior Volker the minstrel said: "I do not think
these warriors dare to stand against us. I have always heard it said
that they hate us; truly, it could never come about better for them
than now. Have the horses led to our lodgings," Volker went on,
"and let us ride again toward evening, when the time comes. Perhaps the
queen will give the prize to the Burgundians."

Then they saw a knight riding up so showily that none of all the
Huns could match him. He surely must have had a sweetheart at the time;
he was dressed as finely as a noble knight's bride. Volker said
again: "How could I let that pass? That ladies' darling is going to get
a knock. No one can stop it: it will cost him his life. I do not care
if King Etzel's wife is angry about it."

"No, for my sake," the king said at once. "People will blame us
if we attack them. Let the Huns begin it; that will suit us far
better." King Etzel was still sitting beside the queen.

"I will add to the buhurt," said Hagen. "Let the ladies and the
warriors see how we can ride; that is a good thing to do. They will give
no prize to King Gunther's men anyway."

Swift Volker rode back into the buhurt. Later that brought many
a woman great grief: he drove his spear through the rich Hun's body.
Both maid and wife were seen weeping over it later. Hagen and his
men, sixty of his warriors, came riding hard after the fiddler, to
where the game was being played. Etzel and Kriemhild saw it quite
plainly. The three kings would not leave their minstrel
unguarded among the enemy. A thousand heroes rode there with great
force; they did what they wanted, in their high-handed way.

When the rich Hun was struck dead, you could hear his kinsmen
shouting and lamenting. The whole household cried out, "Who did this?"
and the answer came: "The fiddler did it, Volker, the bold minstrel."
The margrave's kinsmen from the land of the Huns called at once
for swords and shields; they wanted to strike Volker dead. The host
came hurrying down from a window as fast as he could.

An uproar rose from the people on every side. The kings and
their followers dismounted in front of the hall, and the Burgundians
pushed their horses to the rear. Then King Etzel came, and the lord set
about parting them. He wrenched a strong weapon out of the hand
of one of the Hun's kinsmen whom he found near him, and drove them all
back, for he was very angry. "How I would have wasted all my service
on these heroes if you killed this minstrel here beside me,"
said King Etzel. "That would be a crime. I saw very well how he rode
when he ran the Hun through: it happened by a stumble, through no fault
of his. You must leave my guests in peace." So he became their
escort. The horses were led away to the lodgings; they had many
squires there, ready and eager for every kind of service.

The host went into the great hall with his friends, and he let
no more anger break out there. Then the tables were set and water was
brought for them. The men from the Rhine had plenty of strong enemies
in that room.

It was a long while before the lords sat down. Kriemhild's worry
was pressing her too hard. She said: "Prince of Bern, I come to you for
advice, for help and favor. My affairs stand in a fearful state."

Hildebrand, that praiseworthy warrior, answered her: "Whoever
strikes at the Nibelungs does it without me, whatever treasure he is
offered for it. He may come to regret it: those swift proud knights are
still unbeaten."

Then Lord Dietrich said, with his courteous manners: "Let that
request be, mighty queen. Your kinsmen have done me no such wrong that I
should want to fight those bold warriors. The request does you
little honor, noble prince's wife, that you plot against your own
kinsmen's lives. They came into this land trusting in good faith.
Siegfried will not be avenged by Dietrich's hand."

When she found no treachery in the man of Bern, she quickly
promised into Blödel's hand a rich march that Nudung had once held.
Later Dankwart killed him, so that he quite forgot the gift. She
said: "You must help me, Lord Blödel. My enemies are in this house, the
men who killed Siegfried, my dear husband. Whoever helps me avenge that,
I will be in his debt forever."

Blödel answered her: "Lady, you should know that I dare not
advise any hatred against them, for fear of Etzel. He is very glad to
see your kinsmen here. If I did them any harm, the king would never
forgive me."

"Not so, Lord Blödel; I will always be your friend. I will give
you silver and gold as your reward, and a beautiful girl, Nudung's
bride, so that you can take your pleasure in that lovely body of hers.
The land and the castles, I will give you all of it, so that you
can live in joy forever, noble knight, if you win the march where
Nudung sat. Whatever I promise you today, I will keep in loyalty."

When Lord Blödel heard the reward, and that the lady would suit
him well for her beauty, he thought to serve the lovely woman by
fighting. For that the warrior had to lose his life. He said to
the queen: "Go back into the hall. Before anyone notices, I will raise
an uproar. Hagen shall pay for what he did to you; I will hand King
Gunther's man over to you bound."

"Now arm yourselves," said Blödel, "all my men. We are going to
the enemy in their lodgings. Etzel's wife will not let me out of this,
and so we heroes must all put our lives at stake."

When the queen had left Blödel set on fighting, she went to the
table with King Etzel and his men. She had laid savage plans against
the guests.

Since the fight could be started in no other way — Kriemhild's
old grief lay buried in her heart — she had Etzel's son carried to the
table. How could a woman ever do anything more terrible for revenge?
Etzel's men went at once and carried Ortlieb, the young king, to
the princes' table, where Hagen was sitting too. Because of that the
child had to die, through Hagen's murderous hatred.

When the mighty king saw his son, he spoke kindly to his wife's
kinsmen: "Now look, my friends: this is my only son, and your sister's
too. That may be a good thing for all of you. If he takes after
his family, he will grow into a bold man, rich and very noble, strong
and handsome. If I live a while yet, I will give him twelve lands; then
noble Ortlieb's hand may serve you well. So I ask you gladly,
my dear friends: when you ride home to your lands on the Rhine, take
your sister's son with you, and treat the child with great kindness,
and bring him up in honor until he becomes a man. If anyone in
your lands has done you any wrong, he will help you avenge it, once he
is grown." Kriemhild, King Etzel's wife, heard those words too.

"These warriors might well put their trust in him," said Hagen,
"if he grew to be a man. But the young king looks so marked for death
that you will seldom see me going to court to wait on Ortlieb."

The king looked at Hagen; the words grieved him. Though the
noble prince said nothing about it, it troubled his heart and weighed
on his spirit. Hagen was in no mood for amusement. It pained all
the princes, and the king with them, that Hagen had spoken that way
about his child. That they had to put up with it sat badly with them.
They did not yet know what would happen to them later through that
warrior.
