Chapter 30: How the Kings Went to Bed, and What Happened in the Night

The day was over now and night was coming on. The travel-weary
warriors had one worry on their minds: when they could rest and go to
their beds. Hagen raised the matter, and they were soon given their
answer.

Gunther said to the host: "God keep you well. We want to go to
sleep now; please give us your leave. Whenever you command it, we will
come back early tomorrow." Etzel parted from his guests in the best of
spirits.

Everywhere you looked, the guests were being jostled. Bold
Volker said to the Huns: "How dare you get under these warriors' feet?
If you do not stop, you will get hurt. I will give one of you
such a heavy stroke of my fiddle bow that if he has a loyal friend
anywhere, that friend will have cause to weep for it. Why not get out
of our way, warriors? That seems the wise thing to me. You are all
called warriors, but you are not all of the same mettle."

When the fiddler spoke so angrily, bold Hagen looked back over
his shoulder. "The bold minstrel is giving you good advice," he said.
"Go to your lodgings, you heroes of Kriemhild. What you have in
mind, I do not think any of you will do. If you want to start
something, come to us early tomorrow, and tonight let us strangers
have our rest. I do not think heroes ever went about things with
intentions like yours."

Then the guests were brought into a wide hall. They found it
made ready for the warriors from end to end with rich beds, long and
broad. Lady Kriemhild was plotting the very worst of harm against
them. You could see many a finely worked quilt from Arras there,
of bright silk, and many a bedspread of Arabian silk, the best there
could be, with borders on them that shone splendidly. Many
coverlets of ermine and of black sable could be seen, and under them
the men were to take their rest that night until the bright day came.
No king with his household ever lay down so splendidly.

"Alas for this night's lodging," said young Giselher, "and alas
for my kinsmen who have come here with us. However kindly my sister
has received us, I am afraid that through her we shall all lie dead."

"Put your worries aside now," said Hagen the warrior. "I will
keep the shield-watch myself tonight. I am confident I can guard us
well until day comes. Have no fear on that score; after that, let
whoever can save himself."

They all bowed to him and thanked him for it. Then they went to
their beds, and it was not long before the handsome men had lain
down. Bold Hagen, the hero, began to arm himself.

Then Volker the fiddler, the warrior, said: "If it is not
beneath you, Hagen, I would like to keep the shield-watch with you
tonight, until early morning." The hero thanked Volker warmly.
"God in heaven reward you, dear Volker. In all my troubles I
would want no one but you alone, wherever I was in peril. I will
repay you well for it, unless death prevents me."

Then the two of them put on their bright armor. Each took his
shield in his hand, and they went out of the house and stood before
the door. There they kept watch over the guests; that was done out of
loyalty. Swift Volker laid his good shield down against the
wall of the hall. Then he went back inside and fetched his fiddle, and
served his friends as well suited the hero. He sat down on the
stone step in the doorway of the house. A bolder fiddler there never
was. When the music of his strings rang out so sweetly, the proud
warriors, so far from home, thanked Volker for it.

His strings rang until the whole house echoed with them; his
strength and his skill were both great. Then he played sweeter and
sweeter, and lulled many an anxious man to sleep in his bed.
When they had fallen asleep and he could see it, the warrior
took up his shield again in his hand, went back to the room and stood
in front of the door, and guarded the men far from home against
Kriemhild's men.

Toward the middle of the night — I do not know, it may have
been earlier — bold Volker saw a helmet gleam far off in the dark.
Kriemhild's men would gladly have done the guests harm. The
fiddler said: "Hagen, my friend, this is a worry we should carry
together. I see armed men standing near the house, and if I am not
mistaken, I think they mean to attack us."

"Then keep quiet," said Hagen, "and let them come closer to us
before they notice us. Then helmets will be knocked askew by the
swords in our two hands, and they will be sent back to Kriemhild in a
sorry state."

One of the Huns' warriors saw very quickly that the door was
guarded. How fast he spoke then: "What we had in mind cannot be done.
I see the fiddler standing on the shield-watch. He wears a
gleaming helmet on his head, bright and hard, strong and whole, and
the rings of his mail shine like fire. Hagen is standing beside him.
The guests are well guarded."

They turned back at once. When Volker saw it, he spoke angrily
to his companion: "Now let me go out to those warriors in front of
the house. I want to ask Lady Kriemhild's men a question or two."

"No, for my sake," said Hagen. "If you leave the house, those
swift warriors will very likely bring you into such peril with their
swords that I would have to help you, even if it meant the death of
all my kinsmen. And once the two of us were caught up in the
fight, two or four of them would run into the house in no time and do
such harm to the sleeping warriors that we could never grieve for it
enough."

Volker answered: "Then at least let us make sure they know I
have seen them, so that Kriemhild's men cannot deny they would gladly
have done a treacherous thing." And at once Volker called out
to them: "Why are you walking about in armor like that, you swift
warriors? Are you riding out to rob somebody, you men of Kriemhild?
Then you should take me and my comrade along to help."

No one answered him. He was furious. "Shame on you, you
miserable cowards!" said the good hero. "Did you mean to murder us in
our sleep? That has very seldom been done to heroes as good as these."

The queen was told exactly how her messengers had come back
with nothing done. She had good reason to be sorry, and she arranged
it another way, for her mood was grim. Because of that, bold good
heroes would die in the end.
