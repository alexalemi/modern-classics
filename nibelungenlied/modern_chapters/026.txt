Chapter 27: How Rüdiger Welcomed the Kings to His House

Then the margrave went to find the ladies, his wife and his
daughter, and told them at once the very welcome news he had heard:
their lady's brothers were coming to their house. "My dearest
wife," said Rüdiger, "you must give the noble kings a very warm welcome
when they come to court here with their retinue. And you must greet
Hagen, Gunther's vassal, handsomely too. With them comes a man
named Dankwart, and another named Volker, a man of fine manners. You and
my daughter are to kiss these six, and treat the warriors with courtesy
and kindness."

The ladies promised, and they were ready to do it. They searched
the chests for the splendid clothes they would wear to go out and meet
the warriors. There was a great deal of effort made by beautiful women
that day. You would have found very little false color on their
cheeks. On their heads they wore bright bands of gold, rich circlets, so
that the wind would not tangle their beautiful hair; that is true, on my
honor.

Let us leave the ladies to their busy work. There was a great
hurrying across the fields by Rüdiger's kinsmen to where the princes
were, and the princes were well received into the margrave's land.
When the margrave saw them coming toward him, swift Rüdiger said
joyfully: "Welcome, my lords, and your men too, here in my land. I am
very glad to see you." The warriors bowed to him in loyalty,
without a trace of ill will. He showed them plainly that he wished them
well. He greeted Hagen specially, for he had known him before, and he
did the same for Volker from the land of Burgundy. He welcomed
Dankwart too. The bold warrior said: "Since you are taking us in, who is
going to look after the retinue we have brought with us?"

The margrave said: "You shall have a good night, and so shall
your whole retinue. Whatever you have brought with you into this land,
horses and clothing, I will set such a guard on it that nothing is lost
that could do you harm, not so much as a single spur. Pitch the
tents on the field, squires. Whatever you lose here, I will make it
good. Take off the bridles and let the horses run." No host had ever
done that for them before.

The guests were glad of it. When all that was arranged, the lords
rode on, and the squires lay down everywhere on the grass and took their
ease. I think they never fared so comfortably on this whole journey.

The noble margravine had come out in front of the castle with her
very beautiful daughter. Beside her stood the lovely ladies and many a
beautiful girl, wearing many arm-rings and splendid clothes. The
precious stones shone far out of their rich garments; they were
beautiful women. Then the guests came and dismounted at once. Hey, what
fine courtesy was found in those Burgundians! Thirty-six girls
and many other women, their bodies made as anyone could wish, went to
meet them with many a bold man. There was a beautiful greeting given by
noble ladies.

The young margravine kissed all three kings, and so did her
mother. Hagen was standing right there. Her father told her to kiss him;
she looked at him, and he seemed so fearsome that she would gladly have
left it undone. But she had to do what the host commanded. Her
color came and went, pale and red. She kissed Dankwart too, and then the
minstrel; that greeting was given him for his courage. The young
margravine took Giselher, the warrior from the land of Burgundy, by the
hand, and her mother did the same with Gunther, the bold man. They went
in with the heroes in high spirits. The host walked beside Gernot
into a wide hall, and knights and ladies sat down there. Good wine was
quickly ordered poured for the guests. Truly, heroes could never have
been better treated.

Rüdiger's daughter was looked at with loving eyes, she was so
lovely. Truly, many a good knight loved her in his thoughts, and she
deserved it, for she was high-spirited. They thought whatever
they liked, but nothing could come of it. Back and forth went many a
look at the girls and the ladies, and there were plenty of them sitting
there. The noble fiddler felt kindly toward the host.

Then, as the custom was, they parted; knights and ladies went
their separate ways. Tables were set up in the wide hall, and from then
on the foreign guests were splendidly served. For the guests'
sake the noble margravine came to table, but her daughter she left with
the girls, where it was proper for her to sit. The guests could not see
her, and truly that irked them.

When they had all drunk and eaten, the beautiful ladies were
brought back into the hall, and no playful talk was left unsaid. Volker,
a bold and cheerful warrior, spoke a great deal of it. The noble
minstrel said out loud: "Mighty margrave, God has dealt very graciously
with you: he has given you a wife so truly beautiful, and a delightful
life along with her. If I were a prince," said the minstrel, "and
was to wear a crown, I would want your beautiful daughter for my wife;
that is what my heart wishes. She is lovely to look at, and noble and
good besides."

The margrave said: "How could that ever be, that a king would
want my dear daughter? My wife and I are exiles here. What good is great
beauty to the girl?"

Gernot, the well-bred man, answered: "If I could have a wife
after my own heart, I would always be glad of such a woman." And Hagen
answered very kindly: "Now my lord Giselher really must take a
wife. The margravine's family is so high that I and all his men would
gladly serve her if she were to wear the crown in Burgundy."

That speech seemed very good to Rüdiger, and to Gotelind too;
truly their hearts rejoiced. Then the heroes brought it about that noble
Giselher took her as his wife, as well befitted a king. What is
meant to happen, who can stand in its way? They asked the young lady to
come to court. They swore to give him the delightful woman, and he in
turn promised to love the lovely girl. Castles and land were
assigned to the young lady; the noble king's hand swore oaths on it, and
Lord Gernot's too, that it would be done.

The margrave said: "Since I have no castles, I will always be
loyally devoted to you. With my daughter I give silver and gold, as much
as a hundred pack-horses can carry at the most, so that the hero's
kinsmen may be pleased with it, as honor requires."

Then, as the custom was, they had the two of them stand inside a
ring. Many a young man stood facing them in a cheerful mood, thinking in
his mind what the young still like to think. When they began to
ask the lovely girl whether she would have the warrior, it was partly
painful to her, and yet she meant to take the handsome man. The question
embarrassed her, as it has embarrassed many a girl. Her father
Rüdiger advised her to say yes and to take him gladly. In a moment noble
Giselher was there and put his white arms around her, though in the
end she had little joy of him.

The margrave said: "You noble and mighty kings, when you ride
back home to Burgundy, as is the custom, I will give you my child so you
can take her with you." They promised that. Whatever merriment
there was, they had to let it go. The young lady was sent to her chamber
and the guests to sleep and rest until day. Then food was prepared; the
host looked after them kindly.

When they had eaten, they wanted to ride on toward the land of
the Huns. "I will put a stop to that," said the noble host. "You must
stay here, for I have seldom had such dear guests." Dankwart
answered: "That cannot be. Where would you find the food, the bread and
the wine, that you would need for so many warriors for another night?"
When the host heard that, he said: "Stop that talk. My dear
lords, do not refuse me. Truly, I can feed you for fourteen days, with
your whole retinue that has come here with you. King Etzel has taken
very little from me yet."

However hard they resisted, they had to stay until the fourth
morning. In that time the host's generosity did such things that word of
it traveled far: he gave his guests both horses and clothes. It
could go on no longer; they had to leave. Bold Rüdiger could hold back
very little, so generous was he: whatever anyone wanted to take, he
refused to no one, and it pleased them all. Their noble retinue
brought the many saddled horses out in front of the gate, and the
foreign warriors came out to them with their shields in their hands, for
they meant to ride into Etzel's land.

The host had offered his gifts to everyone before the noble
guests came out in front of the hall. He knew how to live generously and
with great honor. His beautiful daughter he had given to Giselher.
Then he gave Gunther, that praiseworthy hero who seldom accepted
any gift, a coat of armor, and the noble mighty prince wore it well,
with honor. Gunther bowed over noble Rüdiger's hand. Then he gave
Gernot a fine sword, which Gernot later carried splendidly in battle.
The margrave's wife did not begrudge him the gift at all; and in the
end good Rüdiger lost his life by it. Gotelind offered Hagen her
loving gift, as was only proper for her: since the king had accepted
one, Hagen too should not go from her to the festival without a present
from her. Yet he declined it afterwards. "Of everything I have
ever seen," said Hagen, "I would want nothing more to carry away from
here than that shield there on the wall. I would gladly take that into
Etzel's land."

When the margravine heard Hagen's words, it reminded her of her
grief, and weeping became her. She thought with great pain of Nudung's
death; Witege had killed him, and it had left her in deep sorrow.
She said to the warrior: "I will give you the shield. I wish to
God in heaven that the man who once carried it in his hand were still
alive! He lay dead in battle. I must weep for him forever; that is the
distress of a poor woman like me."

The noble margravine rose from her seat. With her white hands she
took hold of the shield; the lady carried it to Hagen, and he took it in
his hand. The gift was bestowed on the warrior with honor. A
cover of bright silk lay over its colors. Daylight never shone on a
better shield, set with precious stones; if anyone had wanted to buy it
at its cost, it was well worth a thousand marks. Hagen had the
shield carried away ahead of him. Then Dankwart came to court, and the
margrave's child gave him rich clothes, which he later wore among the
Huns in great splendor.

All of the gifts they accepted would never have come into any of
their hands except out of love for the host, who offered them so
handsomely. Later they became such enemies to him that they had to
strike him dead.

Swift Volker went courteously with his fiddle to stand before
Gotelind. He played sweet tunes and sang her his songs. With that he
took his leave as he parted from Pöchlarn. The margravine had a
chest brought to her; now you may hear of friendly gifts. From it she
took twelve arm-rings and slid them onto his hand. "You are to take
these away from here into Etzel's land, and wear them at court
for my sake, so that when you come back I can be told how you served me
there at the festival." What the lady asked of him, he carried out well
in the end.

The host said to his guests: "You will travel all the more
easily, for I will guide you myself and have you well guarded, so that
no one can harm you on the road." Then his pack-horses were loaded
without delay. The host was well equipped, with five hundred men,
with horses and with clothes; he took them with him to the festival in
great splendor. Not one of them ever came back to Pöchlarn alive.

The host parted from his home with loving kisses, and so did
Giselher, as his good breeding told him to. They held beautiful women in
their arms. Later many a young lady had to weep for that. Then
the windows were thrown open everywhere; the host and his men were about
to go to their horses. I think their hearts told them of the crushing
sorrow to come, for many a lady wept there, and many a lovely girl.
Plenty of them ached for their dear kinsmen, whom they never saw
again at Pöchlarn. Yet the men rode off cheerfully down over the sand,
downriver along the Danube and into the land of the Huns.

Then noble Rüdiger, the cheerful knight, said to the Burgundians:
"The news that we are coming to the Huns must not be kept back. King
Etzel has never heard anything that pleased him more." The
messenger rode fast down through Austria, and people everywhere were
told that the heroes were coming from Worms across the Rhine. Nothing
could have pleased the king's household more. The messengers
hurried on ahead with the news that the Nibelungs were in the land of
the Huns. "You must welcome them well, Kriemhild, my lady. Your dear
brothers are coming to you in great honor."

Lady Kriemhild stood at a window, watching for her kinsmen as
friends still watch for friends. She saw many a man from her homeland.
The king heard the news too, and began to laugh for joy. "Oh, how
happy I am!" said Kriemhild. "Here my kinsmen bring many a new shield
and white coats of mail. Whoever wants gold, let him remember my wrongs,
and I will always be his friend."
