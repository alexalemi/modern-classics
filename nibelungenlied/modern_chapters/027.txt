Chapter 28: How the Nibelungs Came to Etzel's Castle

When the Burgundians came into Etzel's land, old Hildebrand of
Bern, the Italian city we call Verona, heard of it. He told his lord,
and it grieved Dietrich deeply; he asked Hildebrand to give the bold,
high-spirited knights a good welcome. Swift Wolfhart had the
horses brought, and many a strong warrior rode out with Dietrich to
greet them on the field, where the guests had pitched many a splendid
tent.

When Hagen of Tronje saw them riding toward him from far off,
he said courteously to his lords: "Now, swift warriors, you should get
up from your seats and go to meet these men who want to welcome you.
A company is coming this way that I know well. They are swift
warriors from the land of the Amelungs, and the lord of Bern leads
them; they are proud men. Do not scorn whatever service they offer
you."

Then many a knight and squire got down from his horse beside
Dietrich, as was only right. They went over to the guests, to where
the heroes were, and greeted the men from the land of Burgundy warmly.

When Lord Dietrich saw them coming toward him, he spoke to
Ute's sons, and now you may like to hear what the warrior said. Their
journey grieved him; he thought Rüdiger knew about the danger and had
told them. "Welcome, my lords Gunther and Giselher, Gernot and
Hagen, and Lord Volker too, and Dankwart the swift. Do you not know
this? Kriemhild still weeps bitterly for the hero of the Nibelung
land."

"She can weep a long time," said Hagen. "He has lain dead for
many a year now. She should love the king of the Huns instead.
Siegfried is not coming back; he was buried long ago."

"Let us leave Siegfried's wounds alone for now. As long as Lady
Kriemhild is alive, harm may still be done." So spoke Dietrich, the
lord of Bern. "You are the Nibelungs' mainstay; guard yourself against
it."

"How am I supposed to guard myself?" said the noble king.
"Etzel sent us messengers — what more should I ask? — to say we should
ride here to him in this land. And my sister Kriemhild has sent us
many messages too."

"I can give you good advice," said Hagen. "Ask Lord Dietrich
and his good heroes to tell you the news in full, so that they let you
know what Lady Kriemhild has in mind."

Then the three mighty kings went aside to talk: Gunther and
Gernot, and Lord Dietrich too. "Now tell us, noble good knight of
Bern, what you know of the queen's state of mind."

The lord of Bern said: "What more can I tell you? Every morning
I hear Etzel's wife weeping and lamenting, in pitiful grief, to the
mighty God of heaven over strong Siegfried's death."

"What we have heard cannot be changed now," said the bold man,
Volker the fiddler. "We must ride to court and let it be seen what may
happen to us swift warriors there among the Huns."

The bold Burgundians rode on to court. They came in splendor,
after the custom of their land, and many a bold man among the Huns
wondered about Hagen of Tronje, what he might look like. It was
enough that the story was told — that he had killed Siegfried of the
Netherlands, the strongest of all warriors, Kriemhild's husband — and
so there was a great deal of asking about Hagen at court. The
hero was well built, that is the plain truth: broad in the chest, his
hair streaked with gray, his legs long, his gaze terrible; and he
walked like a lord.

Then the Burgundian men were shown to their lodgings, and
Gunther's household was quartered apart from them. The queen, who bore
him so much hatred, had advised this; and that is why the squires were
later killed in their lodgings.

Dankwart, Hagen's brother, was the marshal. The king earnestly
put his household in his charge, to look after them well and give them
plenty. The hero of Burgundy wished them all well.

Beautiful Kriemhild went with her attendants to where she
received the Nibelungs with a false heart. She kissed Giselher and
took him by the hand. Hagen of Tronje saw it, and he laced his helmet
tighter.

"After a greeting like that," said Hagen, "swift warriors may
well think again. Kings and their men are greeted differently here. We
have not made a good journey to this festival."

She said: "Be welcome to anyone who is glad to see you. I do
not greet you out of any friendship I feel for you myself. Tell me
what you bring me from Worms across the Rhine, that you should be so
very welcome to me."

"If I had known," said Hagen, "that warriors were supposed to
bring you gifts — I am rich enough — I would have thought ahead and
brought you my gift here to this land."

"Now you shall tell me more. The Nibelung treasure: what have
you done with it? It was mine, as everyone knows. You should have
brought it to me here in Etzel's land."

"Truly, my Lady Kriemhild, it is many a day since I had the
keeping of the Nibelung treasure. My lords had it sunk in the Rhine,
and there it must truly stay until the Last Day."

The queen said: "I thought as much. You have brought me very
little of it here into this land, though it was my own and I once had
it in my keeping. That gives me many a sad day, all my life."

"The devil is what I bring you," said Hagen. "I have enough to
carry with my shield and my coat of mail; my helmet is so bright, and
the sword in my hand — that I will not bring you."

Then the queen said to all the warriors: "No one may carry
weapons into the hall. You heroes, hand them over to me; I will have
them kept safe."

"Truly," said Hagen, "that will never be done. I do not want
the honor, generous lady, of having you carry my shield and my other
weapons to the lodgings. You are a queen. My father never taught me
that; I will be my own chamberlain."

"Oh, what grief is mine," said Lady Kriemhild. "Why will my
brother and Hagen not let their shields be kept? They have been
warned. If I knew who did it, he would have to die."

Prince Dietrich answered her angrily: "It is I who warned the
noble mighty kings and bold Hagen, the Burgundians' man. Go on,
she-devil — you shall not make me pay for it."

Etzel's wife was deeply ashamed at that; she bitterly feared
Dietrich. She went away from him quickly without a word, only casting
fierce looks at her enemies.

Then two warriors took each other by the hand: one was Lord
Dietrich, the other Hagen. The high-spirited warrior said courteously:
"Your coming to the Huns truly grieves me, because of what the
queen has said." Hagen of Tronje said: "It will all come right." So
the two bold men talked together, and King Etzel began to ask about
it.

"I would like to know," said the mighty king, "who that warrior
is whom Lord Dietrich is welcoming so warmly over there. He carries
himself proudly. Whoever his father may be, he could well be a good
hero."

One of Kriemhild's men answered the king: "He was born at
Tronje; his father's name was Aldrian. However cheerful he may act
here, he is a grim man. I will let you see that I have told no lie."

"How am I to tell that he is so grim?" asked the king. At that
time the king did not yet know the many evil tricks that the queen
later played on her kinsmen, so that she let not one of them leave the
Huns alive.

"I knew Aldrian well," Etzel went on. "He was my vassal. He won
praise and great honor here with me. I made him a knight and gave him
my gold. Loyal Helche was fond of him with all her heart. And
that is how I know for certain who they are. Two handsome boys became
my hostages, he and Walther of Spain. They grew to manhood here. Hagen
I sent home again; Walther escaped with Hildegund."

He was thinking of old stories from long ago. He had clearly
recognized his friend from Tronje, the man who had done him great
service in his youth. Later, in Etzel's old age, Hagen killed many of
his dear friends.
