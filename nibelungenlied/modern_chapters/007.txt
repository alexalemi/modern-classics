Chapter 8: How Siegfried Went to Fetch His Nibelung Warriors

Then Siegfried went out through the gate and down to the sand,
wrapped in his cloak of invisibility, and there he found a small boat.
Siegmund's son stepped into it in secret and drove it away from shore
as fast as if the wind were blowing it. No one could see the
boatman. The little boat flew along, driven by Siegfried's strength,
which was very great. People thought some uncommonly strong wind was
pushing it. No: it was Siegfried driving it, the son of beautiful
Sieglinde.

In the space of a day and one night, by his tremendous strength,
he came to a land more than a hundred days' journey away. Its people
were called the Nibelungs, and there he kept the great treasure.
The hero sailed alone to a broad island, and the handsome knight
quickly tied up the boat. Then he walked up to a mountain with a castle
standing on it and asked for lodging, as travelers worn out from the
road do.

He came to the gate and found it locked against him. Truly, these
people guarded their honor, as people still do. The stranger began to
pound on the door, which was well guarded, and inside he found
a huge brute of a man keeping watch over the castle, with his
weapons lying beside him at all hours. "Who is that," he said,
"hammering so hard on the gate?" Lord Siegfried, standing outside,
disguised his voice. "I am a warrior," he said. "Now unlock the
gate, or before the day is out I will have made some of you angry out
here, some man who would rather lie soft and take his ease." What Lord
Siegfried said made the gatekeeper furious.

By now the bold giant had put on his armor and set his helmet on
his head. The powerful man snatched up his shield, swung the gate open,
and ran at Siegfried with savage fury. How dare he wake so many
bold men! Swift blows came from the giant's hand. The noble guest began
to defend himself, and then the gatekeeper burst the clasps of
Siegfried's shield apart with an iron bar. The hero was in real
danger now, and Siegfried began to be somewhat afraid of dying, the
gatekeeper struck with such strength. For that, his master Siegfried
thought well of him afterward.

They fought so hard that the whole castle rang with it, and the
din was heard in Nibelung's hall. He overpowered the gatekeeper and
then bound him, and the news of it spread through all the Nibelung
land. Far through the mountain Alberich heard the fierce
fighting: a wild dwarf, and a very bold one. He armed himself at once
and ran to where he found the noble guest tying up the giant.
Alberich was fierce and more than strong enough. He wore a helmet
and mail on his body and carried a heavy whip of gold in his hand, and
he ran at full speed to where Siegfried stood. Seven heavy knobs
hung from the front of the whip, and with them he beat the shield on
the bold man's arm so cruelly that much of it was smashed to pieces.
The handsome guest began to fear for his life.

He flung the shattered shield from his arm and thrust his sword,
a long one, back into its sheath. He had no wish to strike his own
chamberlain dead; he held himself back, as his good breeding required.
He rushed at Alberich with his strong hands, seized the old
gray-bearded man by the beard, and hauled on it so roughly that
Alberich screamed out loud. The young hero's handling hurt him badly.

The bold dwarf cried out loud: "Let me live! If I could be any
warrior's bondsman but one — I have sworn him oaths that I am his
subject — I would serve you till I died." That was a cunning man
talking. He bound Alberich as he had bound the giant before, and
Siegfried's strength hurt him badly. The dwarf asked: "What is your
name?"

"My name is Siegfried," he said. "I thought you knew me well."

"How glad I am to hear it!" said Alberich the dwarf. "Now I have
seen for myself, from your fighting, that you have every right to be
lord of this land. I will do whatever you command, if you let me live."

Then Lord Siegfried said: "Go quickly and bring me the best
warriors we have, a thousand Nibelungs, so that they can see me here."
Why he wanted that, no one ever heard him say. He untied the
giant and Alberich, and Alberich ran straight to where the warriors
lay. Anxiously he woke the Nibelung men. "Up, heroes!" he said. "You
are to go to Siegfried."

They sprang from their beds, all of them ready and willing. A
thousand quick knights dressed themselves well and went to where they
found Siegfried standing, and there was a handsome greeting, part of it
in deeds. Many candles were lit, and they poured him spiced wine.
He thanked them all for coming so fast. "You are to cross the water
with me," he said, and he found the bold and good heroes very willing.

A good three thousand warriors had come at once, and from them the
best thousand were chosen. Their helmets were brought to them, and the
rest of their gear, because he meant to take them into Brunhild's land.
"Good knights," he said, "I will tell you this: you must wear
very rich clothes at court there, because many lovely women will be
looking at us. So dress yourselves in fine attire."

Early one morning they set out. What swift companions Siegfried
had won! They took good horses and splendid clothing with them, and so
they came in knightly style into Brunhild's land.

The lovely girls were standing on the battlements. "Does anyone
know," the queen said, "who those are that I see sailing so far out on
the sea? They carry rich sails, whiter than snow." The king of
the Rhine said: "They are my men. I left them behind near here on the
journey; I sent for them, and now they have come, my lady." Everyone
looked hard at the magnificent strangers.

Then they saw Siegfried standing at the prow of one of the ships
in splendid clothes, and many other men. "Sir King," the queen said,
"you must tell me: am I to receive these guests, or am I to withhold my
greeting?" He said: "You should go out to meet them in front of
the great hall, so that they understand we are glad to see them." The
queen did as the king advised her, and by her greeting she set
Siegfried apart from the others.

Lodgings were found for the guests, and their gear was taken into
safekeeping. So many visitors had now come into the land that they
crowded each other everywhere in their companies. Now the bold
Burgundians wanted to go home to their own country.

The queen said: "I would be grateful to anyone who knew how to
share out my silver and gold, of which I have so much, among the
king's guests and mine." Dankwart, King Giselher's vassal, answered
her: "Most noble queen, let me keep the keys. I trust myself to
share it out," said the bold warrior, "and whatever disgrace I earn
for it, let it be mine alone." That he was open-handed, the warrior
made very plain.

Once Hagen's brother had taken charge of the keys, the hero's
hand gave out gift after rich gift. Whoever asked for a mark was given
so much that all the poor could live in comfort. He gave by the
hundred pounds without counting, and plenty of men who had never worn
such splendid clothes in their lives walked in front of the hall in
rich dress. The queen heard of it, and truly, it grieved her.

The noble lady said: "Sir King, I could do without your
chamberlain's plan to leave me nothing of my own wardrobe. He is
squandering my gold. If anyone would put a stop to it, I would be
grateful to him forever. He gives such rich gifts that the
warrior must think I have sent for my own death. I mean to go on using
it for a good while yet, and I trust myself to spend what my father
left me. No queen ever had such a generous chamberlain."

Then Hagen of Tronje said: "My lady, let me tell you: the king of
the Rhine has so much gold and so many clothes to give away that we
have no need to carry off any of Brunhild's wardrobe." "No," said
the queen, "for my sake, let me fill twenty traveling chests with gold
and silk, so that my own hand has something to give when we come across
to Gunther's land."

The chests were loaded with precious stones, and her own
chamberlains had to stand by while it was done; she would not trust
Gunther's man with it. Gunther and Hagen began to laugh at that.

The queen said: "To whom shall I leave my lands? Your hand and
mine must first appoint someone." The noble king said: "Call forward
whoever you think fit for it, and we will leave him here as ruler."
The lady saw one of her highest kinsmen standing near her; it was
her mother's brother. She said to him: "Now let my castles and my lands
be in your keeping, until King Gunther's hand rules here."

Then she chose two thousand men from her household to travel with
her to Burgundy, along with those thousand warriors from the Nibelung
land. They made ready for the journey, and you could see them riding
down onto the sand. She took eighty-six women with her and a
hundred maidens as well, all of them very beautiful. They delayed no
longer; they were eager to be off. Oh, how the people they left at
home began to weep!

With courtly grace she left her own land. She kissed her close
kinsmen, those she found around her, and after a proper leave-taking
they came to the sea. To her father's land the lady never came again.
On the voyage all sorts of games were heard, and they had plenty
of amusement, for a true sea wind came to help them on their journey.
So they sailed away from the land in great high spirits.

But she would not let her lord make love to her on the voyage.
That pleasure was saved until they were home, in the castle at Worms,
at the wedding feast, where in time she and her heroes arrived in
great joy.
