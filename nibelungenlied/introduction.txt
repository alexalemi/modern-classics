The Nibelungenlied, the Song of the Nibelungs, was written down around 1200 by a poet whose name nobody recorded, perhaps at the court of the Bishop of Passau on the Danube. It tells a story German-speaking singers had been reciting for centuries, and its oldest layer is history. In 436 the Burgundian kingdom on the Rhine around Worms was destroyed by Hun soldiers fighting for Rome, and its king Gundahar was killed. Attila the Hun died in 453 on the night of his wedding to a Germanic bride. Theoderic the Great, king of the Ostrogoths, ruled Italy from Ravenna and Verona until 526. In the poem they appear as Gunther, as Etzel, and as Dietrich of Bern, which is Verona, all alive at the same time and bound into one tragedy.

The poem has two halves. In the first, Siegfried of Xanten, a prince who has killed a dragon and bathed in its blood, comes to Worms to win Kriemhild, the sister of King Gunther. He helps Gunther win Brunhild, the warrior-queen of Isenland, by a deception, and years later the deception comes out in a quarrel between the two queens. Gunther's vassal Hagen of Tronje kills Siegfried. In the second half, Kriemhild marries Etzel, King of the Huns, and thirteen years later invites her brothers to his court.

It is a poem of 2,379 four-line stanzas, divided into thirty-nine episodes the poet called aventiuren, which this edition calls chapters. Its first readers were knights and court ladies, and much of it is ceremony: who greets whom first, who rides beside whom, what each guest is given. These scenes matter. Honor, loyalty and insult are measured in them, and every great killing in the poem begins with a slight at a feast. The poet also tells you, again and again, how things will end. This is not carelessness. Knowing that they are all riding to their deaths is how the story works.

The poem was forgotten for centuries until a manuscript was found in an Austrian castle in 1755, and in the nineteenth century it was claimed as Germany's national epic. The Nazis used it heavily; in 1943 Göring compared the army trapped at Stalingrad to the Burgundians in Etzel's burning hall. Wagner's Ring cycle is a different version of the same legend, built mostly from Norse sources.

This edition retells every stanza in modern English prose, translated from the Middle High German text of the manuscript known as B, with Daniel B. Shumway's 1909 prose translation consulted for sense only. The names are the poem's own: Etzel, not Attila, and Isenland, not Iceland.
