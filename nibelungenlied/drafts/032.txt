Chapter 33: How the Burgundians Fought the Huns

{1951} When bold Dankwart came in through the door, he told Etzel's
household to step back out of his way. All his clothing was running
with blood, and he carried a mighty weapon bare in his hand.
{1952} Dankwart shouted to the warrior: "You have been sitting here
far too long, brother Hagen. To you and to God in heaven I cry out our
distress: the knights and the squires lie dead in their lodgings."

{1953} Hagen called back to him: "Who did this?"

"Lord Blödel did it, he and his men. And he has paid dearly for it, I
can tell you that: with my own hands I struck his head off."

{1954} "That is small harm," said Hagen, "when the story told of a
warrior is that he lost his life at a warrior's hands. Lovely women
will have all the less cause to mourn him. {1955} Now tell me, brother
Dankwart, why are you so red? I think your wounds are giving you great
trouble. If the man who did this to you is anywhere in this land, it
will cost him his life, unless the foul devil saves him."

{1956} "You see me unhurt. My clothes are wet with blood, but it is
other men's wounds that did that — I have killed so many of them today
that if I had to swear to the number, I could never tell it."

{1957} "Brother Dankwart," he said, "then guard the door for us, and
do not let a single Hun out through it. I want to speak with these
warriors, since our need forces us to. Our household lies dead in
front of them, and they never deserved it."

{1958} "If I am to be the chamberlain," said the bold man, "I know how
to serve such mighty kings. I will keep the stairs as my honor
requires." Nothing could have been less welcome to Kriemhild's
warriors.

{1959} "I wonder very much," Hagen went on, "what the Hun warriors in
here are whispering about. I think they would gladly do without the
man standing at that door — the one who has just brought the
Burgundians the news from court. {1960} For a long time now I have
heard it said of Kriemhild that she would not let her heartbreak go
unavenged. Let us drink to the dead, and pay for the king's wine! The
young lord of the Huns shall be the first."

{1961} Then Hagen, that good hero, struck the child Ortlieb, so that
the blood ran down the sword to his hand and the head sprang into the
queen's lap. At that a murderous slaughter broke out among the
warriors, grim and great. {1962} Next, with both hands, he dealt the
tutor who had charge of the child a swift blow, so that his head
quickly lay on the floor in front of the table. It was a wretched
reward he paid the tutor. {1963} He saw a minstrel sitting at Etzel's
table. Hagen in his rage went straight for him and struck off his
right hand on the fiddle. "Take that as your message to the land of
the Burgundians."

{1964} "Oh, my hand!" said Werbel the minstrel. "Sir Hagen of Tronje,
what did I ever do to you? I came in good faith to your lords' land.
How am I to play the tunes now that I have lost my hand?"

{1965} Hagen cared little whether he ever fiddled again. Then he dealt
out murderous wounds in that hall to Etzel's warriors, and killed a
great many of them; he brought people enough in that house to their
deaths. {1966} Volker the swift sprang up from the table; his fiddle
bow rang out loud in his hand. Gunther's minstrel played roughly. Ha,
what enemies he made himself among the bold Huns!

{1967} The three noble kings sprang up from the tables too. They would
gladly have stopped the fight before more harm was done, but with all
their wits they could not prevent it, once Volker and Hagen had begun
to rage so terribly. {1968} When the lord of the Rhine saw that the
fight could not be parted, the prince himself cut many a wide wound
through his enemies' bright mail rings. He was a hero with his hands,
and he made that very plain. {1969} Strong Gernot came into the fight
as well, and truly he struck many a Hun hero dead with a sharp sword
that Rüdiger had given him. He did Etzel's warriors terrible harm.
{1970} Lady Ute's young son sprang into the fight; his weapon rang
splendidly through the helmets of Etzel's warriors from the land of
the Huns. Bold Giselher's hand did great wonders there. {1971} Brave
as they all were, the kings and all their men, still it was Giselher
you saw standing in front of them all against the enemy. He was a
good hero: with the wounds he gave, he made many a man fall down in
the blood.

{1972} Etzel's men defended themselves hard too. But you could see the
guests go hewing their way with their bright swords through the king's
hall, and on every side you could hear the great clash of weapons.
{1973} Those outside wanted to be in there with their kinsmen, and at
the tower doorway they got very little for their trouble; those inside
would gladly have been out of the hall, but Dankwart let none of them
up the stairs or down them. {1974} So a mighty press grew up in front
of the tower, and a great ringing of helmets under the swords. Bold
Dankwart came into great peril from it, and his brother worried for
him, as his loyalty required.

{1975} Hagen called loudly to Volker: "Do you see my brother standing
over there, comrade, in front of the Hun warriors under heavy blows?
Friend, save my brother for me, before we lose the warrior."

{1976} "I will, for certain," said the minstrel, and he went fiddling
his way through the great hall, a hard sword ringing often in his
hand. The warriors from the Rhine thanked him warmly. {1977} Bold
Volker said to Dankwart: "You have had a very hard time of it today.
Your brother asked me to come to your help. If you will hold the
outside, I will stand inside."

{1978} Dankwart the swift stood outside the door and defended the
stairs against anyone who came up them; you could hear the weapons
ring in the heroes' hands. Inside, Volker of the land of Burgundy did
the same. {1979} The bold fiddler shouted over the crowd: "The hall is
well locked, friend Hagen. Truly, Etzel's door is barred by two
heroes' hands as well as a thousand bolts could bar it."

{1980} When Hagen of Tronje saw the door so well guarded, the famous
good hero slung his shield onto his back. Only now did he begin to
avenge what had been done to him. His enemies had no hope of any kind
of coming out alive.

{1981} When the lord of Bern saw clearly that strong Hagen was
breaking so many helmets, the king of the Amelungs sprang up on a
bench and said: "Hagen is pouring the very worst drink of all here."

{1982} The host was in great fear, as he had good reason to be, with
so many dear friends being snatched from him before his eyes; he
himself barely got away from his enemies alive. He sat there in
dread. What good did it do him that he was king?

{1983} Mighty Kriemhild cried out to Dietrich: "Help me, noble knight,
help me get out of here alive, by the virtue of all the princes of the
Amelung land! If Hagen reaches me, I am as good as dead."

{1984} "How am I to help you, noble queen?" said Lord Dietrich. "Now I
am afraid for myself. Gunther's men are so bitterly enraged that at
this moment I cannot protect anyone."

{1985} "No, no, Lord Dietrich, noble good knight! Show your knightly
spirit today and help me out of here, or I shall be left here dead."
Kriemhild's fear was driving her very hard.

{1986} "I will try, and see if I can help you. For in many a year I
have never seen so many good knights so bitterly enraged." Truly, he
saw the blood spring from the swords through the helmets.

{1987} The chosen warrior began to shout with all his strength, so
that his voice rang out like a bison's horn and the castle far and
wide resounded with the force of it. Dietrich's strength was beyond
all measure.

{1988} Gunther heard the man shouting in the hard fighting, and he
began to listen. He said: "Dietrich's voice has reached my ears. I
think our warriors have taken someone from him here. {1989} I see him
up on the table; he is waving with his hand. You friends and kinsmen
from the land of Burgundy, hold off from the fighting! Let us hear and
see what my men have done to the warrior."

{1990} When King Gunther asked it and commanded it, they held their
swords back in the heat of the fight. That was power indeed, that no
one struck a blow there. Then he asked the man from Bern for his news
quickly enough. {1991} He said: "Most noble Dietrich, what has been
done to you here by my kinsmen? I am willing to make amends to you,
and I am ready for peace. Whatever anyone may have done to you, it
would grieve me to the heart."

{1992} Then Lord Dietrich said: "Nothing has been done to me. Let me
go out of this house under your safe conduct, away from this hard
fight, with my household. I will serve you forever for it, I promise
you."

{1993} "Why are you running away so soon?" said Wolfhart. "Truly, the
fiddler has not barred the door so tight that we cannot open it wide
enough to get out through it."

"Hold your tongue," said Lord Dietrich. "You have done the devil's own
work."

{1994} Then King Gunther said: "I will allow it. Lead out of this
house whoever you like, few or many — all but my enemies. They must
stay here. They have done me such bitter wrong here among the Huns."

{1995} When Dietrich heard that, he put his arm around the noble
queen, whose fear was great. With his other arm he led Etzel out with
him, and six hundred fine men went out with Dietrich too.

{1996} Then the margrave, noble Rüdiger, said: "If any more of those
who would gladly serve you are to come out of this house, let us hear
it. Then a lasting peace will stand between good friends, as it
should."

{1997} Giselher of the land of Burgundy answered him: "Peace and
friendship you shall have from us, since you are steadfast in your
loyalty, you and your men. You may go out of here with your kinsmen
without any fear."

{1998} When Lord Rüdiger left the hall, five hundred men or more
followed him out, kinsmen and vassals of the lord of Pöchlarn. Later
Rüdiger suffered great harm from the king.

{1999} Then a Hun warrior saw Etzel walking close beside Dietrich, and
wanted to join them. The fiddler gave him such a blow that his head
soon lay at Etzel's feet.

{2000} When the lord of the land had come out of the house, he turned
around and looked at Volker. "Oh, these guests of mine! This is a
grim calamity, that all my warriors are to die before them. {2001} Oh,
this festival!" the noble king said. "There is one fighting in there
called Volker — he fights like a wild boar, and he is a minstrel. I
thank my luck that I escaped that devil. {2002} His tunes sound ugly,
and his bow-strokes are red. Truly, his music strikes many a hero
dead. I do not know what this minstrel has against us, but I never had
a guest I liked so little."

{2003} They had let out of the hall the ones they wanted to let out.
Then a tremendous din rose inside. The guests took hard revenge for
what had been done to them before. Bold Volker — ha, what helmets he
broke!

{2004} Noble King Gunther turned toward the noise. "Do you hear the
tunes, Hagen, that Volker is fiddling over there with the Huns,
whoever comes to the tower door? It is a red rosin he has on his
fiddle bow."

{2005} "I regret beyond all measure," said Hagen, "that I ever took a
seat in the hall above that warrior. I was his comrade and he was
mine, and if we ever get home again, we shall still be comrades, in
all loyalty. {2006} Now look, noble king: Volker is devoted to you. He
earns your silver and your gold with a will. His fiddle bow cuts
through the hard steel; he breaks the bright, shining ornaments off
the helmets. {2007} I never saw a fiddler stand so splendidly as the
warrior Volker has stood today. His tunes ring through helmet and
shield-rim. Truly, he deserves to ride good horses and wear splendid
clothes."

{2008} Of all the Huns' kinsmen who had been in the hall, not one
inside it was left alive now. So the din died away, since no one was
fighting with them anymore, and the bold, high-hearted warriors laid
their swords down out of their hands.
