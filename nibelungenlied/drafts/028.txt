Chapter 29: How Hagen and Volker Sat Before Kriemhild's Hall

{1758} Then the two praiseworthy warriors, Hagen of Tronje and Lord
Dietrich, went their separate ways. Gunther's vassal looked over his
shoulder for a comrade-in-arms, and he found one quickly enough.
{1759} He saw Volker, the skilled fiddler, standing beside Giselher, and
asked him to come along, for he knew the fiddler's grim temper well. In
every way Volker was a bold and good knight.

{1760} They left the lords still standing in the courtyard. Only those
two were seen walking off, far across the courtyard, toward a wide
palace. Those chosen warriors feared no one's hatred. {1761} They sat
down on a bench in front of the building, facing a hall that belonged
to Kriemhild, and their splendid clothes shone on their bodies. Plenty
of the people there would gladly have known who they were. {1762} The
Huns gaped at the two proud heroes as if they were wild animals.
Etzel's wife saw them too, through a window, and beautiful Kriemhild
was troubled all over again. {1763} It reminded her of her sorrow, and
she began to weep. Etzel's men wondered greatly what had darkened her
mood so suddenly. She said: "Hagen did that, you bold and good heroes."

{1764} They said to the lady: "How did that happen? Only a moment ago
we saw you glad. No one has ever been so bold that he could do this to
you and keep his life, if you tell us to avenge it."

{1765} "I would serve forever whoever avenged my wrongs. Whatever he
asked for, I would be ready to give it. I throw myself at your feet,"
said the king's wife. "Avenge me on Hagen, so that he loses his life."

{1766} Then sixty bold men armed themselves at once. For Kriemhild's
sake they meant to go over and kill Hagen, that very bold man, and the
fiddler as well; it was all planned out. {1767} But when the queen saw
how small her band was, she said to the heroes in a grim mood: "Give up
what you have in mind. With so few, you can never take on Hagen.
{1768} However strong and bold Hagen of Tronje may be, the man sitting
beside him, Volker the fiddler, is far stronger still. He is a
dangerous man. Truly, you should not take these heroes on so lightly."

{1769} When they heard that, more of them armed themselves — four
hundred swift warriors. The noble queen was set on doing them harm.
Later that brought great trouble on many warriors. {1770} When she saw
her men well armed, the queen said to the swift warriors: "Now wait a
while; you must stand quite still. I will go to my enemies wearing my
crown, {1771} and hear me charge Hagen of Tronje, Gunther's vassal,
with what he has done to me. I know he is so arrogant that he will not
deny a word of it. And I do not care what happens to him for it."

{1772} Then the fiddler, that bold minstrel, saw the noble queen
coming down a flight of steps from a building. When bold Volker saw
that, he said to his comrade-in-arms: {1773} "Now look, friend Hagen,
there she comes, the woman who invited us into this land without a
trace of loyalty. I never saw so many men with swords in their hands
walking so ready for a fight beside any king's wife. {1774} Do you
know, friend Hagen, whether she hates you? If she does, I advise you to
guard your life and your honor all the more carefully. Truly, that
seems wise to me. As far as I can tell, they are in an angry mood,
{1775} and some of them are so broad in the chest that anyone who wants
to protect himself had better do it in good time. I think they are
wearing bright coats of mail underneath. Who they mean it for, I cannot
tell you."

{1776} Then bold Hagen said angrily: "I know very well that all this is
aimed at me, that they carry those bright weapons in their hands. For
all they could do, I could still ride home to the land of Burgundy.
{1777} Now tell me, friend Volker, will you stand by me if Kriemhild's
men want to fight me? Let me hear it, as you love me. I will stand by
you forever in loyal service."

{1778} "I will help you, you may be sure," said the minstrel. "If I saw
the king himself coming at us with all his warriors, as long as I live
I would not yield one foot from your side out of fear."

{1779} "May God in heaven reward you, noble Volker. If they fight me,
what more do I need? Since you will help me, as I have just heard, let
these warriors come on, fully armed."

{1780} "Now let us rise from our seats," said the minstrel. "She is a
queen; let her pass by, and show her honor. She is a noble woman, and
it will do each of us honor too."

{1781} "No — for my sake, no," said Hagen. "These warriors would think
I did it out of fear if I went away from here. I will never rise from
this seat for any one of them. {1782} Truly, it suits us both better to
leave it alone. Why should I honor someone who hates me? I will never
do that as long as I live. And I do not care how much King Etzel's wife
hates me."

{1783} Arrogant Hagen laid a gleaming sword across his knees. In its
pommel shone a bright jasper, greener than grass. Kriemhild knew it
well: it was Siegfried's. {1784} When she recognized the sword, grief
overcame her. Its hilt was gold and its scabbard bound with a red
braid. It reminded her of her sorrow, and she began to weep. I think
bold Hagen had done it for exactly that reason.

{1785} Swift Volker drew a strong fiddle bow closer to him on the
bench, huge and long, very like a sword, sharp and broad. And there the
two proud warriors sat without fear. {1786} The two bold men thought so
much of themselves that they would not rise from their seat for fear
of anyone. So the noble queen walked right up to their feet, and gave
them a hostile greeting.

{1787} She said: "Tell me, Hagen, who sent for you, that you dared to
ride here into this land when you know very well what you have done to
me? If you had any sense, you would rightly have stayed away."

{1788} "No one sent for me," said Hagen. "Three warriors were invited
into this land. They are called my lords, and I am their vassal. I have
seldom stayed behind when they rode to any court."

{1789} She said: "Now tell me more. Why did you do what earned you my
hatred? You killed Siegfried, my dear husband. I will have cause enough
to weep for that until the day I die."

{1790} He said: "What more is there to say? Enough has been said. Yes,
I am Hagen, who killed Siegfried, that hero with his hands. How dearly
he paid for it that beautiful Kriemhild insulted Lady Brunhild.
{1791} There is no denying it, mighty queen. All the guilt for that
ruinous harm is mine. Let whoever wants to avenge it, woman or man. I
would be lying if I said anything else: I have done you great wrong."

{1792} She said: "Now listen, warriors, how he denies not one of my
wrongs. Whatever happens to him for it, men of Etzel, I do not care."
The arrogant warriors looked at one another. {1793} If anyone had
started the fight there, it would have ended with the honor going to
those two comrades, for they had proved themselves in battle many
times. What the Huns had boasted they would do, they had to leave
undone, out of fear.

{1794} One of the warriors said: "Why are you looking at me? What I
promised before, I take back. I will not lose my life for anyone's
reward. King Etzel's wife is trying to lead us to ruin."

{1795} Another beside him said: "I feel the same. If someone gave me
towers of good red gold, I would not take on this fiddler, not after
the fierce looks I have seen in his eyes. {1796} And I have known Hagen
since his young days; no one needs to tell me much about that warrior.
I have seen him in twenty-two battles, where many a woman came to
heartbreak. {1797} He and the man from Spain dealt many a thrust when
they fought all those battles here with Etzel, for the king's honor.
That happened often, and so Hagen rightly deserves honor. {1798} In
those days the warrior was still a boy in years. How gray they are now,
the ones who were young then! Now he has come to his full wits, and he
is a grim man. And he carries Balmung, which he won by evil means."

{1799} So the matter was settled: no one fought. That grieved the queen
to the heart. The heroes turned away; truly, they feared death at the
fiddler's hands, and they had good reason to.

{1800} Then the fiddler said: "We have seen plainly that we shall find
enemies here, as we heard said before. Let us go to court, to the
kings; then no one will dare attack our lords. {1801} How often a man
leaves a thing undone out of fear — when friend stands by friend as a
friend should, and he has the good sense not to do it. Many a man's
harm is well warded off by good sense."

{1802} "Now I will follow you," said Hagen. They went to where they
found the splendid warriors standing in the courtyard, surrounded by a
great crowd of welcomers. Bold Volker began to speak loudly {1803} to
his lords: "How long are you going to stand here and let yourselves be
jostled? You should go to court and hear from the king what mood he is
in." Then the bold and good heroes were seen pairing off.

{1804} The prince of Bern took mighty Gunther of Burgundy by the hand.
Irnfried took bold Gernot, and Rüdiger was seen walking to court with
Giselher. {1805} But however anyone else paired off and went to court,
Volker and Hagen never parted, except in one battle, at the hour of
their death. Later noble women had to weep bitterly for that.

{1806} With the kings a thousand bold men of their noble household were
seen going to court, and sixty warriors besides who had come with them;
bold Hagen had brought those from his own land. {1807} Hawart and
Iring, two chosen men, were seen walking in company beside the kings.
Dankwart and Wolfhart, a splendid warrior, were seen showing their good
breeding before all the others.

{1808} When the lord of the Rhine entered the great hall, mighty Etzel
waited no longer. He sprang from his seat when he saw him coming. No
king ever gave so fine a greeting. {1809} "Welcome, Lord Gunther, and
Lord Gernot too, and your brother Giselher. I sent you my service,
loyally and eagerly, to Worms across the Rhine, and all your household
shall be welcome to me. {1810} Now be very welcome, you two warriors,
Volker the bold and Hagen too, to me and to my lady here in this land.
She sent you many a messenger to the Rhine."

{1811} Hagen of Tronje said: "I have heard a great deal about that. If
I had not come to the Huns for my lords' sake, I would have ridden into
this land in your honor." Then the noble host took his dear guests by
the hand {1812} and led them to the seat where he himself sat. Drink
was poured for the guests, and poured generously, in wide golden bowls:
mead, wine, and morat, the mulberry drink. And the strangers so far
from home were bidden a hearty welcome.

{1813} King Etzel said: "I will tell you this: nothing in the whole
world could please me more than that you heroes have come to me. It
has taken a great sorrow off the queen. {1814} I wonder what I ever
did to you — I have won so many noble guests — that you never cared to
come into my lands. Now that I have seen you, it is turned to joy for
me."

{1815} Rüdiger, a knight of high spirit, answered: "You may well be
glad to see them. Their loyalty is good; my lady's kinsmen know how to
keep it well. They bring many a handsome warrior to your house."

{1816} The lords had come to mighty Etzel's court on midsummer's eve.
Seldom has anyone heard of so high a greeting as the one he gave the
heroes. Now it was time to eat, and the host went with them to the
table. {1817} A host never sat more handsomely among his guests. They
were given food and drink in plenty. Whatever they wanted was ready for
them, for great wonders had been told about those heroes.
