Chapter 3: How Siegfried Came to Worms

{44} Heartache rarely troubled the young lord. But he heard people
telling of a girl in Burgundy, beautiful beyond anything you could
wish for, and from her he would later gain great joy, and great
hardship too.

{45} Her extraordinary beauty was known far and wide, and her high
spirit along with it, and so many a hero came to hear of the young lady
that it drew guests in crowds to Gunther's land.
{46} But however many were seen courting her love, Kriemhild never
once admitted to herself that she wanted any of them for her own. The
man she would one day belong to was still a stranger to her.

{47} Then Sieglinde's son set his heart on high love. Beside
him, every other suitor's wooing was so much wind: he was the man who
could earn a beautiful woman's love. And later noble Kriemhild did
become bold Siegfried's wife.

{48} Since he wanted to set his hopes on a lasting love, his kinsmen
and many of his vassals advised him to court someone who was his
equal. Then bold Siegfried said: "Then I
will take Kriemhild, {49} the beautiful young lady of Burgundy, for her
beauty beyond all measure. I know this much: no emperor was ever so
mighty that, if he wanted a wife, this royal princess would not be a
fitting match for him."

{50} The same news reached Siegmund. His people talked of it, and that
was how he learned what his son intended, and it grieved him deeply
that the boy meant to court that glorious girl. {51} Sieglinde, the
noble king's wife, heard it too, and she was badly frightened for her
child's life, because she knew Gunther and his men well. People began
to press the warrior hard to give the venture up.

{52} Then bold Siegfried said: "Dearest father, I would rather go
without the love of noble women for the rest of my life than court
anywhere except where my heart has fixed its great love. Whatever
anyone says, there is no arguing me out of it."

{53} "If you will not turn back," said the king, "then truly I am glad
of your resolve, and I will help you carry it through as well as I
possibly can. But King Gunther has many arrogant men about him.
{54} If there were no one but Hagen, that warrior alone knows how to
carry his arrogance so high that I badly fear it will bring us sorrow
if we go courting that glorious girl."

{55} "How can that hurt us?" Siegfried said. "Whatever I cannot get
from them by friendly asking, my own hand can win by courage. I am
confident I can wrest from them both their people and their land."

{56} Prince Siegmund said: "I do not like this talk. If word of it were
carried to the Rhine, you could never ride into that land at all. I
have known Gunther and Gernot a long time. {57} No one can win the girl
by force," King Siegmund went on. "I know that for certain. But if you
want to ride into that land with warriors, then whatever friends we
have will be summoned at once."

{58} "That is not what I have in mind," Siegfried answered, "that
warriors should follow me to the Rhine as if on campaign. It would
grieve me to force that lovely girl's hand that way. {59} My hand alone
can win her. I will go into Gunther's land as one of twelve, and you
will help me with that, Father Siegmund." Then his warriors were given
clothes of gray fur and fur of many colors.

{60} Now his mother Sieglinde heard the news too, and she began to
grieve for her dear child, for she was afraid of losing him to
Gunther's men. The noble queen wept and wept. {61} Lord Siegfried went
to find her and spoke to his mother gently: "Lady, you must not weep on
my account. Truly, I will be safe from every fighting man alive.
{62} Help me get ready for the journey to Burgundy, so that I and my
warriors have clothes that proud heroes can wear with honor. For that
I will be grateful to you, truly and with all my loyalty."

{63} "Since you will not turn back," said Lady Sieglinde, "then I will
help you on your journey, my only child, with the finest clothes a
knight ever wore, for you and your companions. You will take plenty of
them with you."

{64} Young Siegfried bowed to the queen. He said: "I want no one on the
journey but twelve warriors, and clothes should be made ready for them.
I am eager to see how things stand with Kriemhild."

{65} So beautiful women sat sewing night and day, and hardly one of
them took any rest until Siegfried's clothes were finished. He would
not hear of giving up his journey. {66} His father had his knightly
gear adorned, so that he could ride out of Siegmund's land in style.
Their bright coats of mail were made ready too, and their strong
helmets, and their shields, handsome and broad. {67} Now the day of
their journey to Burgundy drew near, and women and men alike began to
worry whether they would ever come home again. The heroes had their
weapons and clothes loaded on the pack-horses. {68} Their horses were
handsome, their harness red gold. If any men ever lived in higher
spirits than Siegfried and his companions, they had no call to. Then
he asked leave to ride to Burgundy, {69} and the king and his wife
granted it with heavy hearts. He comforted them both lovingly. "You
must not weep on my account," he said. "Never be afraid for my life."

{70} The warriors were downcast, and many a girl wept. I think their
hearts told them the plain truth: that so many of their kinsmen would
lie dead because of this. They had good reason to lament; truly, the
grief was already upon them.

{71} On the seventh morning the bold riders came onto the river sand at
Worms. All their gear was of red gold, their harness finely made, and
the horses of bold Siegfried's men moved at an easy pace. {72} Their
shields were new, bright and broad, and their helmets were splendid, as
bold Siegfried rode to court in Gunther's land. No one had ever seen
heroes so magnificently dressed. {73} The points of their swords hung
down to their spurs. These chosen knights carried sharp javelins, and
Siegfried's was a full two hand-spans broad, with edges that cut
horribly. {74} They held gold-colored reins in their hands, and the
breast-straps of their horses were silk. So they came into the land,
and everywhere the people began to stare at them, and many of
Gunther's men ran out to meet them.

{75} High-spirited warriors, knights and squires, went up to the lords,
as was only right, received the guests into their masters' land, and
took the horses and the shields from their hands. {76} They wanted to
lead the horses away to rest. But bold Siegfried spoke up quickly:
"Leave the horses standing, mine and my men's. We mean to be gone from
here soon; I fully intend it. {77} Whoever knows, let him not keep it
from me: where can I find the king, mighty Gunther of Burgundy? Someone
tell me." One of them, who knew well, answered: {78} "If you want to
find the lord, that is easily done. I saw him just now in that wide
hall over there, among his heroes. Go in, and you will find many a
splendid man with him."

{79} By now the king had been told that fine knights had arrived,
wearing white coats of mail and magnificent clothes, and that no one in
Burgundy knew who they were. {80} The king wondered where these
splendid warriors could have come from, in their shining clothes, with
such good shields, new and broad; and it annoyed Gunther that no one
could tell him. {81} Ortwin of Metz answered the king, and he had every
right to, being both powerful and bold: "Since we do not know them, you
should send for my uncle Hagen and let him look at them. {82} He knows
the kingdoms and the foreign lands too. If he knows who these lords
are, he will tell us." The king sent for him and his men, and Hagen
was seen striding to court with his warriors in lordly style.

{83} Hagen asked what the king wanted of him. "There are warriors in my
house whom no one here knows. If you have ever seen them, Hagen, tell
me the plain truth."

{84} "I will," said Hagen. He went to a window and let his eyes travel
over the guests. He liked their bearing and their gear, but they were
strangers to him in Burgundy. {85} He said that wherever these warriors
had come from to the Rhine, they might be princes themselves, or
princes' envoys: their horses were handsome and their clothes very
fine, and wherever they came from, they were men of high spirit.

{86} "I will say this," Hagen went on. "Though I have never set eyes on
Siegfried, I am ready to believe, whatever the truth of it, that he is
the warrior striding there so grandly. {87} He brings fresh news to
this land. This hero's hand killed the bold Nibelungs, Schilbung and
Nibelung, the sons of a mighty king, and since then he has done great
wonders with his huge strength.

{88} "Once, when the hero was riding alone, with no help at all, he
came to a mountain, so I have been told, and found there, beside
Nibelung's treasure, a great many bold men. They were strangers to him
until he came to know them there. {89} The whole of Nibelung's treasure
had been carried out of a hollow mountain, and now hear a wonder: the
Nibelung men wanted to divide it up. Siegfried the warrior saw this and
it amazed him. {90} He came so close that he could see the heroes and
they could see him, and one of them said: 'Here comes strong Siegfried,
the hero from the Netherlands.' Strange things he found among the
Nibelungs. {91} Schilbung and Nibelung welcomed the warrior, and with
one voice the noble young princes asked the handsome man to divide the
treasure for them. They begged him so eagerly that the lord agreed.

{92} "He saw so many precious stones, as we hear tell, that a hundred
wagons could not have carried them, and still more red gold from the
Nibelung land, and bold Siegfried's hand was to divide it all. {93} As
his reward they gave him Nibelung's sword. But they were badly served by
the service the good hero Siegfried was to do them: he could not bring
it to an end, and they grew angry. {94} Among their followers they had
twelve bold men who were mighty giants. What good did it do them?
Siegfried's hand killed them in his rage, and he subdued seven hundred
warriors of the Nibelung land {95} with the good sword called Balmung.
Out of sheer terror of that sword and the bold man who held it, many a
young warrior made the land and its castles subject to him. {96} And the
two mighty kings, he killed them both. Then Alberich put him in great
danger: the dwarf meant to avenge his lords on the spot, until he found
out what Siegfried's strength was. {97} The mighty dwarf could not stand
against him. Like wild lions they ran at the mountain, and there
Siegfried took from Alberich the cloak of invisibility. Then Siegfried,
that terrible man, was master of the treasure. {98} All who had dared to
fight lay dead. He had the treasure hauled and carried straight back to
the place the Nibelung men had taken it from, and strong Alberich was
made its keeper. {99} He had to swear oaths to serve Siegfried as his
servant, and he was ready for every kind of task."

So said Hagen of Tronje. "That is what he has done. No warrior ever
had such strength. {100} And I know one thing more about him for
certain: this hero's hand killed a dragon. He bathed in its blood, and
his skin turned to horn, so that no weapon can cut him, as has been
proved many times over. {101} We should receive this lord all the
better for it, so that we do not earn the young warrior's hatred. He is
so bold that we had better have him as a friend; he has done so many
wonders by his strength."

{102} The mighty king said: "You may well be right. See how boldly he
stands there, ready for a fight, that bold man, he and his warriors.
Let us go down and meet him."

{103} "You can do that with honor," Hagen said. "He is of noble blood,
a mighty king's son. From the way he carries himself, Christ knows, I
would say it is no small matter that has brought him riding here."

{104} The king of the land said: "Then let him be welcome. He is noble
and bold, as I have heard, and he shall have the benefit of it in
Burgundy." Then Lord Gunther went to where Siegfried stood. {105} The
host and his warriors received the guest so well that nothing was
lacking in their courtesy, and the handsome man bowed to them, because
they had greeted him so graciously.

{106} "I am curious about one thing," the king said at once. "Where
have you come from into this land, noble Siegfried, and what are you
looking for at Worms on the Rhine?" The guest answered the king: "I
will not hide it from you. {107} I was told in my father's land that
here with you were the boldest warriors any king ever had; I have heard
it many times, and I wanted to know it for certain. That is why I have
come. {108} And I hear you yourself credited with bravery: that no king
was ever seen bolder. People say so all through these lands, and I will
not turn back until I know the truth of it. {109} I too am a warrior,
and I am to wear a crown. I mean to make them say of me that I hold my
people and my land by right, and my honor and my head are the pledge.
{110} Now, if you are as bold as I have been told, then I do not care
whether it pleases anyone or not: I will take from you by force
whatever you have. Your lands and your castles shall be subject to me."

{111} The king was amazed, and his men with him, at what they were
hearing: that this man meant to take their lands from them. His
warriors heard it, and anger rose in them. {112} "How have I deserved
this," said Gunther the warrior, "that what my father held so long
with honor we should lose to the strength of any man? That would be a
poor showing of our own knighthood."

{113} "I will not give it up," the bold man answered. "Unless your land
can keep its peace by your own courage, I will rule all of it; and if
you win mine by your strength, then my inheritance shall be subject to
you. {114} Your inheritance and mine shall be staked alike, and
whichever of us beats the other, all of it shall serve him, the people
and the lands."

Hagen spoke against it at once, and so did Gernot. {115} "We have no
wish," said Gernot, "to conquer any land, and no wish for any man to
lie dead at a hero's hands for it. We have rich lands that serve us by
right, and no one has a better claim to them than we do."

{116} His kinsmen stood there grim-faced, and among them was Ortwin of
Metz. "This talk of peace sits badly with me," he said. "Strong
Siegfried has challenged you without any cause. {117} If you and your
brothers had no defense at all, and if he had brought a whole king's
army with him, I would still trust myself to fight so hard that this
bold man would have every reason to drop his arrogance."

{118} That made the hero from the Netherlands furious. "Your hand will
not presume against me," he said. "I am a mighty king; you are a king's
vassal. Twelve of your kind could never stand against me in a fight."

{119} Then Ortwin of Metz shouted loudly for swords; he was every inch
Hagen of Tronje's sister's son. That Hagen had kept silent so long
troubled the king. Then Gernot stepped between them, that bold and
cheerful knight. {120} He said to Ortwin: "Put your anger away. Lord
Siegfried has done us nothing we cannot still settle with good manners.
That is my advice: let us keep him as a friend. It does us more credit."

{121} Strong Hagen said: "It may well grieve us, and all your warriors,
that he ever rode to the Rhine looking for a fight. He should have left
it alone. My lords would never have done such a wrong to him."

{122} Siegfried, that powerful man, answered: "If what I have said
offends you, Sir Hagen, then I will let you see that these hands of
mine mean to hold great power here in Burgundy."

{123} "I will prevent that single-handed," said Gernot, and he forbade
all his warriors to say a word in arrogance that might lead to grief.
And Siegfried, too, thought of the glorious girl. {124} "How would it
suit us to fight you?" Gernot went on. "However many heroes lay dead
for it, we would have little honor from it and you little profit."
Siegfried, King Siegmund's son, answered him: {125} "Then why are Hagen
and Ortwin waiting? Why do they not rush to fight, with all the kinsmen
they have here in Burgundy?" But they had to hold their tongues; that
was Gernot's counsel.

{126} "You are welcome among us," said Ute's son, "with the companions
who have come with you. We will gladly serve you, I and my kinsmen."
Then Gunther's wine was poured for the guests. {127} The lord of the
land said: "Everything we have, if you will accept it honorably, is at
your disposal, and we will share it with you, our lives and our goods."
At that Lord Siegfried's mood softened a little.

{128} All their gear was taken and kept for them, and the best lodgings
to be found were sought out; Siegfried's squires were made comfortable.
From then on the guest was a welcome sight in Burgundy. {129} For many
days after that he was shown great honor, a thousand times more than I
can tell you. His courage had earned it, believe me. Hardly anyone who
saw him bore him any ill will.

{130} The kings and their men kept themselves busy with sports, and
whatever they took up, he was always the best at it. No one could keep
up with him, so great was his strength, whether they were throwing the
stone or hurling the javelin. {131} And whenever the gallant knights,
as courtesy demanded, passed the time in the company of the ladies, the
hero from the Netherlands was always a welcome sight there, for he had
set his mind on high love. {132} Whatever was begun, he was ready for
it. But in his heart he carried one lovely girl, and she carried him in
hers, the lady he had still never seen, who in private often spoke of
him with great fondness.

{133} Whenever the young men, knights and squires, played their games
in the courtyard, Kriemhild the noble princess very often watched them
from her window. In those days she needed no other pastime. {134} Had
he known that she whom he carried in his heart was watching him, he
would have had pastime enough for the rest of his life. And had his
eyes seen her, I know it for certain: nothing in this world could ever
have made him happier. {135} Whenever he stood among the heroes in the
courtyard, as people still do to pass the time, Sieglinde's son stood
there so winningly that many a lady loved him for it with all her
heart. {136} And many a time he thought: "How is it ever going to
happen that I see with my own eyes the noble girl I love with all my
heart, and have loved for so long? She is still a stranger to me, and
it leaves me standing here in sorrow."

{137} Whenever the mighty kings rode out across their lands, the
warriors all had to ride with them, and so Siegfried had to go too.
The lady was sorry for that; and he, for love of her, often suffered
great hardship. {138} So he lived with the lords, it is the plain
truth, a full year in Gunther's land, and in all that time he never
once saw the lovely girl from whom so much joy would come to him later,
and so much sorrow too.
