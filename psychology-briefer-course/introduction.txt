William James (1842–1910) trained as a physician, taught anatomy and physiology at Harvard, and then taught the first courses there in the new experimental psychology. In 1890, after twelve years of work, he published The Principles of Psychology, two volumes and some fourteen hundred pages. Two years later his publisher, Henry Holt, brought out this single volume for students. Its readers soon called the long book "the James" and this one "the Jimmy".

James's preface says what he did: he cut the argument with other writers, added short chapters on the senses for students who knew no physiology, and wrote about two fifths of the book new. The rest, he says, is "scissors and paste". Most of the famous passages survived the cutting. Chapter X calls habit "the enormous fly-wheel of society". Chapter XI, "The Stream of Consciousness", describes thought as a continuous stream rather than a chain of separate ideas. Chapter XII divides the self into the material, the social and the spiritual. Chapter XXIV gives his theory of emotion, that "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble", and not the other way round.

The book starts with sensation and the nervous system and works up to attention, memory, reasoning, emotion and will. James treats psychology throughout as a natural science. He assumes that every state of mind has a state of the brain as its condition, and he says plainly that this is an assumption. The Epilogue then asks what the assumption leaves out, and answers: "This is no science, it is only the hope of a science." A reader should also know that his words "sensation", "fringe" and "specious present" are technical terms, each defined where it first appears. They are not loose descriptions.

This is a restored edition: James's words are unchanged. The Project Gutenberg transcription it is built from was compared with two Holt printings from the same plates, of 1893 and 1910, and where both agree against it this edition follows them. That corrected seven misread words, among them "Gallon" for Galton and "this hook" for this book. James refers back to his own pages more than sixty times; each reference now names the chapter it points to, or says "above" or "below". The figures are the full-size plates. The index and the page-numbered synopsis in the table of contents are omitted, and this edition adds a description of every figure, modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
