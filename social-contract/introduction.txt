Jean-Jacques Rousseau published this in Amsterdam in 1762 and signed the title page citizen of Geneva. Within weeks the Parlement of Paris had condemned it along with his book on education, Émile, and ordered his arrest, and Geneva, the city he had just named himself a citizen of, had both books burned by the public hangman. He fled, moved from one refuge to another for years, and renounced his Genevan citizenship the following year.

Its first sentence is the most quoted in political philosophy. Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains. It is nearly always used as a call to break the chains, and that is not what it is doing. Rousseau's next lines say he does not know how the change came about, and that the question he can answer is a different one: what could make the chains legitimate. The whole book is an attempt to describe the conditions under which obeying a government is not slavery, and it takes for granted that going back to the woods is not on offer.

The term that has done the most damage by being read loosely is the general will. It is not majority opinion and it is not what people happen to want. It is what a citizen wills when he asks what is good for the whole body he belongs to instead of what is good for himself, and Rousseau distinguishes it carefully from the will of all, which is only private interests added together. The limit is concrete: an act of the general will has to come from everyone and apply to everyone, so it can impose duties on a class of citizens and can never name a man. A decree about one person is not a law at all. His formula that a man who refuses to obey will be forced to be free has been quoted against him ever since, by people defending it and by people using it to explain where the guillotine came from.

The rest is short and fast. Sovereignty cannot be handed to representatives, which is why he says the English are free only during the election of members of parliament and are slaves the rest of the time. Democracy in his strict sense has never existed and would suit a people of gods rather than men. There is a long stretch on Roman institutions, the assemblies and the tribunate and the dictatorship and the censorship, which most abridgements cut and which is here in full, and a closing chapter on civil religion arguing that Christianity makes bad citizens because it points them at another world.

A generation later the French revolutionaries were quoting it. Hobbes' Leviathan is in this collection, and Rousseau argues with Hobbes by name, as he does with Grotius; so are Locke's Two Treatises and Burke's Reflections, which argues back at Rousseau.
