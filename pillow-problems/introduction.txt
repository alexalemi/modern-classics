Lewis Carroll published Pillow Problems in 1893, as the second part of a series he called Curiosa Mathematica. The seventy-two problems in it were all worked out the same way: lying awake at night, in the dark, in his head, before a diagram was drawn or a line written down. Geometry, algebra, and a good deal of probability. Five beggars divide a sack of pennies. A bag holds one counter, which is either black or white. A wine company's travelling salesman is quietly robbing it. A billiard table is triangular, with a pocket at each corner.

The Introduction is the reason to read the book. Carroll wants it understood that he is not recommending mathematics as a cure for insomnia. He has never had insomnia, and he changed the title from "sleepless nights" to "wakeful hours" because kind friends kept writing to sympathise with an illness he did not have. What he offers the problems as is a remedy for "the harassing thoughts that are apt to invade a wholly-unoccupied mind". Then he says which thoughts he means: sceptical ones that seem for a moment to uproot the firmest faith, blasphemous ones that dart unbidden into the most reverent souls, unholy ones that torture the fancy that would fain be pure. It is a careful and devout man telling you what happens to him at three in the morning, and two pages later the trigonometry starts.

He is very funny about himself. The Introduction ends with a list of his own errors in the earlier edition — in No. 55 he put "tan" for "sin"; elsewhere, he says, he was apparently under the delusion that a sin B sin C was the same thing as sin A times bc — and he hopes the reader will find more, since the pleasure of the discovery, and the intellectual superiority the reader will thereby recognise in himself over the author, may repay the time the book has cost him.

The book comes in three parts that share one numbering: the Questions, then the Answers, then the full Solutions. The mathematics has been reset in modern notation. Carroll's factorial sign was a vertical bar with the number written under it, and it is written 3! here; his decimal point sat at mid height and is now a full stop; "&c." is "etc.". That is the opposite of the decision taken for his Symbolic Logic, also in this collection, where the old vocabulary is kept because the argument is built out of it. Here the notation carries none of the argument. His own terms of art are untouched — a Tetragon is still a Tetragon — and the solutions have been left compressed, because their compression is the point of a book about what a mind can hold in the dark. Euclid and His Modern Rivals and A Tangled Tale are here too.
