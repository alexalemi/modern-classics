Harold Speed (1872–1957) was an English painter, best known for portraits, who trained at the Royal Academy Schools and later taught drawing himself. The Practice and Science of Drawing was published in London in 1913. He followed it in 1924 with a companion book on oil painting. The drawing book has been in print, in one edition or another, for most of the century since.

Speed's starting point is that drawing is not copying. What the eye receives is a pattern of light and colour, and a drawing is a way of expressing what we feel about it, so a drawing can be accurate and dead, or inaccurate and alive. He divides the subject in two. Line drawing works through contours and edges. Mass drawing works through tones, the way a painter sees. He treats each first in principle and then in practice, with exercises. The later chapters take up what makes a drawing hold together: rhythm, which he divides into variety and unity, then balance and proportion. He ends with portrait drawing, the training of the visual memory, the order of work, and materials from pencil to etching. A short appendix on proportion describes a series of lengths in which each is the sum of the two before, and how close its neighbours come to one fixed ratio.

The illustrations are part of the argument. Some are drawings and paintings by other artists, among them Leonardo, Botticelli, Holbein, Rubens, Velázquez, Watteau and Blake, reproduced from photographs, and some are Speed's own studies. Then there are many small diagrams he drew to show a single point, such as how a curve reads against a straight line or how a mass balances. A few things belong to 1913. The student is always "he," and Speed's judgments of other schools, the academic drawing of his day among them, are his own and are left as he wrote them.

This is a restored edition, and Speed's words are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the 1913 edition, compared word by word with two scanned copies of that printing. In two places both copies disagree with the transcription, a word it dropped and a letter it misread, and those two places follow the print. All eighty-eight illustrations are kept, taken from the larger of the two sizes the transcription provides. Each now has a description of what it shows, which also serves as its alt text. Where Speed points to an illustration or a later chapter by its page number, the reference now names the plate, diagram or chapter instead. The index, whose page numbers mean nothing in a reflowable book, is omitted. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
