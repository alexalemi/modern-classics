Benjamin Franklin published Poor Richard's Almanack every year from 1733 to 1758, under the name Richard Saunders. An almanac was a calendar with weather predictions, tide tables, sunrise times and the phases of the moon, and it was the one book a colonial household bought every year. Franklin filled the empty space around the dates with proverbs about work, money, debt and time. That is where early to bed and early to rise comes from, and the sleeping fox, and no gains without pains.

This is the preface he wrote for the last of those almanacs, in 1758. Instead of the usual note to the reader, he went back through twenty-five years of his own margins, picked out the best of the sayings, and put every one of them into a single speech. Richard Saunders stops his horse at a crowd waiting for an auction of merchants' goods. They are complaining about the times and about how heavy the taxes are, and one of them asks an old man with white hair what he makes of it. The old man, Father Abraham, answers at length, and every second sentence ends with the words as Poor Richard says. That refrain is the joke and it is kept here exactly, all the way through.

The proverbs themselves are mostly not Franklin's inventions. He took them from earlier English and European collections, from the Bible, and from common speech, and then cut them shorter and made them land. His versions are the ones people still say, which is what the editing was for. They are kept here in their familiar wording, with nothing paraphrased away.

The piece runs to a few thousand words and it is the most reprinted thing Franklin ever wrote. It was pulled straight back out of the almanac and printed on its own, as a pamphlet and as a broadside, on both sides of the Atlantic and then in translation. Later books about getting ahead by working hard and spending little are mostly working the same ground.

One warning, because it changes what the thing is. The speech is not the whole piece. Richard Saunders is standing there listening, and at the end he reports what the crowd did once Father Abraham stopped talking. Do not skip the last paragraph, and do not read it first.

Franklin's Autobiography, which describes how the almanac started and how much it earned him, is in this collection as a separate volume, as is Cato's Letters, the newspaper series he was reprinting in Boston at sixteen.
