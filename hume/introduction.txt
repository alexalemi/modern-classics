David Hume published this book in 1748, at thirty-seven, after the failure of a much longer one. His Treatise of Human Nature had come out eight years earlier and, in his own words, fell dead-born from the press. He concluded that the fault was his — too long, too intricate, published too young — and rewrote its first part as the twelve short sections here, aimed at an ordinary educated reader rather than at philosophers. It worked. This is the version that was read, argued with, and eventually credited with waking Kant up.

Very little in it sounds old, and that is the difficulty. Hume writes a clean, quick, often funny English that a modern reader glides through, and several of his terms are ordinary words doing technical work. A matter of fact is not something prosaic; it is a truth about the world that can only be known by experience, as opposed to a relation of ideas like a sum in arithmetic, and the distinction between those two is the hinge the whole book turns on. An impression is a sensation as you have it, an idea the fainter copy of it you have afterwards. Custom means habit, and it is doing more work than any other word in the book.

What he argues is that we have no rational grounds whatever for expecting the future to resemble the past. Every piece of reasoning about cause and effect rests on that expectation, and no argument can establish it without assuming it. We do it anyway, because we are creatures of habit rather than of reason, and Hume's point is not that we should stop but that we should be clear about what we are doing. Section Four is where it happens, and the sun failing to rise tomorrow is the example he uses.

Two sections caused trouble and both are here entire. Section Ten argues that no testimony has ever been, or could be, strong enough to establish a miracle, and closes by observing that the Christian religion cannot be believed by any reasonable person without one. Section Eleven puts an argument against divine providence into the mouth of a friend speaking in the person of Epicurus, a piece of deniability Hume built in deliberately. He was refused professorships at Edinburgh and Glasgow on the strength of writing like this, and he never held an academic post.

His famous sentences are already plain — custom is the great guide of human life, a wise man proportions his belief to the evidence — and where a sentence was already clear this edition has left it alone.
