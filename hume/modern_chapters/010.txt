Section Eight: Liberty and Necessity

(Part 1 of 3)

Part One

You might reasonably expect that in questions argued over with great eagerness ever since science and philosophy began, the disputants would at least have agreed on what the terms mean, and that in the course of two thousand years our enquiries would have got past the words to the real subject of the controversy. How easy it seems to give exact definitions of the terms used in an argument, and to make those definitions, rather than the mere sound of words, the thing examined afterwards. But look at the matter more closely and we shall be inclined to draw exactly the opposite conclusion. From the single fact that a controversy has been kept going a long time and is still undecided, we may guess that there is some ambiguity in the language, and that the disputants attach different ideas to the terms. Since the faculties of the mind are supposed to be naturally alike in everybody — otherwise nothing could be more pointless than reasoning or arguing together — it would be impossible, if people attached the same ideas to their terms, for them to go on holding different opinions about the same subject for so long, especially when they communicate their views and each side turns itself in every direction looking for arguments to defeat the other. It is true that if people take up questions lying entirely beyond human capacity — the origin of worlds, say, or the arrangement of the realm of spirits — they may beat the air in fruitless contests for a very long time and never arrive at any settled conclusion. But if the question concerns some subject of ordinary life and experience, one would think nothing could keep the dispute undecided so long except some ambiguous expressions, which hold the antagonists apart and stop them ever getting to grips with each other.

This has been the case in the long-disputed question of liberty and necessity — and to such a remarkable degree that, unless I am much mistaken, we shall find that all mankind, learned and ignorant alike, have always held the same opinion on the subject, and that a few intelligible definitions would have ended the whole controversy at once. I admit the dispute has been so thoroughly worked over on all sides, and has led philosophers into such a labyrinth of obscure sophistry, that it would be no wonder if a sensible reader spared himself the trouble and turned a deaf ear to a question he can expect neither instruction nor entertainment from. But the way the argument is set out here may perhaps renew his attention: it has more novelty, it promises at least some settling of the controversy, and it will not trouble him with any intricate or obscure reasoning.

So I hope to show that everyone has always agreed on the doctrine both of necessity and of liberty, on any reasonable sense that can be given to those terms, and that the whole controversy has so far turned merely on words. We shall begin by examining the doctrine of necessity.

It is universally granted that matter in everything it does is driven by a necessary force, and that every natural effect is so precisely determined by the energy of its cause that no other effect could possibly have followed in those particular circumstances. The degree and direction of every motion is prescribed by the laws of nature so exactly that a living creature could as easily arise from the collision of two bodies as a motion of any degree or direction other than the one actually produced. So if we want a just and precise idea of *necessity*, we must consider where the idea comes from when we apply it to what bodies do.

It seems clear that if all the scenes of nature were continually shifting in such a way that no two events resembled each other, and every object were entirely new with no likeness to anything ever seen before, we should never in that case have arrived at the least idea of necessity, or of a connection among these objects. On that supposition we might say that one object or event had followed another, but not that one was produced by the other. Cause and effect would be entirely unknown to mankind. Inference and reasoning about the operations of nature would end from that moment, and memory and the senses would remain the only channels by which knowledge of any real existence could reach the mind. Our idea of necessity and causation, then, arises entirely from the uniformity we can observe in nature's operations, where similar objects are constantly joined together and the mind is determined by custom to infer the one from the appearance of the other. These two circumstances are the whole of the necessity we attribute to matter. Beyond the constant *conjunction* of similar objects, and the *inference* we consequently make from one to the other, we have no notion of any necessity or connection at all.

So if it turns out that everyone has always granted, without doubt or hesitation, that these two circumstances occur in the voluntary actions of men and in the operations of the mind, it must follow that everyone has always agreed on the doctrine of necessity, and that they have been disputing merely for want of understanding each other.

As to the first circumstance, the constant and regular conjunction of similar events, the following considerations may satisfy us. It is universally acknowledged that there is a great uniformity in what people do, in all nations and ages, and that human nature stays the same in its principles and operations. The same motives always produce the same actions; the same events follow from the same causes. Ambition, greed, self-love, vanity, friendship, generosity, public spirit — these passions, mixed in various proportions and spread through society, have been from the beginning of the world, and still are, the source of every action and enterprise ever observed among mankind. Would you like to know the feelings, inclinations and way of life of the Greeks and Romans? Study the temper and behaviour of the French and English closely: you cannot go far wrong in transferring to the first *most* of the observations you have made about the second. Mankind is so much the same at all times and in all places that history tells us nothing new or strange in this respect. Its chief use is simply to reveal the constant and universal principles of human nature, by showing men in every variety of circumstance and supplying us with material from which to form our observations and get to know the regular springs of human behaviour. These records of wars, intrigues, factions and revolutions are so many collections of experiments, by which the politician or moral philosopher fixes the principles of his science, in just the way the physician or natural philosopher gets to know the nature of plants and minerals by the experiments he makes on them. The earth and water and other elements Aristotle and Hippocrates examined are no more like the ones we observe today than the men Polybius and Tacitus describe are like the men who govern the world now.

If a traveller returning from a distant country brought us an account of men entirely different from any we had ever known — men wholly free of greed, ambition or vengefulness, who knew no pleasure but friendship, generosity and public spirit — we should detect the falsehood immediately from those very details, and convict him of lying with the same certainty as if he had stuffed his story with centaurs and dragons. And if we want to demolish a forgery in history, we can use no more convincing argument than to prove that the actions attributed to some person run directly contrary to the course of nature, and that no human motive in those circumstances could ever have led him to behave so. Quintus Curtius is as much to be doubted when he describes the supernatural courage of Alexander, which drove him to attack multitudes single-handed, as when he describes the supernatural strength that let him withstand them. So readily and so universally do we acknowledge a uniformity in human motives and actions, as much as in the operations of bodies.

Hence too the value of the experience gained by a long life and a wide variety of business and company, which teaches us the principles of human nature and regulates our conduct as well as our speculation. Guided by it, we work up from people's actions, expressions and even gestures to a knowledge of their inclinations and motives, and then back down again to the interpretation of their actions from what we know of their motives. The general observations stored up over a course of experience give us the key to human nature and teach us to unravel all its intricacies. Pretexts and appearances no longer deceive us. Public declarations pass for the plausible colouring of a cause. And though virtue and honour are allowed their proper weight, that perfect disinterestedness which is so often claimed is never expected of crowds and parties, seldom of their leaders, and scarcely even of individuals of any rank. But if there were no uniformity in human actions, and every experiment we could make of this kind came out irregular and anomalous, it would be impossible to gather any general observations about mankind, and no experience, however carefully digested by reflection, would ever serve any purpose. Why is the old farmer more skilful at his work than the young beginner, except that there is a certain uniformity in the way sun and rain and earth work together to produce crops, and experience has taught the old hand the rules that govern it?

We must not, however, expect this uniformity of human action to be carried so far that everyone in the same circumstances will always behave in exactly the same way, with no allowance made for differences of character, prejudice and opinion. Such uniformity in every particular is found nowhere in nature. On the contrary, observing the variety of conduct in different people is what enables us to form a greater variety of maxims — maxims which still assume a degree of uniformity and regularity.

Are the manners of men different in different ages and countries? From that we learn the great force of custom and education, which mould the human mind from infancy and form it into a settled character. Is the behaviour of the one sex very unlike that of the other? It is from this that we come to know the different characters nature has stamped on the sexes and preserves with constancy and regularity. Are the actions of the same person very different at different periods of his life, from infancy to old age? That gives room for many general observations about the gradual change in our feelings and inclinations, and the different maxims that prevail at different ages. Even the characteristics peculiar to each individual have a uniformity in their influence — otherwise our acquaintance with people and our observation of their conduct could never teach us their dispositions, or help us direct our own behaviour towards them.

I grant it is possible to find some actions that seem to have no regular connection with any known motive, and that are exceptions to every rule of conduct ever laid down. But if we want to know what judgement to form about such irregular and extraordinary actions, we may consider what people commonly think about the irregular events that turn up in the course of nature. Not all causes are joined to their usual effects with the same uniformity. A craftsman handling nothing but dead matter can be disappointed of his aim, just as much as the politician directing the conduct of intelligent agents.

Ordinary people, who take things as they first appear, put the uncertainty of events down to an uncertainty in the causes, which they suppose often fail of their usual influence even though nothing obstructs them. But philosophers, noticing that almost every part of nature contains a vast variety of hidden springs and principles — hidden because they are too small or too far off to see — find that the contrariety of events may at least possibly come not from any contingency in the cause but from the secret working of contrary causes. Further observation turns this possibility into a certainty, when they notice that on exact examination a contrariety of effects always betrays a contrariety of causes, and comes from their opposing one another. A peasant can give no better reason for a clock stopping than to say it does not usually keep good time; but a craftsman easily sees that the same force in the spring or pendulum always has the same influence on the wheels, and fails of its usual effect only because of, say, a grain of dust that has brought the whole movement to a halt. From observing several parallel instances, philosophers form the maxim that the connection between all causes and effects is equally necessary, and that its apparent uncertainty in some cases comes from the secret opposition of contrary causes.

So, for instance, in the human body, when the usual symptoms of health or sickness disappoint our expectation, when medicines do not work with their accustomed power, when irregular events follow from some particular cause — the philosopher and the physician are not surprised, and are never tempted to deny in general the necessity and uniformity of the principles governing the animal economy. They know that a human body is an immensely complicated machine; that many hidden powers lurk in it entirely beyond our comprehension; that it must often look very uncertain in its operations to us; and that therefore the irregular events that show themselves outwardly are no proof that the laws of nature are not being observed with the greatest regularity in its inner workings.
