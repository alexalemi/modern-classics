Section Six: Probability

Though there is no such thing as *Chance* in the world, our ignorance of the real cause of an event has the same effect on the understanding and produces the same sort of belief.

There is certainly a kind of probability that comes from having more chances on one side than the other; and as that superiority grows and outstrips the opposing chances, the probability rises in proportion and produces a still higher degree of belief in the favoured side. If a die were marked with one number of spots on four faces and another number on the remaining two, the first would be more likely to come up than the second — and if it had a thousand faces marked alike and only one face different, the probability would be far higher and our expectation steadier and more secure. This process of thought may look trivial and obvious, but anyone who examines it more closely may find something worth curious speculation in it.

It seems clear that when the mind looks ahead to see what will come of throwing such a die, it treats the turning up of each particular face as equally likely — and that is exactly what chance is, the making of every particular outcome inside it entirely equal. But finding that more faces point to one result than to the other, the mind is carried more often to that result, and meets it more frequently as it runs over the various possibilities the final outcome depends on. This convergence of several views on one particular result immediately produces, by some inexplicable contrivance of nature, the feeling of belief, and gives that result the advantage over its rival, which fewer views support and which comes back to the mind less often. If we grant that belief is nothing but a firmer and stronger conception of an object than the one attending the mere fictions of imagination, this operation can perhaps be accounted for to some extent. The convergence of these several views stamps the idea more strongly on the imagination, gives it superior force, makes its influence on the passions more perceptible, and in a word produces the reliance or security that belief consists in.

Probability from causes works the same way as probability from chance. Some causes are entirely uniform and constant in producing a particular effect, and no instance of failure or irregularity in them has ever been found. Fire has always burned, and water always drowned every human creature; the production of motion by impact and by gravity is a universal law that has so far admitted no exception. But other causes have been found more irregular and uncertain: rhubarb has not always purged, nor opium put to sleep everyone who took it. It is true that when a cause fails to produce its usual effect, philosophers do not put this down to any irregularity in nature, but suppose that some hidden cause in the particular structure of the parts has prevented it from working. Our reasoning and our conclusions about the event, however, are the same as if no such principle existed. Since custom determines us to carry the past over to the future in all our inferences, where the past has been entirely regular we expect the event with the greatest confidence and leave no room for any contrary supposition. But where different effects have been found to follow causes that are to *appearance* exactly alike, all these various effects must occur to the mind as it carries the past over to the future, and must be taken into account when we settle how probable the event is. Though we prefer the effect that has been found most usual and believe it will happen, we must not overlook the others; each of them must be given its own weight and authority in proportion to how frequently we have found it. In almost every country in Europe it is more probable that there will be frost at some point in January than that the whole month will stay mild — though this probability varies with the climate and comes near certainty in the more northern kingdoms.

Here, then, it seems clear that when we carry the past over to the future in order to settle what effect a cause will have, we carry over all the different outcomes in the same proportions they appeared in before, conceiving one to have happened a hundred times, say, another ten times, and another once. And since a great many views converge here on one outcome, they fortify and confirm it to the imagination, produce the feeling we call *belief*, and give its object the preference over the contrary outcome, which fewer trials support and which does not come back to the mind so often as we carry the past over to the future. Let anyone try to account for this operation of the mind on any of the received systems of philosophy, and he will feel the difficulty. For my part I shall be satisfied if these hints arouse the curiosity of philosophers, and make them aware how inadequate all the usual theories are on subjects so curious and so lofty.
