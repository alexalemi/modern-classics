Section Five: A Sceptical Solution to These Doubts

(Part 2 of 2)

If we tried to give a *definition* of this feeling, we should probably find the task very difficult if not impossible — much as if we tried to define the feeling of cold, or the passion of anger, to a creature who had never experienced them. Belief is the true and proper name of this feeling, and nobody is ever at a loss for what the word means, because every man is conscious of the feeling at every moment. It may not be out of place, though, to attempt a *description* of it, in the hope of arriving at some analogies that explain it better. What I say is that belief is nothing but a more vivid, lively, forcible, firm and steady conception of an object than the imagination alone can ever manage. This string of terms may look unphilosophical, but it is meant only to express the act of mind that makes realities — or what we take for realities — more present to us than fictions, gives them more weight in our thinking, and lets them exert more influence over the passions and the imagination. As long as we agree about the thing, there is no need to argue about the words.

The imagination has command over all its ideas and can join and mix and vary them in every possible way. It can conceive fictitious objects with all the circumstances of place and time. It can set them before our eyes, so to speak, in their true colours, exactly as they might have existed. But since it is impossible for imagination on its own ever to reach belief, plainly belief consists not in the particular nature or arrangement of the ideas but in the *manner* of conceiving them, and in how they *feel* to the mind. I confess it is impossible to explain this feeling, or manner of conception, perfectly. We can use words that come near it. But its true and proper name, as I said, is *belief* — a term everybody understands well enough in ordinary life. And in philosophy we can go no further than to assert that *belief* is something felt by the mind, which marks off the ideas of judgement from the fictions of the imagination. It gives them more weight and influence, makes them seem more important, presses them on the mind, and makes them the governing principle of what we do. At this moment, for instance, I hear the voice of somebody I know, and the sound seems to come from the next room. This impression on my senses immediately carries my thought to the person, and to everything around him. I picture them to myself as existing right now, with the same qualities and relations I knew them to have before. These ideas take a firmer hold on my mind than the idea of an enchanted castle. They feel quite different, and they have far greater influence of every kind, whether to give pleasure or pain, joy or sorrow.

So let us take in the whole scope of this doctrine, and grant that the feeling of belief is nothing but a conception more intense and steady than the one attending the mere fictions of imagination, and that this *manner* of conception arises from a customary conjunction of the object with something present to the memory or senses. On those assumptions it will not be hard, I think, to find other operations of the mind that work the same way, and to trace all these phenomena back to still more general principles.

We have already observed that nature has established connections among particular ideas, and that as soon as one idea occurs to us it introduces its partner and draws our attention towards it by a gentle, imperceptible movement. We reduced these principles of connection to three — *Resemblance*, *Contiguity* and *Causation* — which are the only bonds tying our thoughts together and producing the regular train of reflection or conversation that goes on, more or less, in everybody. Now a question arises here on which the solution of the present difficulty depends. Does it happen, in all these relations, that when one of the objects is presented to the senses or memory, the mind is not only carried to conceive its partner but reaches a steadier and stronger conception of it than it could otherwise have managed? That seems to be the case with the belief arising from cause and effect. And if it holds for the other relations too, it may be set down as a general law governing everything the mind does.

For a first experiment to that purpose, notice that when the picture of an absent friend appears, our idea of him is plainly enlivened by the *resemblance*, and every feeling the idea arouses, whether joy or sorrow, gains new force. Two things combine to produce this effect: a relation, and a present impression. Where the picture bears him no resemblance, or at any rate was not meant for him, it does not so much as carry our thought to him. And where the picture is absent as well as the person, then although the mind may pass from the thought of one to the thought of the other, it finds its idea weakened rather than enlivened by the transition. We take pleasure in looking at a friend's picture when it is in front of us; but once it is taken away, we would rather think about him directly than through the reflection of an image equally distant and equally dim.

The ceremonies of the Roman Catholic religion may be considered instances of the same thing. The devotees of that superstition usually plead, in excuse for the mummeries they are reproached with, that they feel the good effect of those outward motions and postures and actions in enlivening their devotion and quickening a fervour that would otherwise fade if it were directed entirely at distant and immaterial objects. We sketch out the objects of our faith, they say, in perceptible types and images, and by the immediate presence of these types make them more present to us than we possibly could by intellectual contemplation alone. Objects we can see and touch always have more influence on the fancy than anything else, and they readily pass that influence on to the ideas they are related to and resemble. All I shall infer from these practices and this reasoning is that the effect of resemblance in enlivening ideas is very common; and since in every case a resemblance and a present impression have to combine, we have experiments in abundance to prove the principle.

We can add force to these experiments with others of a different kind, by considering the effects of *contiguity* as well as of *resemblance*. Distance certainly weakens the force of every idea, and as we approach an object, even before it shows itself to our senses, it works on the mind with an influence that imitates an immediate impression. Thinking about any object readily carries the mind to whatever is next to it; but only the actual presence of an object carries it with superior liveliness. When I am a few miles from home, everything to do with home touches me more nearly than when I am two hundred leagues away — though even at that distance, thinking about anything near my friends or family naturally produces an idea of them. But in the second case both objects before the mind are ideas, and although the transition between them is easy, the transition alone cannot give either of them superior liveliness, for want of some immediate impression.

Nobody can doubt that causation works the same way as resemblance and contiguity. Superstitious people are fond of the relics of saints and holy men for the same reason that they look for images: to enliven their devotion and get a closer, stronger conception of the exemplary lives they want to imitate. Now plainly one of the best relics a devotee could get hold of would be something a saint had made with his own hands — and if his clothes and furniture are ever regarded in that light, it is because they were once his, and were handled and affected by him, so that they count as imperfect effects, connected to him by a shorter chain of consequences than anything else we learn his existence from.

Suppose the son of a friend long dead or long absent were brought before us. Plainly the sight would instantly revive the associated idea, and call back all our old intimacies in livelier colours than they would otherwise have appeared in. That is another phenomenon which seems to prove the principle.

Notice that in all these cases the belief in the associated object is always presupposed, and without it the relation could have no effect at all. The influence of the picture assumes that we *believe* our friend once existed. Being near home can never excite our ideas of home unless we *believe* home really exists. Now I maintain that this belief, where it reaches beyond memory and the senses, is of the same nature and arises from the same causes as the transition of thought and liveliness of conception just described. When I throw a dry piece of wood on a fire, my mind is immediately carried to the thought that it will feed the flame, not put it out. That transition of thought from cause to effect does not come from reason; it originates entirely in custom and experience. And because it starts from an object present to the senses, it makes the idea of flame stronger and livelier than any loose, floating daydream. The idea arises at once. The thought moves instantly towards it, carrying to it all the force of conception derived from the present impression. When a sword is levelled at my chest, does not the idea of a wound and of pain strike me more forcibly than when a glass of wine is held out to me — even supposing the same idea happened to occur to me after the wine? But what is there in the whole business to produce so strong a conception, except a present object and a customary transition to the idea of another object we are used to finding joined with it? That is the whole operation of the mind in all our conclusions about matters of fact and existence, and it is a satisfaction to find some analogies that help explain it. The transition from a present object always gives strength and solidity to the related idea.

So here is a sort of pre-established harmony between the course of nature and the succession of our ideas. Though the powers and forces governing nature are entirely unknown to us, our thoughts and conceptions have nevertheless, we find, run along in the same track as the rest of nature's work. Custom is the principle by which this correspondence has been brought about — so necessary to the survival of our species and to the regulation of our conduct in every situation life produces. If the presence of an object had not instantly aroused the idea of the objects commonly joined with it, all our knowledge would have been confined to the narrow sphere of memory and the senses, and we could never have fitted means to ends, or used our natural powers either to produce good or to avoid harm. Those who delight in discovering and contemplating *final causes* have ample material here for their wonder.

Let me add one more confirmation of this theory. Since the operation of mind by which we infer like effects from like causes, and the reverse, is so essential to the survival of every human creature, it is unlikely that it could have been entrusted to the unreliable deductions of our reason — which works slowly, does not appear at all in the first years of infancy, and at best is extremely liable to error at every age. It is more in keeping with nature's ordinary wisdom to secure so necessary an act of the mind by some instinct or mechanical tendency, which can be infallible in its working, can show itself at the first appearance of life and thought, and can be independent of all the laboured deductions of the understanding. Just as nature has taught us the use of our limbs without giving us any knowledge of the muscles and nerves that move them, so she has planted in us an instinct that carries our thought forward on a course matching the one she has established among external objects — even though we know nothing of the powers and forces on which that regular course and succession of objects entirely depends.
