Harlan Tarbell (1890–1960) was an American magician, illustrator and lecturer. In the 1920s he wrote a correspondence course in magic, issued to subscribers in instalments by Tarbell System, Inc., which copyrighted it in 1927. It taught sleight of hand and stage magic step by step, every move shown in his own drawings. It became one of the best-known courses in magic, and many American magicians learned the craft from it.

In 1941 the New York magic dealer Louis Tannen began republishing the course as a set of books, revised and expanded with new effects contributed by working magicians, under the editorship of Ralph W. Read. This is the second volume, published in 1942, covering Lessons 20 to 33. Tarbell opens it with a lesson on how to please an audience, on showmanship, timing, speech, dress and presentation, and then teaches, trick by trick, magic with wands, double paper cones, coins, cigarettes, cards, rising cards, eggs, billiard balls, silk handkerchiefs and ropes, "Ghostlite" effects for a darkened room, and full stage illusions.

The method is the same throughout. Each trick gives the effect as the audience sees it, the apparatus needed, the preparation, and the performance, with the magician's patter set in italics. The drawings carry as much of the teaching as the words: a palm, a fold or a thread is shown at each stage, and Tarbell restarts his figure numbers with each trick. Many effects are credited to the magicians who devised them, such as Cardini, Thurston and Al Baker, which makes the book a record of American magic at mid-century as well as a manual.

Some of what it describes is of its time. Several tricks are built around cigarettes, and the prices, props and theatre arrangements are those of the 1940s.

This is a restored edition of Volume II as published in 1942, from a scan of a later printing of the same text. The words were read from the scan's OCR and corrected page by page against the page images. All of Tarbell's drawings, more than a thousand, were cut from the scan and set where they stand in the text, each with the number he printed and a short description written for this edition. The title and copyright pages, the contents and a closing advertisement for Volume I are omitted; the publisher's farewell note is kept. The volume's copyright was registered in 1942 and never renewed, so it is in the public domain in the United States.
