TEXT ANALYSIS — The Mysterious Island
(Jules Verne, L'Île mystérieuse, 1874–1875; Hetzel)
TRANSLATED FROM THE FRENCH. The fourth book of the "Verne recovery"
strand, after Twenty Thousand Leagues (twenty-thousand-leagues/), Around
the World in Eighty Days (eighty-days/) and A Journey to the Center of the
Earth (journey-center-earth/). THIS BOOK IS THE SEQUEL TO TWENTY THOUSAND
LEAGUES: every name and term Nemo shares with that book is locked to
twenty-thousand-leagues' renderings (see LOCKED TERMINOLOGY).

WHY THIS BOOK, AND WHY IT MATTERS THAT IT IS COMPLETE

The Mysterious Island is the purest engineering fantasy ever written:
five Union prisoners escape besieged Richmond by balloon in March 1865,
are blown 7,000 miles across the Pacific, and land on an uncharted
volcanic island with nothing — not a knife, not a match. Cyrus Smith,
engineer, rebuilds civilization from first principles: fire, pottery,
bricks, iron, steel, glass, soap, candles, nitroglycerin, guncotton, a
hydraulic elevator, a telegraph. Every chapter is a problem solved with
what is to hand. Around that runs a mystery — someone on the island is
helping them — which resolves in Part Three into the death of Captain
Nemo, the sequel to Twenty Thousand Leagues and the place where Verne
finally tells who Nemo was: Prince Dakkar, an Indian prince who led the
rising of 1857 against British rule.

The two public-domain English versions fail it in opposite directions.
Kingston (1875) is the one reprinted for a century, and it censors the
book's climax for a British market. White (1876) keeps Nemo but cuts the
engineering, which is the book's pleasure. A complete, accurate, modern
retelling restores both halves.

THE VOICES — six people, a dog and an orangutan

- CYRUS SMITH — the engineer. Massachusetts-born, a scientist of the
  first rank whom the Union put in charge of its railways during the
  war; a Union staff officer when captured; abolitionist "by reason and by
  heart".
  Calm, precise, kind, never flustered, explains as he works. Verne's
  hero of applied reason; never smug. Narration calls him "the engineer"
  as often as by name — keep that.
- GÉDÉON SPILETT — war correspondent of the New York Herald, cool under
  fire, a crack shot, a sketcher, takes notes on everything (he keeps
  the colony's records). Dry, brave, a little sardonic. He calls the
  engineer "my dear Cyrus".
- PENCROFF — the sailor (his baptismal name is Bonadventure, which is
  how the boat gets its name in II.12), from the Vineyard (the
  whaling island off Massachusetts). The book's comic engine and its
  heart: loud, superstitious, boastful, devoted, a pipe-smoker whose
  greatest tragedy is having no tobacco. Salty, quick, warm, never a
  clown — he is the best practical hand in the colony. "Mr. Cyrus",
  "Mr. Spilett".
- HARBERT BROWN — fifteen, son of Pencroff's late captain, Pencroff's
  ward; a keen young naturalist who names every plant and animal (the
  book's Conseil, without the comedy). Earnest, brave, eager.
- NAB (Nebuchadnezzar) — born on Smith's family estate to enslaved
  parents, freed long ago by Smith, who chose to stay with him and
  crossed the Confederate lines into Richmond to be near him. Thirty,
  strong, gentle, cheerful, the colony's cook. VERNE GIVES NAB NO
  DIALECT. His French is plain, correct, often warm and sometimes dry
  ("Eh! Pencroff, if that's all the game you promised my master, it
  won't take much of a fire to roast it"). Render it so. No invented
  dialect, no "Massa" — that was the Victorian translators' doing, not
  Verne's. See SENSITIVE CONTENT for every passage that touches race.
- AYRTON — the man marooned on Tabor Island, quartermaster of the
  Britannia turned mutineer under the name Ben Joyce (Captain Grant's
  Children), found reduced to a wild state after twelve years alone;
  redeemed by the colonists. Grave, ashamed, laconic, then steadily
  braver. His remorse is the book's moral center.
- CAPTAIN NEMO — appears only in III.15–17: old, dying, formal,
  magnificent, bitter, still burning. Same voice as twenty-thousand-
  leagues' Nemo (brilliant, formal, wounded, never campy).
- TOP — Smith's dog. JUP — the orangutan who becomes the household's
  servant ("Master Jup"). Both are characters; give them their comedy.

STYLE AND TONE

- ADVENTURE AND INGENUITY. Clear, propulsive modern prose; Verne's long
  periods broken to natural rhythm; contractions welcome in dialogue,
  fine in narration. The narrator is third-person, omniscient, with
  Verne's wink ("as we shall see").
- THE ENGINEERING IS THE BOOK. Every process stays complete and in
  order: the watch-glass burning lens; the pottery kiln and the
  brick count; the iron smelting by the Catalan method and the steel;
  the pyrites, sulfuric acid, nitric acid, glycerin, soap and
  nitroglycerin of I.17; the granite-wall triangulation of I.14 and the
  latitude/longitude work; the candles, the glass, the elevator, the
  boat's specifications (keel, beam, draft, rig), the windmill, the
  telegraph battery, the guncotton. Do not compress a single step.
  A process cut to "they made glass" is the summarization failure in
  its worst form, because it reads smoothly. Make it LUCID: a bright
  fourteen-year-old should be able to follow every step. Use the plain
  modern chemical name where one exists (azotic acid = nitric acid;
  potassium nitrate/saltpeter; iron sulfate) — Verne's science stays as
  he wrote it otherwise (no silent corrections, dated or not).
- NATURAL HISTORY likewise: Harbert's species (tragopans, couroucous,
  kangaroos, peccaries, onaggas, koulas, culpeos, the capybara) stay,
  with the standard English common name and Verne's Latin where he gives
  it. Follow twenty-thousand-leagues' marine glossary where it overlaps
  (dugong, sperm whale, right whale, etc.).
- "Colonists" for les colons — Verne's word and the book's idea (they
  colonize a desert island); "castaways" for les naufragés.

UNITS — THIS BOOK IS THE INVERSE OF THE OTHER VERNE NOVELS

The precedents keep Verne's units as he wrote them: twenty-thousand-
leagues kept metric plus his feet/miles; journey-center-earth kept
metric, rendered toise as "fathom" and pouces as inches; eighty-days
kept miles and pounds and DROPPED Verne's franc conversions of sterling
sums. Alex's later rule (Euler, modern-units memory) converts OBSOLETE
measures to modern US units. Here Verne wrote AMERICAN units, because
the cast is American — pieds (225), milles (131+), yards, acres,
gallons, pints, bushels, pounds, grains, degrees Fahrenheit — and then
added metric conversions FOR FRENCH READERS, in parentheses and in
footnotes. For an English reader the American unit is already the
modern one. So:
- KEEP feet, inches (pouces), miles, yards, acres, gallons, pints,
  bushels, pounds (livres), grains, fathoms (brasses), knots, and
  degrees Fahrenheit, with Verne's numbers unchanged.
- DROP Verne's centigrade parentheticals after a Fahrenheit reading
  ("huit degrés Fahrenheit (13 degrés centigrades au-dessous de zéro)"
  -> "eight degrees Fahrenheit") — the eighty-days franc rule exactly.
- CONVERT the one stand-alone centigrade reading (I.16: "a centigrade
  thermometer ... ten to twelve degrees above zero") to Fahrenheit
  ("fifty to fifty-four degrees") — all temperatures in this book are
  Fahrenheit once translated, as the colonists' own thermometer is.
- encablures -> "cable lengths" (Pencroff's world; a cable is about 200
  yards — gloss once in the sentence if the distance matters).
- lieues (outside the Twenty Thousand Leagues title): convert at Verne's
  own 4 km = about 2.5 miles. Norfolk Island, "six lieues de tour" ->
  "some fifteen miles around" (III.2).
- Latitudes, longitudes and the arithmetic (I.14's 333-foot wall, the
  wheat-grain multiplication, Washington's 77° 3′ 11″) stay exactly.
- Say what was done in the editor's introduction.

VERNE'S SLIPS — KEPT AS WRITTEN (the Verne rule: no silent corrections)
- II.9: Cyrus Smith says Tabor Island lies "about a hundred and fifty
  miles to the northeast". By the bottle's coordinates (Tabor 37°11′ S,
  153° W; Lincoln 34°57′ S, 150°30′ W) it lies to the SOUTHWEST. Keep
  "northeast".
- II.9 recalls the first fix (I.14) as "longitude 150° to 155° west,
  latitude 30° to 35° south"; I.14 itself said between the 35th and
  37th parallels. Keep both as written. (eighty-days reconciled a date
  given two ways for one event because its plot is a clock; nothing
  here depends on the inconsistency, and Alex's brief says keep.)
- I.2 footnote: "On April 5, Richmond fell to Grant" — Richmond fell on
  April 3, 1865. The note is folded into the text (FOOTNOTES) with
  Verne's date as he gave it.
- III.16: Nemo kept Aronnax "seven months" (Twenty Thousand Leagues
  says ten), and the chronology of Nemo's age and of Ayrton's twelve
  years does not square with the earlier books. Verne's publisher
  printed two notes admitting it (II.17, III.16). Keep the text as
  written; drop the notes (see FOOTNOTES); the editor's introduction
  should mention the discordance.
- III.16 footnote 2 / II.10 footnote 2: see FOOTNOTES (one is a factual
  slip we must not fold in).

SENSITIVE CONTENT — scholarly modernization of a canonical historical
text. Measured register; the author's argument rendered as HIS; never
sensationalized, never bowdlerized. Decisions per passage, by the
project's test (does the reader LOSE information or GAIN a false one?),
logged here and in running_notes.txt. FLAGGED FOR ALEX where marked.

1. NEMO'S ANTI-COLONIAL HISTORY (III.16) — FULL STRENGTH, NO NOTE. This
   is the book's subject and the passage Kingston censored. Prince
   Dakkar's hatred of England, the Sepoy Rebellion as a war of
   independence ("those heroes who had risen to free their country"),
   the price on his head, his family killed in his place, "Right, once
   again, had fallen before might", the English frigate he sank — all
   as Verne wrote it, Verne's sympathies and all. Verne's own aside —
   "but civilization never retreats, and it seems to borrow every right
   from necessity" — stays too: it is Verne's ambivalence, and the
   reader needs it. "Indien"/"Indou" -> "Indian"/"Hindu" as Verne uses
   them.
2. THE CIVIL WAR AND SLAVERY. Verne's colonists are Union men and the
   book is abolitionist ("America's immense effort against America
   itself, to abolish slavery" is what reconciles Nemo to humanity).
   "Sudistes" = "the Southerners"/"the Confederates"; "les séparatistes"
   = "the secessionists". Lincoln's assassination "by a fanatic" kept.
3. NAB — the word. Verne's narrator often calls Nab "le nègre" in place
   of his name, as he calls Pencroff "the sailor" and Spilett "the
   reporter". A dated WORD, not a dated claim (the Mill / Hume
   precedent): render it "Nab" (or "Nab, a Black man" where the
   description itself is the point, as in his introduction in I.2).
   Never the period noun.
4. NAB — his history (I.2): born to enslaved parents on Smith's estate,
   freed by Smith "an abolitionist by reason and by heart", stayed out
   of love, crossed the lines to Richmond. KEEP ENTIRE — it is the
   author's subject and it answers for itself.
5. NAB — "mon maître". Nab calls Smith "my master" (about 10 times; the
   cry when he finds Smith alive is "Mon maître ! mon maître !"). In
   English from a freed Black man this lands hard; but it is exactly
   the relationship Verne depicts — a free man who chose to stay — and
   Verne has said so two pages earlier. RECOMMENDATION: keep "my
   master". FLAGGED FOR ALEX (alternative: "Mr. Cyrus", which erases
   the difference Verne draws between Nab and Pencroff).
6. NAB — two racial asides, CUT per the Boltzmann ruling (an aside, not
   the subject; the reader gains a false impression, loses nothing):
   (a) II.2: "Nab dansait... comme un nègre" -> "Nab danced for joy"
       (the dance stays, the simile goes);
   (b) I.12: "Nab and Pencroff, to whom the cooking naturally fell, the
       one in his capacity as a Negro, the other in his capacity as a
       sailor" -> "Nab and Pencroff, the colony's natural cooks" — the
       cooking stays, the racial attribution goes. (Nab is the cook
       because he is Smith's servant; Pencroff because ships' crews
       cook — that much survives.)
   FLAGGED FOR ALEX.
7. NAB — "montrant ses dents étincelantes de blancheur" (I.11, when he
   names Claw Cape). A stock period description; render "with a
   dazzling grin" — the joy stays. FLAGGED (judgment call, minor).
8. NAB AND JUP. II.8: Nab trains Jup and "one would have said the
   negro and the ape understood each other when they talked together".
   The friendship is plot; the noun makes it a sneer. Render with the
   name ("Nab and the ape seemed to understand each other") — the
   Mill rule again. The "ton supérieur" exchange (II.9):
     Pencroff: "What a servant, Nab! What a servant!"
     Nab: "My pupil — and soon my equal!"
     Pencroff, laughing: "Your superior, you mean — because you talk,
     Nab, and he doesn't!"
   KEEP WHOLE. It is a servant-backtalk joke (Jup never answers back),
   Pencroff's teasing of a friend he teases constantly, and Verne makes
   the reason explicit in the line. Rendered with its reason intact it
   reads as the joke Verne wrote. FLAGGED FOR ALEX because of its
   proximity to (8) above.
9. THE CONVICTS. Escaped Norfolk Island convicts turned pirates; Verne
   is merciless to them and generous to the one who repents (Ayrton).
   Render the violence (Harbert shot, the corral, the bodies found)
   plainly, without relish. "Convicts" is Verne's English word.
10. "MALAIS". The colonists fear Malay pirates — real 1860s piracy in
   those seas; keep. One narrator's comparison generalizes the people:
   "plus féroces que les Malais eux-mêmes" (III.2) -> "fiercer even
   than the Malay pirates" — the comparison to the pirates stays, the
   slur on a people goes.
11. AYRTON'S WILD STATE (II.14–16): "un sauvage" = "a wild man" — it is
   about a man reduced by solitude, not about any people.
12. Animals: hunting, the dugong, the whale, the jaguar — as written.
