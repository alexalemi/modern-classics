Chapter 16: Captain Nemo

_Captain Nemo — His first words — The story of a hero of independence — His hatred of the invaders — His companions — Life beneath the sea — Alone — The Nautilus's last refuge at Lincoln Island — The mysterious genie of the island_

At these words the man lying on the divan raised himself, and his face came into the full light: a magnificent head, a high forehead, a proud gaze, a white beard, and thick hair swept back from his brow.

He leaned with one hand on the back of the divan he had just left. His gaze was calm. It was plain that some slow illness had been wearing him down little by little, but his voice still sounded strong when he spoke, in English, in a tone of the utmost surprise:

"I have no name, sir."

"I know you!" answered Cyrus Smith.

Captain Nemo fixed a burning look on the engineer, as though he meant to annihilate him.

Then, sinking back onto the cushions of the divan, he murmured:

"What does it matter, after all? I am going to die!"

Cyrus Smith went up to Captain Nemo, and Gédéon Spilett took his hand, which he found burning hot. Ayrton, Pencroff, Harbert and Nab stood respectfully apart in a corner of that magnificent salon, whose air was charged with electricity.

But Captain Nemo at once drew his hand away, and with a gesture he asked the engineer and the reporter to sit down.

They all looked at him with real emotion. So here he was, the one they had called "the genie of the island," the powerful being whose help had come so effectively on so many occasions, the benefactor to whom they owed so great a debt of gratitude! Before their eyes they had only a man, where Pencroff and Nab had expected to find something close to a god — and that man was about to die!

But how did Cyrus Smith come to know Captain Nemo? And why had the captain started up so sharply on hearing that name, which he must have believed unknown to everyone?...

The captain had settled back on the divan and, propped on his arm, he was looking at the engineer, who sat beside him.

"You know the name I once bore, sir?" he asked.

"I know it," answered Cyrus Smith, "just as I know the name of this wonderful submarine machine..."

"The Nautilus?" said the captain, with half a smile.

"The Nautilus."

"But do you know... do you know who I am?"

"I know."

"And yet it has been thirty years since I had any communication with the inhabited world — thirty years that I have lived in the depths of the sea, the only place where I have found independence! Who, then, can have betrayed my secret?"

"A man who had never made you any promise, Captain Nemo, and who therefore cannot be accused of betrayal."

"That Frenchman whom chance threw aboard my ship sixteen years ago?"

"The same."

"Then that man and his two companions did not die in the Maelström, where the Nautilus had been caught?"

"They did not die. And a book has appeared, under the title Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea, that contains your story."

"My story for a few months only, sir!" the captain answered sharply.

"That's true," Cyrus Smith went on, "but a few months of that strange life were enough to make you known..."

"As a great criminal, no doubt?" answered Captain Nemo, letting a haughty smile pass over his lips. "Yes — a rebel, an outlaw cast out from humanity, perhaps!"

The engineer did not answer.

"Well, sir?"

"It is not for me to judge Captain Nemo," answered Cyrus Smith, "at least as far as his past life is concerned. Like everyone else, I don't know what drove him to so strange an existence, and I cannot judge the effects without knowing the causes. But what I do know is that a kindly hand has been stretched out over us ever since we came to Lincoln Island. I know that every one of us owes his life to a being who is good, generous and powerful — and that this being, so powerful, so generous and so good, is you, Captain Nemo!"

"It is I," the captain answered simply.

The engineer and the reporter had risen. Their companions had drawn closer, and the gratitude that was overflowing their hearts was about to pour out in gestures and words...

Captain Nemo stopped them with a sign, and in a voice more moved than he would have wished, no doubt, he said:

"When you have heard me."

And in a few sentences, clear and hurried, the captain told them the whole of his life.

His story was brief, and yet he had to gather into himself all the strength he had left to tell it to the end. It was obvious that he was struggling against an extreme weakness. Several times Cyrus Smith urged him to rest a little, but he shook his head like a man to whom tomorrow no longer belongs, and when the reporter offered to tend him, he answered:

"It is useless. My hours are numbered."

Captain Nemo was an Indian, Prince Dakkar, the son of a rajah of the then independent territory of Bundelkhand, and nephew of the Indian hero Tipu Sultan. When the boy was ten years old, his father sent him to Europe to receive a complete education there — with the secret intention that one day he might fight on equal terms against the men he regarded as the oppressors of his country.

From the age of ten to thirty, Prince Dakkar, superbly gifted, great of heart and of mind, made himself master of everything. In the sciences, in literature and in the arts he carried his studies high and far.

Prince Dakkar traveled throughout Europe. His birth and his fortune made him sought after everywhere, but the attractions of society never drew him. Young and handsome, he remained serious and somber, devoured by a thirst for learning, with an implacable resentment riveted to his heart.

Prince Dakkar hated. He hated the one country where he had always refused to set foot, the one nation whose advances he rejected again and again: he hated England — and all the more because, on more than one point, he admired her.

For this Indian carried in himself all the fierce hatred of the conquered for the conqueror. The invader could find no forgiveness in the invaded. He was the son of one of those sovereigns whose servitude the United Kingdom had managed to secure only in name; a prince of the family of Tipu Sultan, raised on ideas of reclaiming what had been taken and of vengeance, bound by an inescapable love to his poetic land weighed down with English chains. He would never set foot on that accursed soil to which India owed its enslavement.

Prince Dakkar became an artist whom the wonders of art stirred nobly, a scientist to whom nothing in the higher sciences was foreign, a statesman schooled in the courts of Europe. To those who observed him only superficially, he may perhaps have passed for one of those cosmopolitans, curious to know but disdainful of acting — one of those wealthy travelers, proud and detached spirits, who wander the world endlessly and belong to no country.

It was nothing of the kind. This artist, this scientist, this man had remained Indian at heart, Indian in his longing for revenge, Indian in the hope he nourished of one day reclaiming his country's rights, driving out the foreigner and giving it back its independence.

And so Prince Dakkar returned to Bundelkhand in the year 1849. He married a noble Indian woman whose heart bled, as his did, for the misfortunes of their homeland. They had two children, whom he cherished. But happiness at home could not make him forget India's enslavement. He waited for his opportunity. It came.

The English yoke had perhaps pressed too heavily on the peoples of India. Prince Dakkar gave voice to the discontented. He poured into their minds all the hatred he felt for the foreigner. He traveled not only through the regions of the Indian peninsula that were still independent, but also through the lands directly under English rule. He reminded the people of the great days of Tipu Sultan, who had died a hero's death at Seringapatam defending his homeland.

In 1857 the great Sepoy Rebellion broke out. Prince Dakkar was its soul. He organized that immense uprising. He put his talents and his wealth at the service of the cause. He did not spare himself: he fought in the front rank; he risked his life like the humblest of those heroes who had risen to free their country; he was wounded ten times in twenty battles, and still had not found death when the last soldiers of independence fell beneath English bullets.

Never had British power in India been in such danger. And if, as they had hoped, the sepoys had found help from outside, the influence and the dominion of the United Kingdom in Asia might well have come to an end.

The name of Prince Dakkar was famous then. The hero who bore it did not hide; he fought in the open. A price was put on his head, and though no traitor could be found to hand it over, his father, his mother, his wife, his children paid for him, before he could even know of the dangers they were running because of him...

Right, once again, had fallen before might. But civilization never retreats, and it seems to borrow every right from necessity. The sepoys were defeated, and the land of the old rajahs fell back under England's tighter rule.

Prince Dakkar, who had not been able to die, went back into the mountains of Bundelkhand. There, alone from then on, seized by an immense disgust for everything that bore the name of man, filled with hatred and horror for the civilized world and wishing to flee it forever, he turned what was left of his fortune into money, gathered some twenty of his most faithful companions, and one day they all vanished.

Where, then, had Prince Dakkar gone to look for the independence that the inhabited earth refused him? Beneath the waters, in the depths of the sea, where no one could follow him.

The man of war gave way to the man of science. A deserted island in the Pacific served him as a shipyard, and there a submarine boat was built to his designs. Electricity, whose immeasurable mechanical power he had learned to harness by means that will one day be known, and which he drew from inexhaustible sources, was put to every use his floating machine required: it drove her, lit her and heated her. The sea, with its infinite treasures, its myriads of fish, its harvests of seaweed and sargassum, its enormous mammals — and not only everything that nature kept there but everything that men had lost there too — amply supplied the needs of the prince and his crew. And that fulfilled his dearest wish, since he wanted no more communication of any kind with the land. He named his submarine machine the Nautilus, he called himself Captain Nemo, and he disappeared beneath the seas.

For many years the captain roamed every ocean, from one pole to the other. A pariah from the inhabited world, he gathered admirable treasures in those unknown realms. The millions lost in Vigo Bay in 1702 by the Spanish galleons gave him an inexhaustible mine of wealth, which he always spent, anonymously, on behalf of peoples fighting for the independence of their countries — the Cretans in their rising among them.

At last, when he had had no communication with his fellow men for a long time, three men were thrown aboard his ship during the night of November 6, 1866: a French professor, his servant and a Canadian fisherman. These three men had been hurled into the sea by a collision between the Nautilus and the United States frigate Abraham Lincoln, which was hunting her.

Captain Nemo learned from this professor that the Nautilus — taken sometimes for a giant mammal of the whale family, sometimes for a submarine machine carrying a crew of pirates — was being pursued on every sea.

Captain Nemo could have given those three men back to the ocean, men whom chance had flung so suddenly across his mysterious existence. He did not. He kept them prisoner, and for seven months they were able to gaze on all the wonders of a voyage that went on for twenty thousand leagues under the sea.

One day, June 22, 1867, these three men, who knew nothing of Captain Nemo's past, managed to escape after seizing the Nautilus's dinghy. But since at that moment the Nautilus was being dragged toward the coast of Norway in the whirlpools of the Maelström, the captain was bound to believe that the fugitives had drowned in those terrifying eddies and met their death at the bottom of the abyss. So he did not know that the Frenchman and his two companions had been miraculously thrown up on the shore, that fishermen of the Lofoten Islands had taken them in, and that the professor, on his return to France, had published the book in which seven months of the Nautilus's strange and adventurous voyage were told and delivered up to public curiosity.

For a long time still, Captain Nemo went on living in the same way, roaming the seas. But little by little his companions died and went to rest in their coral cemetery at the bottom of the Pacific. The Nautilus grew empty, and at last Captain Nemo was left alone, the only one remaining of all those who had taken refuge with him in the depths of the ocean.

Captain Nemo was sixty years old by then. When he found himself alone, he managed to bring his Nautilus back to one of the undersea harbors he sometimes used as ports of call.

One of these harbors was hollowed out beneath Lincoln Island, and it was the one now sheltering the Nautilus.

The captain had been there for six years, no longer sailing, waiting for death — that is, for the moment when he would be reunited with his companions — when chance made him a witness to the fall of the balloon that was carrying the Southerners' prisoners. He was walking under the water in his diving suit, a few cable lengths from the island's shore, when the engineer was flung into the sea. A generous impulse carried the captain away... and he saved Cyrus Smith.

At first he meant to flee from these five castaways. But his harbor of refuge was closed. Because the basalt had been heaved up under the action of the volcano, he could no longer pass through the entrance to the crypt. Where there was still enough water for a light boat to cross the bar, there was no longer enough for the Nautilus, which drew a comparatively great depth of water.

So Captain Nemo stayed. Then he watched these men, thrown without resources onto a deserted island, but he did not want to be seen. Little by little, when he saw that they were honest and full of energy, bound to one another by a brotherly friendship, he took an interest in their efforts. Almost in spite of himself, he came to know all the secrets of their lives. With the diving suit it was easy for him to reach the bottom of the inner well of Granite House, and by climbing the projections of the rock up to its upper mouth he could hear the colonists talking over the past and studying the present and the future. From them he learned of America's immense effort against America itself, to abolish slavery. Yes! These were men worthy to reconcile Captain Nemo with the humanity they represented so honestly on the island!

Captain Nemo had saved Cyrus Smith. It was he, too, who brought the dog back to the Chimneys; who flung Top out of the waters of the lake; who ran aground at Jetsam Point the chest that held so many useful things for the colonists; who sent the canoe back down the current of the Mercy; who threw down the rope from the top of Granite House when the monkeys attacked; who made Ayrton's presence on Tabor Island known by means of the document sealed in the bottle; who blew up the brig with a torpedo laid at the bottom of the channel; who saved Harbert from certain death by bringing the quinine sulfate; and it was he, finally, who struck down the convicts with those electric bullets whose secret he alone possessed and which he used in his hunts beneath the sea. That explained so many incidents that must have seemed supernatural, and that all bore witness to the captain's generosity and power.

And yet this great misanthrope thirsted to do good. He still had useful advice to give the people he had protected. And besides, feeling his heart, restored to itself by the approach of death, beating once more, he summoned the colonists from Granite House, as the reader knows, by means of a wire with which he had connected the corral to the Nautilus, which was fitted with an alphabet dial... Perhaps he would not have done it had he known that Cyrus Smith knew enough of his story to greet him by the name of Nemo.

The captain had finished the story of his life. Then Cyrus Smith spoke. He recalled all the incidents that had had so beneficial an influence on the colony, and in his companions' name as well as his own he thanked the generous being to whom they owed so much.

But Captain Nemo had no thought of claiming any reward for the services he had rendered. One last thought was troubling his mind, and before he took the hand the engineer held out to him, he said:

"Now, sir — now that you know my life, judge it!"

In speaking this way, the captain was clearly alluding to a grave incident that the three strangers flung aboard his ship had witnessed — an incident the French professor would necessarily have told in his book, and which must have caused a terrible stir.

And indeed, a few days before the professor and his two companions escaped, the Nautilus, chased by a frigate in the North Atlantic, had hurled herself like a battering ram against that frigate and sunk her without mercy.

Cyrus Smith understood the allusion and made no reply.

"It was an English frigate, sir!" cried Captain Nemo, for a moment Prince Dakkar again. "An English frigate, do you hear me? She was attacking me! I was hemmed in, in a narrow, shallow bay!... I had to get through, and... I got through!"

Then, in a calmer voice, he added:

"I was in the right, and justice was on my side. Everywhere I have done the good I could — and the harm I had to do as well. Not all justice lies in forgiveness!"

A few moments of silence followed this answer, and Captain Nemo spoke the same words again:

"What do you think of me, gentlemen?"

Cyrus Smith held out his hand to the captain, and in answer to his question he said in a grave voice:

"Captain, your mistake was to believe that the past could be brought back to life, and you fought against necessary progress. It was one of those errors that some admire and others condemn, which God alone can judge, and which human reason must absolve. A man who goes wrong with an intention he believes to be good may be fought, but he does not lose our esteem. Your error is one that does not rule out admiration, and your name has nothing to fear from the judgment of history. History loves heroic follies, even while it condemns the consequences they bring."

Captain Nemo's chest heaved, and his hand reached toward the sky.

"Was I wrong? Was I right?" he murmured.

Cyrus Smith went on:

"All great deeds go back to God, for they come from Him! Captain Nemo, the honest men here, whom you have helped, will mourn you forever!"

Harbert had come close to the captain. He knelt, took his hand and kissed it.

A tear slid from the dying man's eyes.

"My child," he said, "may you be blessed!..."
