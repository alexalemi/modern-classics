Chapter 14: Measuring the Granite Wall

_Measuring the granite wall — An application of the theorem of similar triangles — The latitude of the island — An excursion to the north — An oyster bed — Plans for the future — The sun crosses the meridian — The coordinates of Lincoln Island_

The next day, April 16 — Easter Sunday — the colonists came out of the Chimneys at first light and set about washing their linen and cleaning their clothes. The engineer meant to make soap as soon as he could get hold of the raw materials soap-making calls for: soda or potash, and fat or oil. The all-important question of renewing their wardrobe would also be dealt with in its own time and place. In any case, their clothes would easily last another six months, because they were sturdy and could stand up to the wear and tear of manual work. But everything would depend on where the island lay in relation to inhabited lands. That was what would be settled this very day, if the weather allowed.

And the sun, rising over a clear horizon, promised a magnificent day, one of those beautiful autumn days that are like the warm season's last goodbye.

So the task was to complete the data from the evening before by measuring the height of Prospect Plateau above sea level.

"Don't you need an instrument like the one you used yesterday?" Harbert asked the engineer.

"No, my boy," he answered. "We're going to go about it another way, and one that's very nearly as precise."

Harbert, who loved to learn about everything, followed the engineer, who walked away from the foot of the granite wall and down to the edge of the beach. Meanwhile, Pencroff, Nab, and the reporter busied themselves with various jobs.

Cyrus Smith had brought along a sort of straight pole, about twelve feet long, which he had measured as exactly as he could by comparing it with his own height, which he knew to within a twelfth of an inch. Harbert carried a plumb line that Cyrus Smith had given him — simply a stone tied to the end of a flexible fiber.

When he was about twenty feet from the water's edge and about five hundred feet from the granite wall, which rose straight up, Cyrus Smith drove the pole two feet into the sand. Wedging it carefully, and checking it with the plumb line, he managed to stand it exactly upright — at right angles to the level of the horizon.

That done, he stepped back as far as he needed to so that, lying flat on the sand, he could send his line of sight from his eye past the top of the pole and the crest of the wall at the same time, just grazing both. Then he marked that spot carefully with a peg.

Then he turned to Harbert.

"Do you know the first principles of geometry?" he asked.

"A little, Mr. Cyrus," answered Harbert, who didn't want to claim too much.

"You remember the properties of two similar triangles?"

"Yes," said Harbert. "Their corresponding sides are proportional."

"Well, my boy, I've just built two similar triangles, both of them right triangles. The first, the smaller one, has for its sides the upright pole and the distance from the peg to the foot of the pole, and my line of sight for its hypotenuse. The second has for its sides the upright wall, whose height is what we want to measure, and the distance from the peg to the foot of that wall, and once again my line of sight for its hypotenuse — which happens to be the first triangle's hypotenuse, extended."

"Oh, Mr. Cyrus, I see!" cried Harbert. "Just as the distance from the peg to the pole is in proportion to the distance from the peg to the foot of the wall, the height of the pole is in the same proportion to the height of the wall."

"Exactly, Harbert," the engineer answered. "Once we've measured those first two distances, since we know the height of the pole, we'll only have one proportion to work out. That will give us the height of the wall and save us the trouble of measuring it directly."

The two horizontal distances were taken, using the pole itself as the measuring rod. Its length above the sand was exactly ten feet.

The first distance, between the peg and the point where the pole was driven into the sand, was fifteen feet.

The second distance, between the peg and the foot of the wall, was five hundred feet.

With these measurements finished, Cyrus Smith and the boy went back to the Chimneys.

There the engineer took a flat stone he had brought back from one of his earlier excursions, a kind of slate on which it was easy to scratch figures with a sharp shell. And he set out the following proportion — 15 is to 500 as 10 is to x:

    15 : 500 :: 10 : x
    500 x 10 = 5000
    5000 / 15 = 333.33

From which it was established that the granite wall was three hundred and thirty-three feet high.

Cyrus Smith then picked up the instrument he had made the evening before, whose two little boards, by the spread between them, gave him the angular distance of the star Alpha above the horizon. He measured the opening of that angle very exactly on a circle that he divided into three hundred and sixty equal parts. Now, this angle — once he had added the twenty-seven degrees that separate Alpha from the south pole, and corrected the observation to sea level for the height of the plateau it had been made from — came to fifty-three degrees. Subtract those fifty-three degrees from ninety degrees — the distance from the pole to the equator — and thirty-seven degrees were left. So Cyrus Smith concluded that Lincoln Island lay on the thirty-seventh degree of south latitude; or, allowing for an error of five degrees, given how rough his methods were, that it must lie somewhere between the thirty-fifth and the fortieth parallels.

There remained the longitude, to complete the island's coordinates. The engineer would try to find it that very day at noon — that is, at the moment when the sun crossed the meridian.

It was decided that this Sunday would be spent on a walk, or rather on an exploration of the part of the island lying between the north end of the lake and Shark Gulf, and that, if the weather allowed, they would push the survey on as far as the northern side of South Mandible Cape. They would have lunch in the dunes and not come back until evening.

At half past eight in the morning the little troop was walking along the edge of the channel. On the other side, on Safety Islet, a great many birds were strolling about with a solemn air. They were diving birds of the penguin kind, easy to recognize by their unpleasant cry, which sounds like a donkey's bray. Pencroff considered them only from the point of view of eating, and he learned, not without some satisfaction, that their flesh, though blackish, is quite edible.

They could also see large amphibious animals crawling on the sand — seals, no doubt — which seemed to have chosen the islet as their refuge. There was little point in considering these as food, because their oily flesh is disgusting. Still, Cyrus Smith watched them closely, and, without explaining what he had in mind, he told his companions that very soon they would pay the islet a visit.

The shore the colonists were following was strewn with countless shells, some of which would have delighted any collector of mollusks. Among others there were pheasant shells, lamp shells, trigonias, and so on. But something far more useful was a huge oyster bed, uncovered at low tide, which Nab spotted among the rocks about four miles from the Chimneys.

"Nab hasn't wasted his day!" cried Pencroff, looking at the bed of oysters stretching out from the shore.

"It's a lucky find, all right," said the reporter. "And if it's true, as they say, that every oyster produces fifty to sixty thousand eggs a year, we'll have an inexhaustible supply there."

"Only I don't think oysters are very nourishing," said Harbert.

"No," answered Cyrus Smith. "An oyster contains very little nitrogenous matter — the stuff that builds up the body — and a man who lived on nothing else would need no fewer than fifteen or sixteen dozen a day."

"Fine!" said Pencroff. "We can swallow dozens and dozens of dozens before we use up the bed. How about taking a few for lunch?"

And without waiting for an answer to his proposal, knowing perfectly well that it was approved in advance, the sailor and Nab pried loose a good quantity of the shellfish. They put them in a sort of net made of hibiscus fibers that Nab had put together, which already held the meal's menu. Then they went on up the coast between the dunes and the sea.

Every now and then Cyrus Smith looked at his watch, so as to be ready in time for the observation of the sun, which had to be made at exactly noon.

This whole part of the island was very barren, all the way to the point that closed off Union Bay, the one that had been named South Mandible Cape. There was nothing to see there but sand and shells, mixed with fragments of lava. A few seabirds haunted this desolate coast — gulls, great albatrosses, and wild ducks too, which quite rightly roused Pencroff's appetite. He tried to bring some down with his arrows, but without success, because they hardly ever settled, and he would have had to hit them on the wing.

Which led the sailor to say to the engineer once again:

"You see, Mr. Cyrus, as long as we don't have a hunting gun or two, our equipment's going to leave something to be desired!"

"No doubt, Pencroff," the reporter answered, "but that's entirely up to you! Get us iron for the barrels, steel for the locks, saltpeter, charcoal, and sulfur for the powder, mercury and nitric acid for the fulminate that sets off the charge, and lead for the bullets, and Cyrus will make us first-class guns."

"Oh," answered the engineer, "we'll no doubt be able to find all those substances on the island, but a firearm is a delicate instrument, and it calls for tools of great precision. Well, we'll see later on."

"Why," cried Pencroff, "why did we have to throw overboard all those weapons we had in the basket with us, and our tools, right down to our pocketknives!"

"But if we hadn't thrown them out, Pencroff, the balloon would have thrown us into the bottom of the sea!" said Harbert.

"That's true enough, what you say, my boy!" answered the sailor.

Then, moving on to another idea:

"But now that I think of it," he added, "how flabbergasted Jonathan Forster and his friends must have been the next morning, when they found the place empty and the machine flown away!"

"What they might have thought is the least of my worries!" said the reporter.

"All the same, it was my idea!" said Pencroff, looking pleased with himself.

"A fine idea, Pencroff," answered Gédéon Spilett with a laugh, "and it's put us where we are!"

"I'd rather be here than in the hands of the Southerners!" cried the sailor. "Especially since Mr. Cyrus was kind enough to come and join us!"

"So would I, honestly!" replied the reporter. "Besides, what are we missing? Nothing!"

"Except... everything!" answered Pencroff, bursting out laughing and shaking his broad shoulders. "But one of these days we'll find a way to get out of here!"

"And sooner perhaps than you imagine, my friends," the engineer said then, "if Lincoln Island is only a moderate distance from an inhabited archipelago or a continent. Within an hour we'll know. I don't have a map of the Pacific, but I remember its southern part very clearly. The latitude I got yesterday puts Lincoln Island level with New Zealand to the west and with the coast of Chile to the east. But between those two lands the distance is at least six thousand miles. So what's left is to decide what point the island occupies in that wide stretch of sea, and that's what the longitude will give us in a little while — closely enough, I hope."

"Aren't the Tuamotus the islands nearest to us in latitude?" asked Harbert.

"Yes," answered the engineer, "but they're more than twelve hundred miles away from us."

"And over that way?" asked Nab, who was following the conversation with the greatest interest, and his hand pointed south.

"Over that way, nothing," answered Pencroff.

"Nothing, indeed," the engineer added.

"Well, Cyrus," asked the reporter, "what if Lincoln Island turns out to be only two or three hundred miles from New Zealand or Chile?..."

"Well," answered the engineer, "instead of building a house, we'll build a boat, and Master Pencroff will take charge of sailing her..."

"Why, of course, Mr. Cyrus," cried the sailor, "I'm all ready to become a captain — as soon as you've found a way to build a boat good enough to go to sea!"

"We'll do it, if it's necessary!" answered Cyrus Smith.

But while these men talked — men who truly had doubts about nothing — the hour was drawing near when the observation was to be made. How would Cyrus Smith go about recording the moment the sun crossed the island's meridian, without any instrument at all? That was something Harbert could not guess.

The observers were now six miles from the Chimneys, not far from the part of the dunes where the engineer had been found after his mysterious rescue. They halted at that spot and got everything ready for lunch, since it was half past eleven. Harbert went to fetch fresh water from the stream that ran close by, and he brought it back in a jug Nab had brought along.

While these preparations went on, Cyrus Smith set everything up for his astronomical observation. He chose a perfectly clear spot on the beach that the sea, as it went out, had leveled perfectly smooth. This layer of very fine sand was as flat as a mirror, without a single grain sticking up above the rest. Actually, it didn't matter whether this layer was level or not, and it mattered no more whether the stick, six feet tall, that was planted in it stood exactly upright. On the contrary, the engineer even tilted it toward the south — that is, away from the sun. For we must not forget that the colonists of Lincoln Island, precisely because the island lay in the Southern Hemisphere, saw the radiant sun trace its daily arc above the northern horizon, not above the southern one.

Harbert understood then how the engineer meant to record the sun's culmination — that is, its passage across the island's meridian, or, in other words, local noon. He would do it by means of the shadow the stick cast on the sand, a method that, lacking any instrument, would give him a close enough approximation for the result he wanted.

For the moment when this shadow reached its shortest length would be exactly noon, and it would be enough to follow the tip of the shadow to catch the instant when, after growing steadily shorter, it began to lengthen again. By tilting his stick away from the sun, Cyrus Smith made the shadow longer, and so its changes would be easier to see. After all, the bigger the hand of a dial, the more easily you can follow the movement of its tip. And the stick's shadow was nothing but the hand of a dial.

When he judged that the moment had come, Cyrus Smith knelt on the sand, and, with little wooden markers that he pushed into the sand, he began to mark off the shadow's successive shortenings. His companions, bending over him, followed the operation with the keenest interest.

The reporter held his chronometer in his hand, ready to note the time it showed when the shadow was at its shortest. What's more, since Cyrus Smith was working on April 16 — one of the days when true time and mean time are the same — the time given by Gédéon Spilett would be the true time in Washington at that moment, which would simplify the calculation.

Meanwhile the sun moved slowly on. The stick's shadow shrank little by little, and when it seemed to Cyrus Smith that it was starting to grow again:

"What time is it?" he asked.

"One minute past five," Gédéon Spilett answered at once.

All that was left was to do the arithmetic. Nothing could be easier. There was, as you can see, a difference of five hours, in round numbers, between the meridian of Washington and the meridian of Lincoln Island — that is, it was noon on Lincoln Island when it was already five in the afternoon in Washington. Now, the sun, in its apparent motion around the earth, travels one degree every four minutes, or fifteen degrees an hour. Fifteen degrees multiplied by five hours gave seventy-five degrees.

So, since Washington lies at 77° 3′ 11″ — call it seventy-seven degrees — counted from the meridian of Greenwich, which the Americans, along with the English, take as the starting point for longitude, it followed that the island lay seventy-seven degrees plus seventy-five degrees west of the meridian of Greenwich — that is, at one hundred and fifty-two degrees west longitude.

Cyrus Smith announced this result to his companions. And allowing for errors in the observations, as he had done with the latitude, he felt he could state that Lincoln Island lay between the thirty-fifth and the thirty-seventh parallels, and between the one hundred and fiftieth and the one hundred and fifty-fifth meridians west of Greenwich.

The margin he allowed for errors of observation was, as you can see, five degrees either way. At sixty miles to the degree, that could mean an error of three hundred miles in latitude or in longitude from the true position.

But this error would make no difference to the decision they had to make. It was perfectly obvious that Lincoln Island lay so far from any land or island group that no one could risk crossing that distance in a simple, fragile boat.

In fact, his reckoning put it at least twelve hundred miles from Tahiti and the islands of the Tuamotus, more than eighteen hundred miles from New Zealand, and more than four thousand five hundred miles from the American coast!

And when Cyrus Smith searched his memory, he could not recall that any island at all occupied, in that part of the Pacific, the position assigned to Lincoln Island.
