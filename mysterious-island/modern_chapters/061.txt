Chapter 20: A Rock in the Pacific

_A lone rock in the Pacific — The last refuge of the colonists of Lincoln Island — Death in sight — Unexpected help — Why and how it comes — The last kindness — An island on solid ground — The tomb of Captain Nemo_

A lone rock thirty feet long and fifteen wide, barely ten feet out of the water — that was the only solid point the waves of the Pacific had not swallowed.

It was all that was left of the great block of Granite House! The wall had been toppled and then broken apart, and some of the rocks of the great hall had piled up in such a way as to form this highest point. Everything around it had vanished into the abyss: the lower cone of Mount Franklin, torn open by the explosion; the lava jaws of Shark Gulf; Prospect Plateau; Safety Islet; the granite of Balloon Harbor; the basalt of the Dakkar Crypt; the long Serpentine Peninsula, even though it lay so far from the center of the eruption! Of Lincoln Island, nothing could be seen now but this narrow rock, which served as a refuge for the six colonists and their dog Top.

The animals had perished in the catastrophe too, the birds as well as every other member of the island's wildlife, all of them crushed or drowned. Even poor Jup, alas, had met his death in some crevice of the ground!

If Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, Pencroff, Nab and Ayrton had survived, it was because they had been together in their tent at the time, and had been flung into the sea at the moment when the fragments of the island were raining down on every side.

When they came back up to the surface, all they could see, half a cable length away, was this heap of rocks. They swam to it and climbed onto it.

They had been living on this bare rock for nine days! A few provisions taken from the storeroom of Granite House before the catastrophe, and a little fresh water that the rain had left in a hollow of the rock — that was all the unfortunate men possessed. Their last hope, their ship, had been smashed. They had no way of leaving this reef. They had no fire, and nothing to make one with. They were doomed to die!

That day, March 18, they had only two days' worth of preserved food left, even though they had eaten no more than the bare minimum. All their science, all their intelligence, could do nothing in such a situation. They were entirely in the hands of God.

Cyrus Smith was calm. Gédéon Spilett, more on edge, and Pencroff, in the grip of a smoldering anger, paced back and forth on the rock. Harbert never left the engineer's side, and kept looking at him as though asking him for help that he could not give. Nab and Ayrton were resigned to their fate.

"Oh, misery! Misery!" Pencroff would say again and again. "If only we had a nutshell — anything at all — to carry us to Tabor Island! But nothing! Nothing!"

"Captain Nemo did well to die!" Nab said once.

For the next five days Cyrus Smith and his unlucky companions lived with the most extreme economy, eating only just enough to keep from collapsing with hunger. They were dreadfully weak. Harbert and Nab began to show some signs of delirium.

In that situation, could they keep even a shadow of hope? No! What was their only chance? That a ship might pass within sight of the reef? But they knew very well from experience that ships never visited this part of the Pacific! Could they count on some truly providential coincidence bringing the Scottish yacht to Tabor Island to look for Ayrton at precisely this moment? It was unlikely. And besides, even supposing it did come, the colonists had never been able to leave a notice there explaining what had changed in Ayrton's situation. So the yacht's commander, after searching the islet and finding nothing, would put to sea again and head back to lower latitudes.

No! They could keep no hope at all of being saved, and a horrible death, death by hunger and thirst, awaited them on this rock!

And already they were lying stretched out on the rock, motionless, no longer aware of what was going on around them. Only Ayrton, by a last supreme effort, still lifted his head now and then and cast a despairing look over that empty sea!...

But then, on the morning of March 24, Ayrton's arms stretched out toward a point in the distance. He raised himself, first to his knees, then to his feet, and his hand seemed to be making a signal...

A ship was in sight of the island! This ship was not roaming the sea at random. The reef was her goal. She was making straight for it, under full steam, and the unfortunate castaways would have seen her hours before if they had still had the strength to watch the horizon!

"The Duncan!" murmured Ayrton, and he fell back without moving.

When Cyrus Smith and his companions came to, thanks to the care that had been lavished on them, they found themselves in a cabin aboard a steamer, with no idea how they had escaped death.

One word from Ayrton was enough to tell them everything.

"The Duncan!" he murmured.

"The Duncan!" Cyrus Smith answered.

And raising his arms toward the sky, he cried out:

"Almighty God! So it was your will that we should be saved!"

It was indeed the Duncan, Lord Glenarvan's yacht, now commanded by Robert, the son of Captain Grant. She had been sent to Tabor Island to find Ayrton and bring him home after his twelve years of atonement!...

The colonists were saved. They were already on their way home!

"Captain Robert," Cyrus Smith asked, "what on earth gave you the idea, after you left Tabor Island and found Ayrton gone, to sail a hundred miles from there to the northeast?"

"Mr. Smith," Robert Grant replied, "I was coming to find not only Ayrton, but your companions and you!"

"My companions and me?"

"Of course! On Lincoln Island!"

"Lincoln Island!" cried Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, Nab and Pencroff all together, utterly astonished.

"How do you know about Lincoln Island," asked Cyrus Smith, "when the island isn't even on the charts?"

"I learned of it from the notice you left on Tabor Island," Robert Grant answered.

"A notice?" cried Gédéon Spilett.

"Of course. Here it is," Robert Grant replied, holding out a document that gave the position of Lincoln Island in longitude and latitude, and described it as "the present home of Ayrton and five American colonists."

"Captain Nemo!..." said Cyrus Smith, when he had read the notice and recognized that it was in the same hand that had written the document found at the corral.

"Ah!" said Pencroff. "So it was him who took our Bonadventure — him who risked the trip to Tabor Island, all alone!..."

"To leave this notice there!" Harbert answered.

"So I was right," cried the sailor, "when I said that even after his death the captain would do us one last service!"

"My friends," said Cyrus Smith, in a voice deeply moved, "may the God of all mercies receive the soul of Captain Nemo, our savior!"

The colonists had bared their heads at Cyrus Smith's last words, and they murmured the captain's name.

At that moment Ayrton came up to the engineer and said simply:

"Where should I put this casket?"

It was the casket Ayrton had saved at the risk of his life at the moment the island was sinking, and now he had come to hand it over faithfully to the engineer.

"Ayrton! Ayrton!" said Cyrus Smith, deeply moved.

Then, turning to Robert Grant, he added:

"Sir, where you left a guilty man, you find a man whom atonement has made honest again, and I am proud to shake his hand!"

Robert Grant was then told the whole strange story of Captain Nemo and the colonists of Lincoln Island. Then, after taking the bearings of what was left of the reef, which from now on would have to be marked on the charts of the Pacific, he gave the order to come about.

Two weeks later the colonists landed in America, and they found their country at peace again, after the terrible war that had ended in the triumph of justice and right.

Of the riches in the casket that Captain Nemo had left to the colonists of Lincoln Island, the greater part went to buy a large estate in the state of Iowa. A single pearl, the most beautiful one, was set aside from the treasure and sent to Lady Glenarvan, in the name of the castaways the Duncan had brought home.

There, on that estate, the colonists called to work — that is, to fortune and happiness — all those to whom they had meant to offer the hospitality of Lincoln Island. There a great colony was founded, and they gave it the name of the island that had vanished into the depths of the Pacific. It had a river, which was called the Mercy; a mountain, which took the name of Franklin; a little lake, which became Lake Grant; and forests, which became the forests of the Far West. It was like an island on solid ground.

There, under the intelligent hand of the engineer and his companions, everything prospered. Not one of the old colonists of Lincoln Island was missing, for they had sworn always to live together: Nab wherever his master was; Ayrton ready to sacrifice himself at any opportunity; Pencroff more of a farmer than he had ever been a sailor; Harbert, who finished his studies under Cyrus Smith's guidance; and Gédéon Spilett himself, who founded the New Lincoln Herald, which became the best-informed newspaper in the whole world.

There Cyrus Smith and his companions were visited several times by Lord and Lady Glenarvan; by Captain John Mangles and his wife, Robert Grant's sister; by Robert Grant himself; by Major McNabbs; by all those who had played a part in the twin stories of Captain Grant and Captain Nemo.

There, at last, they were all happy, united in the present as they had been in the past. But they were never to forget that island where they had arrived poor and naked, the island that for four years had supplied all their needs, and of which nothing now remained but a block of granite beaten by the waves of the Pacific — the tomb of the man who had been Captain Nemo!
