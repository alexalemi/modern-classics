Chapter 2: An Episode of the Civil War

_An episode of the Civil War — The engineer Cyrus Smith — Gédéon Spilett — Nab — The sailor Pencroff — Young Harbert — An unexpected proposal — A meeting at ten o'clock at night — Departure in the storm_

The men the hurricane had just thrown onto this coast were neither professional balloonists nor amateurs who liked to go adventuring in the air. They were prisoners of war, whose daring had driven them to escape under extraordinary circumstances. A hundred times they should have died! A hundred times their torn balloon should have flung them into the abyss! But heaven was saving them for a strange destiny. On March 24, after fleeing Richmond, which General Ulysses Grant's troops had under siege, they found themselves seven thousand miles from the capital of Virginia, the chief stronghold of the secessionists during the terrible Civil War. Their voyage through the air had lasted five days.

Here, then, are the curious circumstances in which the prisoners had escaped — an escape that was to end in the catastrophe we already know.

That same year, in February 1865, during one of the surprise attacks General Grant launched, without success, to take Richmond, several of his officers fell into enemy hands and were held in the city. One of the most distinguished of those captured belonged to the Federal general staff, and his name was Cyrus Smith.

Cyrus Smith, a native of Massachusetts, was an engineer and a scientist of the first rank, to whom the government of the Union had entrusted, during the war, the running of the railroads, whose strategic role was so enormous. He was a true Northerner: thin, bony, lanky, about forty-five years old, his close-cropped hair already going gray, and his beard too, of which he kept only a thick mustache. He had one of those fine heads that look as if they were made to be stamped on a coin or a medal — burning eyes, a serious mouth, the face of a scientist of the fighting kind. He was one of those engineers who chose to begin by handling the hammer and the pick, the way some generals choose to start out as common soldiers. So along with an ingenious mind he had supremely skillful hands. His muscles were remarkably firm and strong. Truly a man of action as much as a man of thought, he acted without effort, driven by a great overflow of vitality, with the kind of stubborn persistence that defies every stroke of bad luck. Very learned, very practical, and "très débrouillard," to borrow a phrase from French army slang — a man who could always find a way out of a fix — he had a superb temperament. He stayed master of himself whatever the circumstances, and he met to the highest degree the three conditions that together make up human energy: activity of mind and body, intensity of desire, and power of will. His motto could have been that of William of Orange in the seventeenth century: "I need no hope to undertake, nor success to persevere."

Cyrus Smith was also courage itself. He had been in every battle of the Civil War. He had started out under Ulysses Grant with the Illinois volunteers, and he had fought at Paducah, at Belmont, at Pittsburg Landing, at the siege of Corinth, at Port Gibson, at the Black River, at Chattanooga, in the Wilderness, on the Potomac — everywhere, and bravely, a soldier worthy of the general who once answered, "I never count my dead!" A hundred times Cyrus Smith should have been among those dead that the terrible Grant did not count. But in those battles, where he hardly spared himself, luck was always on his side, until the moment he was wounded and taken prisoner on the battlefield at Richmond.

On the same day as Cyrus Smith, another important figure fell into Southern hands. It was none other than the Honorable Gédéon Spilett, "reporter" for the New York Herald, who had been assigned to follow the ups and downs of the war with the armies of the North.

Gédéon Spilett belonged to that astonishing breed of English and American correspondents, men like Stanley, who will stop at nothing to get accurate news and send it to their papers as fast as it can possibly go. The newspapers of the Union, such as the New York Herald, are true powers in the land, and their envoys are representatives that have to be reckoned with. Gédéon Spilett stood in the front rank of those envoys.

He was a man of great merit — energetic, quick, ready for anything, full of ideas. He had traveled the whole world. He was a soldier and an artist, fiery in council, decisive in action, counting neither trouble nor fatigue nor danger when it came to finding out everything, for himself first and for his paper second. A true hero of curiosity, of news, of the never-before-told, of the unknown, of the impossible, he was one of those fearless observers who write under a hail of bullets, file their stories under cannon fire, and count every danger as a stroke of good luck.

He too had been in every battle, in the front line, a revolver in one hand and a notebook in the other, and grapeshot never made his pencil shake. He did not clog the wires with endless telegrams, like people who talk when they have nothing to say; but every one of his dispatches, short, sharp and clear, threw light on some important point. And he did not lack a sense of humor. It was he who, after the fight at the Black River, determined at any cost to keep his place at the telegraph office window so that he could report the result of the battle to his paper, wired the first chapters of the Bible for two solid hours. It cost the New York Herald two thousand dollars, but the New York Herald had the news first.

Gédéon Spilett was tall, forty at most. Reddish-blond side-whiskers framed his face. His eye was calm and sharp, quick to move from one thing to the next — the eye of a man used to taking in every detail of a landscape at a glance. Solidly built, he had been hardened in every climate, the way a bar of steel is tempered in cold water.

For ten years Gédéon Spilett had been the New York Herald's staff reporter, enriching its pages with his articles and his drawings, since he handled a pencil as well as a pen. When he was captured, he was in the middle of describing and sketching the battle. The last words found in his notebook were these: "A Southerner is aiming at me, and..." And the Southerner missed Gédéon Spilett, for, as was his unfailing habit, he came out of the affair without a scratch.

Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett, who knew each other only by reputation, had both been taken to Richmond. The engineer's wound healed quickly, and it was while he was recovering that he met the reporter. The two men liked each other and came to value each other. Soon the life they shared had only one aim: to escape, to rejoin Grant's army, and to fight in its ranks again for the unity of the Union.

So the two Americans had made up their minds to seize any chance that came. But although they had been left free to move about the city, Richmond was so strictly guarded that escape had to be considered impossible.

Meanwhile Cyrus Smith had been joined by a servant who was devoted to him in life and in death. This fearless man was a Black man, born on the engineer's estate to a father and mother who were slaves. But Cyrus Smith, an abolitionist by reason and by heart, had freed him long ago. The slave who had become a free man had not wanted to leave his master. He loved him enough to die for him. He was thirty years old, strong, nimble, skillful, intelligent, gentle and calm, sometimes naive, always smiling, helpful and kind. His name was Nebuchadnezzar, but he answered only to the short, familiar name of Nab.

When Nab learned that his master had been taken prisoner, he left Massachusetts without a moment's hesitation and made his way to Richmond. By cunning and skill, after risking his life twenty times over, he managed to get inside the besieged city. There are no words for Cyrus Smith's pleasure at seeing his servant again, or for Nab's joy at finding his master.

But if Nab had managed to get into Richmond, getting out was another matter entirely, because the Federal prisoners were watched very closely. It would take an extraordinary opportunity to attempt an escape with any chance of success, and not only did that opportunity fail to come, it was very hard to see how to create one.

Meanwhile Grant was pressing on with his operations, hard. His victory at Petersburg had been bitterly contested and very dearly bought. His forces, joined to Butler's, were still getting nowhere in front of Richmond, and nothing suggested that the prisoners would be set free any time soon. The reporter, whose tedious captivity no longer gave him a single interesting detail to write down, could not stand it any longer. He had only one idea: to get out of Richmond, at any cost. Several times he even tried it, and was stopped by obstacles he could not get past.

Meanwhile the siege went on. And if the prisoners were in a hurry to escape and rejoin Grant's army, some of the besieged were in just as much of a hurry to get away and rejoin the secessionist army — among them a certain Jonathan Forster, a fanatical Southerner. For in fact, if the Federal prisoners could not leave the city, neither could the Confederates, because the Northern army had them surrounded. The governor of Richmond had been cut off from General Lee for a long time already, and it was of the greatest importance to let Lee know the city's situation, so as to hurry the relief army along. This Jonathan Forster then had the idea of rising into the air in a balloon, crossing over the besieging lines, and reaching the secessionist camp that way.

The governor authorized the attempt. A balloon was built and put at Jonathan Forster's disposal, and five of his companions were to go up with him. They were supplied with weapons, in case they had to defend themselves when they landed, and with food, in case their voyage through the air went on longer than planned.

The balloon's departure had been set for March 18. It was to take place at night, and with a moderate northwest wind the balloonists expected to reach Lee's headquarters within a few hours. But that northwest wind turned out to be no mere breeze. As early as the 18th, anyone could see it was turning into a hurricane. Soon the storm was so violent that Forster's departure had to be put off, because there was no way to risk the balloon and the men it would carry in the middle of the raging elements.

So the balloon, inflated in the great square of Richmond, stood there ready to leave at the first lull in the wind, and in the city people grew very impatient to see that the weather did not change.

March 18 and 19 went by without any change in the storm. In fact, they had great trouble protecting the balloon, which was tied to the ground and which the gusts kept knocking flat against it.

The night of the 19th to the 20th passed, but in the morning the hurricane was blowing even harder. Departure was impossible.

That day the engineer Cyrus Smith was approached in one of the streets of Richmond by a man he did not know. It was a sailor named Pencroff, somewhere between thirty-five and forty years old, powerfully built, deeply tanned, with bright, blinking eyes but a good, kindly face. This Pencroff was a Northerner who had sailed every sea on the globe, and when it came to adventures, everything extraordinary that can happen to a two-legged creature without feathers had already happened to him. It goes without saying that he had an enterprising nature, ready to dare anything, and that nothing could surprise him. At the start of that year Pencroff had come to Richmond on business with a boy of fifteen, Harbert Brown of New Jersey, the son of his captain — an orphan whom he loved as if he were his own child. Not having managed to leave the city before the first operations of the siege, he found himself trapped there, very much to his displeasure, and he too had only one idea left: to get away by any means possible. He knew the engineer Cyrus Smith by reputation. He knew how impatiently that determined man was chafing at the bit. So that day he did not hesitate to come up to him and say, without any more preamble:

"Mr. Smith, have you had enough of Richmond?"

The engineer stared at the man who had spoken to him like this, and who added in a low voice:

"Mr. Smith, do you want to escape?"

"When?" the engineer answered quickly — and it is safe to say the answer slipped out of him, since he had not yet taken a good look at the stranger speaking to him.

But after studying the sailor's honest face with a penetrating eye, he could not doubt that the man in front of him was a decent one.

"Who are you?" he asked curtly.

Pencroff told him.

"Good," answered Cyrus Smith. "And how do you propose we escape?"

"In that lazy good-for-nothing of a balloon they've left sitting there doing nothing. It looks to me like it's waiting just for us!"

The sailor did not need to finish his sentence. The engineer had understood at a word. He took Pencroff by the arm and led him off to his lodgings.

There the sailor laid out his plan, which was very simple, really. All they risked by carrying it out was their lives. The hurricane was at its most violent, true, but a skillful, daring engineer like Cyrus Smith would surely know how to handle a balloon. If he, Pencroff, had known how to work one, he wouldn't have hesitated to take off — with Harbert, of course. He had seen plenty worse, and he wasn't going to start worrying about a storm at this point!

Cyrus Smith had listened to the sailor without a word, but his eyes were shining. The opportunity was right there, and he was not a man to let it slip. The plan was merely very dangerous — and therefore it could be done. At night, despite the guards, they could get to the balloon, slip into the basket, and cut the ropes that held it down! Of course they might be killed, but on the other hand they might succeed. And without this storm... But without this storm the balloon would have left already, and the opportunity they had looked for so long would not be there now!

"I'm not alone!" Cyrus Smith said at last.

"How many people do you want to bring, then?" asked the sailor.

"Two: my friend Spilett and my servant Nab."

"So that makes three," answered Pencroff, "and with Harbert and me, five. The balloon was supposed to carry six..."

"That's enough. We'll go!" said Cyrus Smith.

That "we" committed the reporter, but the reporter was not a man to back out, and when the plan was explained to him he approved it without reservation. What surprised him was that such a simple idea had not already occurred to him. As for Nab, he followed his master wherever his master wanted to go.

"Tonight, then," said Pencroff. "The five of us will just wander over that way, like people out for a look!"

"Tonight, at ten o'clock," answered Cyrus Smith, "and heaven grant that this storm doesn't die down before we go!"

Pencroff took his leave of the engineer and went back to his lodgings, where young Harbert Brown had stayed behind. The brave boy knew the sailor's plan, and it was with some anxiety that he was waiting to hear how the approach to the engineer had gone. So, as you can see, it was five determined men who were about to throw themselves into the storm, in the teeth of a hurricane!

No! The hurricane did not die down, and neither Jonathan Forster nor his companions could dream of facing it in that flimsy basket! The day was terrible. The engineer feared only one thing: that the balloon, held to the ground and blown over flat by the wind, would be torn into a thousand pieces. For hours he prowled around the nearly deserted square, keeping an eye on it. Pencroff did the same on his side, hands in his pockets, yawning when the occasion called for it, like a man who has no idea how to kill time — but he too was dreading that the balloon would tear, or even break its ropes and fly off into the sky.

Evening came. The night was very dark. Thick mists drifted like clouds along the ground. Rain mixed with snow was falling. It was cold. A kind of fog hung heavy over Richmond. It seemed as if the violent storm had brought about a sort of truce between besiegers and besieged, as if the cannon had decided to fall silent before the tremendous blasts of the hurricane. The streets of the city were empty. In such horrible weather it had not even seemed necessary to guard the square where the balloon lay struggling. Everything was clearly in the prisoners' favor. But a voyage through those raging gusts!

"Nasty tide!" Pencroff muttered to himself, jamming his hat down with his fist as the wind tried to tear it off his head. "But bah! We'll come through all the same!"

At half past nine Cyrus Smith and his companions slipped into the square from different directions. The gas lamps had been blown out by the wind, and the square lay in deep darkness. They could not even see the enormous balloon, which was lying almost completely flattened on the ground. Besides the ballast bags that weighted the cords of the net, the basket was held by a strong cable passed through a ring set into the paving stones, with both ends brought back up on board.

The five prisoners met beside the basket. No one had seen them, and it was so dark that they could not even see each other.

Without a word, Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, Nab and Harbert took their places in the basket, while Pencroff, on the engineer's orders, unhooked the bundles of ballast one after another. It took only a few moments, and then the sailor joined his companions.

The balloon was now held only by the doubled cable, and all Cyrus Smith had left to do was give the order to go.

At that moment a dog leaped up into the basket in a single bound. It was Top, the engineer's dog, who had broken his chain and followed his master. Afraid of too much weight, Cyrus Smith wanted to send the poor animal back.

"Bah! One more!" said Pencroff, and he heaved two bags of sand out of the basket.

Then he let go one end of the doubled cable, and the balloon shot off at a slant and vanished — after knocking its basket against two chimneys and bringing them down in the fury of its departure.

The hurricane was now raging with appalling violence. During the night the engineer could not even think of trying to come down, and when day came, the mists hid every sight of land. It was only five days later that a break in the clouds revealed the immense sea beneath the balloon, which the wind was sweeping along at a terrifying speed!

We know how, of those five men who had left on March 20, four were thrown on March 24 onto a deserted coast more than six thousand miles from their country — while at home, on April 5, Richmond fell to Grant, the secessionists' revolt was crushed, Lee withdrew to the west, and the cause of American unity triumphed!

And the one who was missing, the one the four survivors of the balloon had rushed off to rescue first of all, was their natural leader — the engineer Cyrus Smith!
