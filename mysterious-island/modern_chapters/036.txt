Chapter 15: A Few Tears

_The return — Discussion — Cyrus Smith and the stranger — Balloon Harbor — The third harvest — A windmill — The first flour and the first bread — The engineer's devotion — A moving experiment — A few tears fall_

The next day, October 20, at seven o'clock in the morning, after four days at sea, the Bonadventure ran gently aground on the beach at the mouth of the Mercy.

Cyrus Smith and Nab had grown very uneasy over the bad weather and their companions' long absence. They had climbed up to Prospect Plateau at first light, and at last they had sighted the boat that had taken so long to come back!

"Thank God! There they are!" Cyrus Smith had cried.

As for Nab, he had started dancing for joy, spinning round and round, clapping his hands and shouting, "Oh, my master!" — a pantomime more touching than the finest speech!

The engineer's first thought, as he counted the people he could see on the Bonadventure's deck, had been that Pencroff had not found the castaway of Tabor Island — or at least that the poor man had refused to leave his island and trade one prison for another.

And in fact Pencroff, Gédéon Spilett and Harbert were alone on the Bonadventure's deck.

When the boat came in, the engineer and Nab were waiting for her on the shore, and before the passengers had even jumped down onto the sand, Cyrus Smith was saying to them:

"We've been very worried that you were so late, my friends! Did something go wrong?"

"No," replied Gédéon Spilett. "Quite the opposite — everything went wonderfully. We'll tell you all about it."

"All the same," the engineer went on, "your search failed, since there are only three of you, just as when you left?"

"Beg your pardon, Mr. Cyrus," replied the sailor. "There are four of us!"

"You found the castaway?"

"Yes."

"And you brought him back?"

"Yes."

"Alive?"

"Yes."

"Where is he? Who is he?"

"He is," answered the reporter, "or rather he was, a man! That's all we can tell you, Cyrus!"

The engineer was told at once what had happened on the voyage. They described how the search had been carried out; how the islet's one dwelling had been abandoned long ago; and finally how they had captured a castaway who no longer seemed to belong to the human race.

"It's got to the point," Pencroff added, "where I don't know if we did right to bring him here."

"Of course you did right, Pencroff!" the engineer answered quickly.

"But the poor wretch has lost his reason, hasn't he?"

"Now, perhaps," replied Cyrus Smith. "But only a few months ago this unhappy man was a man like you and me. And who knows what would become of the last one of us left alive, after a long solitude on this island? Woe to the man who is alone, my friends! Isolation must destroy the reason quickly indeed, since you found the poor creature in such a state!"

"But, Mr. Cyrus," asked Harbert, "what makes you think the poor man has only sunk this low in the last few months?"

"Because the document we found had been written recently," answered the engineer, "and no one but the castaway could have written it."

"Unless," Gédéon Spilett pointed out, "it was written by a companion of his who has since died."

"That's impossible, my dear Spilett."

"Why?" asked the reporter.

"Because the document would have spoken of two castaways," replied Cyrus Smith, "and it speaks of only one."

Harbert told the story of the crossing in a few words, and he dwelt on one curious fact: the passing sort of resurrection that had taken place in the prisoner's mind when, for a moment, at the height of the storm, he had become a sailor again.

"Good, Harbert," replied the engineer. "You're right to attach great importance to that. This unfortunate man can't be incurable. It's despair that has made him what he is. But here he'll find his fellow men again, and since there's still a soul in him, we'll save that soul!"

So the castaway of Tabor Island was brought out of the cabin he occupied in the Bonadventure's bow, to the engineer's deep pity and Nab's great astonishment. The moment he was set ashore, he showed every intention of running away.

But Cyrus Smith came up to him and laid a hand on his shoulder with a gesture full of authority, and looked at him with infinite gentleness. At once the wretched man fell under a kind of instant spell. Little by little he grew calm; his eyes dropped, his head bowed, and he put up no more resistance.

"Poor marooned soul!" murmured the engineer.

Cyrus Smith had studied him closely. To judge by appearances, there was nothing human left in this miserable being. And yet Cyrus Smith, as the reporter had done before him, caught in his look something like an elusive glimmer of intelligence.

It was decided that the marooned man — or rather the stranger, for that was what his new companions called him from then on — would stay in one of the rooms of Granite House. He could not escape from there in any case. He let himself be led to it without any trouble, and with good care to help, they could perhaps hope that one day he would make one more companion for the colonists of Lincoln Island.

During lunch, which Nab had hurried along — the reporter, Harbert and Pencroff were dying of hunger — Cyrus Smith had them tell him in detail about everything that had happened on the voyage of exploration to the islet. He agreed with his friends that the stranger must be English or American. The name Britannia suggested as much, and besides, through that unkempt beard, under the thicket that served him for hair, the engineer believed he had made out the distinctive features of an Anglo-Saxon.

"But come to think of it," said Gédéon Spilett, turning to Harbert, "you never told us how you ran into this wild man. All we know is that he'd have strangled you if we hadn't had the luck to get there in time to help you!"

"Honestly," answered Harbert, "I'd be hard put to say what happened. I think I was busy gathering my plants when I heard a noise like an avalanche coming down out of a very tall tree. I barely had time to turn around... This poor man, who must have been crouching up in a tree, threw himself on me in less time than it takes me to tell you, and if it hadn't been for Mr. Spilett and Pencroff..."

"My boy!" said Cyrus Smith. "You ran a real risk there. But perhaps without it the poor creature would always have slipped away from your search, and we wouldn't have one more companion."

"So you hope to make a man of him again, Cyrus?" asked the reporter.

"Yes," replied the engineer.

When lunch was over, Cyrus Smith and his companions left Granite House and went back down to the beach. Then they unloaded the Bonadventure. The engineer examined the weapons and the tools, but he saw nothing that might allow him to establish the stranger's identity.

The pigs captured on the islet were judged likely to be a great asset to Lincoln Island, and they were taken to the sties, where they would surely settle in easily.

The two kegs of powder and shot, and the packets of percussion caps, were very well received. They even agreed to set up a small powder magazine, either outside Granite House or else in the upper cave, where there was no explosion to fear. All the same, they would go on using guncotton. It gave excellent results, so there was no reason to replace it with ordinary gunpowder.

When the boat had been unloaded:

"Mr. Cyrus," said Pencroff, "I think it would be wise to put our Bonadventure somewhere safe."

"Isn't she properly placed at the mouth of the Mercy?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"No, Mr. Cyrus," replied the sailor. "Half the time she's aground on the sand, and that strains her. She's a good boat, you see, and she behaved beautifully in that gale that hit us so hard on the way back."

"Couldn't we keep her afloat in the river itself?"

"We could, Mr. Cyrus, no doubt about it. But the river mouth gives her no shelter at all, and in an east wind I think the Bonadventure would take a terrible beating from the seas."

"Well, then, where do you want to put her, Pencroff?"

"At Balloon Harbor," replied the sailor. "That little cove, sheltered by the rocks, looks to me like just the harbor she needs."

"Isn't it a bit far?"

"Bah! It's not more than three miles from Granite House, and we've got a fine straight road to take us there!"

"Go ahead, Pencroff, and take your Bonadventure there," replied the engineer. "Still, I'd rather have her where we can keep a closer eye on her. When we have the time, we'll have to build her a little harbor of her own."

"Splendid!" cried Pencroff. "A harbor with a lighthouse, a breakwater and a dry dock! Honestly, Mr. Cyrus, with you everything's too easy!"

"Yes, my good Pencroff," replied the engineer, "but only on condition that you help me — because you do a good three-quarters of all our work!"

So Harbert and the sailor went back aboard the Bonadventure. The anchor was weighed, the sail hoisted, and the sea breeze carried her swiftly to Claw Cape. Two hours later she was riding on the calm waters of Balloon Harbor.

In the first days the stranger spent at Granite House, had he already given any reason to think that his wild nature had changed? Was a brighter light shining in the depths of that darkened mind? Was the soul, in short, coming back into the body? Yes, beyond any doubt — so much so that Cyrus Smith and the reporter wondered whether the poor man's reason had ever been completely snuffed out.

At first, used as he was to the open air and to the boundless freedom he had enjoyed on Tabor Island, the stranger had shown some fits of sullen rage, and they had to fear that he might hurl himself down onto the beach through one of the windows of Granite House. But little by little he calmed down, and they were able to leave him free to move about as he liked.

So there was reason to hope, and to hope a great deal. Already the stranger was forgetting the instincts of a beast of prey. He accepted food less brutish than what he had gorged himself on at the islet, and cooked meat no longer filled him with the disgust he had shown aboard the Bonadventure.

Cyrus Smith had taken advantage of a moment when he was asleep to cut his unkempt hair and beard, which had formed a kind of mane and given him such a wild look. He had also dressed him more decently, after taking away the scrap of cloth that had covered him. Thanks to this care, the stranger took on a human face again, and it even seemed that his eyes had grown gentler. Surely, when intelligence once lit it, this man's face must have had a sort of handsomeness.

Every day, Cyrus Smith made it his duty to spend a few hours in his company. He would come and work near him, busying himself with one thing and another, so as to catch his attention. A single flash, after all, might be enough to rekindle that soul; a single memory crossing that brain might be enough to call his reason back. They had seen it happen during the storm aboard the Bonadventure!

The engineer also made a point of talking out loud, so as to reach the depths of that numbed intelligence through the ears and the eyes at the same time. Sometimes one of his companions joined him, sometimes another, sometimes all of them. Most often they talked about things to do with the sea, which ought to move a sailor more than anything. At moments the stranger seemed to pay a vague kind of attention to what was being said, and the colonists soon became convinced that he partly understood them. Sometimes, even, his face wore an expression of deep pain — proof that he was suffering inside, for his features could not have been so misleading as that. But he did not speak, though more than once they might have thought a few words were about to escape his lips.

Be that as it may, the poor man was calm and sad! But was his calm only on the surface? Was his sadness only the result of being shut in? Nothing could be said for certain yet. He now saw only certain things, within a narrow field; he was constantly in contact with the colonists, and he was bound to grow used to them in the end; he had no desire left unsatisfied; he was better fed and better clothed. It was only natural that his physical nature should change little by little. But had a new life really entered into him? Or — to use a word that could fairly be applied to him — had he only been tamed, the way an animal is tamed by its master? That was an important question, and Cyrus Smith was eager to settle it. Yet he did not want to rush his patient! For to him the stranger was simply a patient! Would he ever be a convalescent?

And so how closely the engineer watched him, every moment! How he lay in wait for his soul, if one may put it that way! How ready he was to seize it!

The colonists followed every stage of the cure Cyrus Smith had undertaken with sincere emotion. They helped him, too, in this work of humanity, and all of them — except perhaps the doubting Pencroff — soon came to share his hope and his faith.

The stranger's calm was deep, as has been said, and he showed a kind of attachment to the engineer, whose influence he plainly felt. So Cyrus Smith decided to put him to the test by taking him into different surroundings: before the ocean his eyes had once been used to gazing on, at the edge of forests that must remind him of the ones where so many years of his life had been spent!

"But," said Gédéon Spilett, "can we hope that once he's set free he won't run away?"

"It's an experiment we have to try," answered the engineer.

"Ha!" said Pencroff. "Once that fellow has open space in front of him and gets a whiff of the fresh air, he'll be off as fast as his legs will carry him!"

"I don't think so," replied Cyrus Smith.

"Let's try it," said Gédéon Spilett.

"Let's try it," replied the engineer.

That day was October 30, which meant the castaway of Tabor Island had been a prisoner in Granite House for nine days. It was warm, and a fine sun was beating down on the island.

Cyrus Smith and Pencroff went to the stranger's room. They found him lying near the window, gazing at the sky.

"Come, my friend," the engineer said to him.

The stranger got up at once. His eyes fixed on Cyrus Smith, and he followed him, while the sailor walked behind, not very confident about how the experiment would turn out.

When they reached the door, Cyrus Smith and Pencroff had him take his place in the elevator, while Nab, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett waited for them at the foot of Granite House. The basket went down, and in a few moments they were all together on the beach.

The colonists moved a little way off from the stranger, so as to leave him some freedom.

He took a few steps toward the sea, and his eyes shone with an extraordinary eagerness, but he made no attempt at all to escape. He was watching the little waves that broke against the islet and came in to die on the sand.

"That's still only the sea," Gédéon Spilett remarked, "and it may not make him want to run off!"

"Yes," replied Cyrus Smith. "We need to take him up to the plateau, to the edge of the forest. There the experiment will tell us more."

"Anyway, he can't get away," Nab pointed out, "since the bridges are raised."

"Oh!" said Pencroff. "As if a man like that would let a little stream like Glycerin Creek stand in his way! He'd be across it in no time — in a single jump, even!"

"We'll see," was all Cyrus Smith answered, and his eyes never left his patient's.

The stranger was then led toward the mouth of the Mercy, and they all climbed up the left bank of the river and reached Prospect Plateau.

When he came to the spot where the first fine trees of the forest grew, their leaves stirring lightly in the breeze, the stranger seemed to drink in, intoxicated, the deep fragrance that filled the air, and a long sigh broke from his chest!

The colonists stayed back, ready to hold him if he made a move to escape!

And in fact the poor creature was on the point of leaping into the creek that separated him from the forest. For an instant his legs tensed like a spring... But almost at once he drew back into himself, he half sank to the ground, and a large tear rolled from his eyes!

"Ah!" cried Cyrus Smith. "So you've become a man again, since you're weeping!"
