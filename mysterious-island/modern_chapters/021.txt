Chapter 22: The Mysterious Well

_The traps — The foxes — The peccaries — The wind swings round to the northwest — A snowstorm — The basket makers — The hardest cold of the winter — Crystallizing the maple sugar — The mysterious well — The planned exploration — The lead pellet_

The bitter cold lasted until August 15, though it never went below the lowest Fahrenheit reading they had seen so far. When the air was still, the low temperature was easy enough to bear. But when the north wind blew, it was hard going for people who were not properly dressed. Pencroff even got to the point of wishing that Lincoln Island had given shelter to a few families of bears, instead of these foxes and seals, whose fur left a lot to be desired.

"Bears," he would say, "are generally well dressed, and I'd ask nothing better than to borrow the warm coats they wear, just for the winter."

"But maybe," Nab answered with a laugh, "those bears wouldn't agree to give you their coats, Pencroff. They aren't Saint Martins, those beasts — they don't go cutting their cloaks in half for whoever's cold!"

"We'd make them, Nab, we'd make them," Pencroff replied, in a thoroughly commanding tone.

But those formidable meat-eaters did not exist on the island — or at least they had not shown themselves so far.

All the same, Harbert, Pencroff, and the reporter set about building traps on Prospect Plateau and along the edges of the forest. In the sailor's opinion any animal at all would be a fine catch, and whatever rodents or meat-eaters were the first to try out the new traps would be welcome at Granite House.

The traps were extremely simple, by the way. They were pits dug in the ground, with a ceiling of branches and grass over the top to hide the opening, and at the bottom some bait whose smell might draw the animals in. That was all. It should be said, though, that they had not been dug just anywhere, but in particular spots where a greater number of tracks showed that four-footed animals passed often. The traps were checked every day, and three times in those first days they found specimens of the culpeos that had already been seen on the right bank of the Mercy.

"Well, now! Is there nothing but foxes in this country?" cried Pencroff, the third time he pulled one of these animals out of the pit, where it was sitting looking thoroughly sheepish. "Beasts that aren't good for anything!"

"Oh, but they are," said Gédéon Spilett. "They're good for something!"

"And what's that?"

"For bait, to catch other animals with!"

The reporter was right, and from then on the traps were baited with the carcasses of these foxes.

The sailor had also made snares out of kurrajong fiber, and the snares brought in more than the traps did. Hardly a day went by without some rabbit from the warren letting itself get caught. It was always rabbit, but Nab knew how to vary his sauces, and the diners never thought of complaining.

Once or twice, however, in the second week of August, the traps gave the hunters animals other than culpeos, and more useful ones. They were some of the wild boars that had already been reported north of the lake. Pencroff did not need to ask whether these beasts were good to eat. That was plain enough from how much they looked like the pigs of America or Europe.

"But these aren't pigs, Pencroff," Harbert told him. "I'm warning you."

"My boy," answered the sailor, leaning over the trap and pulling out one of these representatives of the pig family by the little appendage that served it for a tail, "let me go on believing they're pigs!"

"Why?"

"Because it makes me happy!"

"So you're really fond of pork, Pencroff?"

"I'm very fond of pork," the sailor answered, "especially the feet. And if a pig had eight of them instead of four, I'd like it twice as much!"

As for the animals in question, they were peccaries, belonging to one of the four genera that make up the family. They were even of the species called "tajaçu," the collared peccary, recognizable by its dark color and by the lack of the long canine teeth that arm the mouths of its relatives. Peccaries usually live in herds, and it was likely that they were plentiful in the wooded parts of the island. In any case they were edible from head to foot, and Pencroff asked nothing more of them.

Toward August 15 the weather changed suddenly when the wind swung round to the northwest. The temperature rose a few degrees, and the moisture that had built up in the air soon came down as snow. The whole island was covered with a white layer and showed itself to its inhabitants in a new light. The snow fell heavily for several days, and it was soon two feet deep.

Before long the wind freshened with extreme violence, and from high up in Granite House they could hear the sea roaring over the reefs. At certain corners the air swirled in quick eddies, and the snow, caught up in them, formed tall spinning columns, like those waterspouts at sea that twirl on their bases and that ships fire their cannon at to break them up. Still, the hurricane was coming from the northwest, so it struck the island from behind, and the way Granite House faced protected it from a direct assault. But in the middle of that blizzard, as terrible as if it had been raging over some polar country, neither Cyrus Smith nor his companions could venture outside, however much they wanted to, and they stayed shut in for five days, from August 20 to 25. They could hear the storm roaring in Jacamar Wood, which was bound to suffer for it. Many trees would no doubt be torn up by the roots, but Pencroff consoled himself with the thought that he would not have the trouble of chopping them down.

"The wind's turned woodcutter — let it get on with it," he kept saying.

And anyway, there would have been no way to stop it.

How the residents of Granite House must have thanked Heaven, then, for providing them with this solid, unshakable retreat! Cyrus Smith certainly deserved his fair share of the thanks. But after all, it was nature that had hollowed out this huge cavern, and he had only discovered it. In there, all of them were safe, and the blows of the storm could not reach them. If they had built a house of brick and wood on Prospect Plateau, it would certainly not have stood up to the fury of this hurricane. As for the Chimneys, just from the crash of the breakers, which they could hear so loudly, it was clear that the place must be utterly uninhabitable, because the sea, sweeping over the islet, must be pounding it furiously. But here in Granite House, in the heart of this mass of rock that neither water nor air could get a grip on, there was nothing to fear.

During those few days of confinement the colonists did not sit idle. There was no shortage of wood, already sawn into planks, in the storeroom, and little by little they finished the furniture, tables and chairs, solid ones for sure, since no one had spared the material. These pieces were a bit heavy, and they hardly lived up to the old name for furniture — "movables" — which makes being easy to shift the whole point. But they were the pride of Nab and Pencroff, who would not have traded them for pieces by Boulle, the great cabinetmaker of the French kings.

Then the carpenters became basket makers, and they did not do badly at this new craft either. Near the point that the lake thrust out to the north, they had discovered a rich willow bed where purple osiers grew in large numbers. Before the rainy season Pencroff and Harbert had harvested these useful shrubs, and their stems, now well sorted, could be put to good use. The first attempts were shapeless. But thanks to the skill and intelligence of the workers — talking it over among themselves, remembering models they had seen, trying to outdo one another — baskets and hampers of various sizes soon added to the colony's equipment. The storeroom was stocked with them, and Nab put away his harvests of rootstocks, stone-pine nuts, and dragon-tree roots in special hampers of their own.

During the last week of August the weather changed once again. The temperature dropped a little and the storm died down. The colonists rushed outside. There were easily two feet of snow on the beach, but the surface of the snow had hardened, and they could walk on it without too much trouble. Cyrus Smith and his companions climbed up to Prospect Plateau.

What a change! The woods, which they had left green — especially the nearby part, where conifers grew thickest — had now vanished under a single color. Everything was white, from the summit of Mount Franklin down to the shore: the forests, the meadow, the lake, the river, the beaches. The water of the Mercy ran under a vault of ice, which broke up with a crash at every rise and fall of the tide. Numerous birds were fluttering over the frozen surface of the lake: ducks and snipe, pintails and guillemots. There were thousands of them. The rocks between which the waterfall poured over the edge of the plateau bristled with ice. You would have said the water was gushing from a monstrous gargoyle, carved with all the fancy of a Renaissance artist. As for judging how much damage the hurricane had done to the forest, that was not yet possible. They would have to wait until the immense white layer had melted away.

Gédéon Spilett, Pencroff, and Harbert did not miss this chance to go and check their traps. It was not easy to find them under the snow that covered them. They even had to be careful not to tumble into one or another of them, which would have been dangerous and humiliating at the same time: to be caught in your own trap! But in the end they avoided that embarrassment and found the traps completely intact. No animal had fallen into them, and yet there were many tracks all around, among them certain very sharply printed claw marks. Harbert did not hesitate to declare that some meat-eater of the cat family had passed that way, which bore out the engineer's opinion that there were dangerous wild beasts on Lincoln Island. No doubt these beasts usually lived in the dense forests of the Far West, but, driven by hunger, they had ventured as far as Prospect Plateau. Perhaps they could smell the residents of Granite House?

"So what exactly are these cats of yours?" asked Pencroff.

"They're tigers," Harbert answered.

"I thought those animals were only found in hot countries?"

"In the New World," the boy answered, "they've been seen everywhere from Mexico to the Pampas of Buenos Aires. And since Lincoln Island lies at about the same latitude as the provinces of the River Plate, it isn't surprising that there should be a few tigers here."

"All right, we'll keep watch," Pencroff answered.

Meanwhile the snow eventually disappeared as the temperature rose again. Rain began to fall, and its dissolving action wiped the white layer away. In spite of the bad weather, the colonists restocked their reserves of everything: stone-pine nuts, dragon-tree roots, rootstocks, and maple sap on the vegetable side; rabbits from the warren, agoutis, and kangaroos on the animal side. That called for a few trips into the forest, and they found that a good number of trees had been brought down by the last hurricane. The sailor and Nab even pushed on with the cart as far as the coal seam, to bring back a few tons of fuel. On the way they saw that the chimney of the pottery kiln had been badly damaged by the wind and had lost a good six feet at least from its top.

Along with the coal, the supply of firewood at Granite House was renewed too, and they took advantage of the Mercy's current, which was running free again, to float several rafts of timber down. The period of hard cold might not be over yet.

They had also paid a visit to the Chimneys, and the colonists could only congratulate themselves on not having lived there during the storm. The sea had left unmistakable marks of its ravages. Heaved up by the winds from offshore, it had leaped over the islet and violently attacked the passages, which were now half filled with sand, and thick layers of seaweed covered the rocks. While Nab, Harbert, and Pencroff were hunting or bringing in more fuel, Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett busied themselves clearing out the Chimneys. They found the forge and the furnaces more or less intact, protected as they had been, right from the start, by the sand piled up over them.

It was not for nothing that the fuel supply had been built up again. The colonists were not yet done with the hard cold. In the Northern Hemisphere, as everyone knows, February is the month best known for great drops in temperature. It was bound to be the same in the Southern Hemisphere, and the end of August, which is North America's February, did not escape this law of climate.

Around the 25th, after another round of snow and rain, the wind jumped to the southeast, and all of a sudden the cold became extremely sharp. By the engineer's estimate, the mercury in a Fahrenheit thermometer would have read no less than eight degrees below zero. This fierce cold, made still more painful by a biting wind, held for several days. The colonists had to shut themselves up in Granite House once again. Since every opening in the front of the cliff had to be sealed up tight, leaving only the bare minimum for fresh air to get in, they went through a great many candles. To save them, the colonists often lit the place only by the flames of the fireplaces, where no one stinted on fuel. Several times one or another of them went down to the beach, among the chunks of ice that the rising tide piled up there at every high water. But they soon climbed back up to Granite House, and it was not without trouble and pain that their hands held on to the rungs of the ladder. In that fierce cold, the rungs burned their fingers.

Once again they had to find ways to fill the idle hours that this confinement forced on the residents of Granite House. So Cyrus Smith took on a job that could be done behind closed doors.

As we know, the only sugar the colonists had was the liquid they drew from the maple by cutting deep notches in the tree. All they had to do was collect this sap in containers, and they used it as it was for all sorts of cooking, all the more readily because, as it aged, the sap tended to turn white and take on the thickness of syrup.

But they could do better than that, and one day Cyrus Smith announced to his companions that they were about to turn into sugar refiners.

"Refiners!" said Pencroff. "That's a pretty hot trade, isn't it?"

"Very hot!" answered the engineer.

"Then it's just right for the season!" the sailor shot back.

The word "refining" should not bring to mind those factories full of complicated machinery and crowds of workers. No! To crystallize this sap, all they had to do was purify it by an extremely easy process. It was put on the fire in big earthenware pots and simply allowed to boil down for a while, and soon a scum rose to the surface. As soon as it began to thicken, Nab took care to stir it with a wooden spatula, which would speed up the evaporation and at the same time keep it from taking on a burnt taste.

After a few hours of boiling over a good fire — which did as much good to the workers as to the stuff they were working on — the sap had turned into a thick syrup. This syrup was poured into clay molds, which had been made beforehand and fired in the kitchen stove itself, and which had been given all sorts of shapes. The next day the syrup had cooled and set into loaves and tablets. It was sugar — a little reddish in color, but almost transparent, and with a perfect taste.

The cold went on until the middle of September, and the prisoners of Granite House were beginning to find their captivity very long. Almost every day they tried a few outings, which could not last long. So they worked constantly at fitting out their home, and they talked as they worked. Cyrus Smith taught his companions about everything, and above all he explained the practical uses of science to them. The colonists had no library at their disposal. But the engineer was a book always ready, always open to the page each one needed — a book that answered all their questions, and that they leafed through often. So the time went by, and these good people did not seem to fear the future at all.

All the same, it was time for this confinement to end. They were all eager to see, if not the fine season, then at least the end of this unbearable cold. If only they had been dressed well enough to brave it, what trips they would have made, to the dunes, or to Shelduck Marsh! The game must have been easy to get close to, and the hunting would certainly have been rewarding. But Cyrus Smith insisted that no one should put his health at risk, because he needed every pair of hands, and his advice was followed.

But it must be said that the most impatient of all at this imprisonment — after Pencroff, at any rate — was Top. The faithful dog felt very cramped in Granite House. He kept going back and forth from one room to the next, and in his own way he showed how bored he was at being cooped up.

Cyrus Smith often noticed that whenever Top went near the dark well that connected with the sea, the one whose opening was at the back of the storeroom, he would give strange growls. Top would circle around this hole, which had been covered over with a wooden lid. Sometimes he even tried to slip his paws under the lid, as if he wanted to lift it. Then he would yelp in a peculiar way, a way that showed both anger and uneasiness.

The engineer watched this performance several times. What could there be in that pit to make such an impression on the intelligent animal? The well ran down to the sea, that much was certain. Did it branch off, then, into narrow tunnels running through the framework of the island? Did it connect with other caverns inside? Did some sea monster come, now and then, to breathe at the bottom of the well? The engineer did not know what to think, and he could not stop himself from dreaming up strange possibilities. Used as he was to traveling far into the realm of scientific fact, he could not forgive himself for letting himself be drawn into the realm of the strange, almost of the supernatural. But how else could he explain why Top — one of those sensible dogs that have never wasted their time barking at the moon — kept stubbornly probing that pit with his nose and his ears, if nothing was happening down there that ought to rouse his uneasiness? Top's behavior puzzled Cyrus Smith more than seemed reasonable to admit, even to himself.

In any case, the engineer shared his impressions only with Gédéon Spilett. He saw no point in letting his companions in on the thoughts that kept coming to him in spite of himself, over what was perhaps nothing more than a whim of Top's.

At last the cold ended. There were rains, squalls mixed with snow, sudden showers of sleet and hail, gusts of wind — but this foul weather did not last. The ice had melted and the snow was gone. The beach, the plateau, the banks of the Mercy, and the forest could all be used again. The return of spring delighted the residents of Granite House, and soon they were spending only the hours of sleep and meals inside.

They hunted a great deal in the second half of September, which led Pencroff to demand, more insistently than ever, the firearms that he claimed Cyrus Smith had promised him. The engineer knew very well that without special tools it would be almost impossible for him to make a gun that would be of any real use, so he kept putting the job off until later. Besides, he pointed out, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett had become skilled archers. All sorts of excellent animals — agoutis, kangaroos, capybaras, pigeons, bustards, wild ducks, snipe, in short every kind of game, furred or feathered — were falling to their arrows, and so they could afford to wait. But the stubborn sailor would not hear of it, and he would give the engineer no rest until his wish was granted. Gédéon Spilett, for that matter, backed Pencroff up.

"If the island has fierce animals on it, as we have some reason to suspect," he said, "we have to think about fighting them and wiping them out. A time may come when that's our first duty."

But at that point it was not the question of firearms that was on Cyrus Smith's mind. It was the question of clothing. The clothes the colonists wore had lasted through the winter, but they would not hold out until the next one. Hides from meat-eating animals or wool from grazing ones — that was what they had to get, at any cost. And since there was no shortage of mouflons, the thing to do was to find a way to gather a flock of them, which would be raised for the colony's needs. A pen for the domestic animals, a poultry yard fitted out for the birds — in short, a kind of farm to be set up at some spot on the island: those would be the two important projects to carry out during the fine season.

So, with these future settlements in view, it was becoming urgent to make a thorough reconnaissance of the whole unknown part of Lincoln Island — that is, under the tall forests that stretched along the right bank of the Mercy, from its mouth to the tip of Serpentine Peninsula, as well as along the whole of the west coast. But they needed settled weather, and another month would have to go by before the exploration could usefully be undertaken.

So they were waiting with a certain impatience, when something happened that made the colonists still more eager to visit every corner of their domain.

It was October 24. That day Pencroff had gone to check the traps, which he always kept properly baited. In one of them he found three animals that were sure to be welcome in the pantry. They were a female peccary and her two young.

So Pencroff came back to Granite House delighted with his catch, and, as always, the sailor made a great show of his hunting.

"Come on! We're going to have a fine meal, Mr. Cyrus!" he cried. "And you too, Mr. Spilett, you'll be eating some of this!"

"I'm quite willing to eat some," the reporter answered. "But what is it I'll be eating?"

"Suckling pig."

"Oh, really? Suckling pig, Pencroff? To hear you talk, I thought you were bringing back a partridge stuffed with truffles!"

"What?" cried Pencroff. "Are you turning up your nose at suckling pig, by any chance?"

"No," answered Gédéon Spilett, without showing any enthusiasm, "as long as we don't overdo it..."

"All right, all right, Mr. Journalist," the sailor shot back — he did not like to hear his hunting run down. "So you're getting fussy, are you? And seven months ago, when we landed on the island, you'd have been only too happy to come across game like this!..."

"There you go, there you go," the reporter answered. "Man is never perfect, and never satisfied."

"Well," Pencroff went on, "I hope Nab will outdo himself. Look! These two little peccaries aren't even three months old! They'll be as tender as quail! Come on, Nab, come with me! I'll keep an eye on the cooking myself."

And the sailor, with Nab following him, made for the kitchen and buried himself in his culinary labors.

They let him do it his own way. So he and Nab prepared a magnificent meal: the two little peccaries, a kangaroo soup, a smoked ham, stone-pine nuts, dragon-tree drink, Oswego tea — everything they had that was best, in short. But among all the dishes, the place of honor was to go to the delicious peccaries, braised in their own juices.

At five o'clock dinner was served in the dining room of Granite House. The kangaroo soup was steaming on the table. Everyone found it excellent.

After the soup came the peccaries, which Pencroff insisted on carving himself, and he served each of the diners an enormous helping.

These suckling pigs were truly delicious, and Pencroff was devouring his share with tremendous gusto, when all at once a cry and an oath escaped him.

"What is it?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"It's... it's... I've just broken a tooth!" answered the sailor.

"Well, now! Are there pebbles in your peccaries, then?" said Gédéon Spilett.

"Must be," answered Pencroff, taking out of his mouth the object that had just cost him a molar!...

It was not a pebble...

It was a lead pellet.
