Chapter 10: A Deadly Fever

_Harbert carried to Granite House — Nab tells what happened — Cyrus Smith visits the plateau — Ruin and devastation — The colonists helpless against illness — Willow bark — A deadly fever — Top barks again!_

The convicts, the dangers that threatened Granite House, the ruins that covered the plateau — none of it mattered anymore. Harbert's condition outweighed everything. Had the journey done him fatal harm by causing some injury inside? The reporter could not say, but he and his companions were in despair.

The cart was brought to the bend of the river. There a few branches, laid out in the shape of a stretcher, received the mattresses on which Harbert lay unconscious. Ten minutes later Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett and Pencroff were at the foot of the cliff, leaving Nab to take the cart back up to Prospect Plateau.

The elevator was set in motion, and soon Harbert was lying in his own bed in Granite House.

The care they lavished on him brought him back to life. He smiled for a moment when he found himself in his room again, but he could barely murmur a few words, he was so weak.

Gédéon Spilett examined his wounds. He was afraid they had reopened, since they had not fully healed... but they had not. Then where did this collapse come from? Why had Harbert's condition grown worse?

The boy then fell into a kind of feverish sleep, and the reporter and Pencroff stayed by his bed.

Meanwhile Cyrus Smith was telling Nab what had happened at the corral, and Nab was telling his master about the events that had just taken place on the plateau.

It was only the night before that the convicts had shown themselves at the edge of the forest, near Glycerin Creek. Nab, who was keeping watch near the poultry yard, had not hesitated to fire at one of the pirates as he was getting ready to cross the stream; but the night was fairly dark, and he could not tell whether he had hit the wretch. In any case, that had not been enough to drive the gang off, and Nab had only just had time to get back up to Granite House, where at least he was safe.

But what was he to do then? How could he prevent the destruction the convicts were threatening to wreak on the plateau? Did Nab have any way of warning his master? And besides, what situation were the people at the corral in themselves?

Cyrus Smith and his companions had left on November 11, and it was now the 29th. So for nineteen days Nab had had no news except what Top had brought him, and that news was disastrous: Ayrton gone, Harbert badly wounded, the engineer, the reporter and the sailor all but imprisoned in the corral!

What was he to do? poor Nab kept asking himself. For himself he had nothing to fear, since the convicts could not reach him in Granite House. But the buildings, the plantations, everything they had built up, all at the mercy of the pirates! Wouldn't it be right to leave it to Cyrus Smith to judge what had to be done, and at least to warn him of the danger that hung over him?

That was when Nab had the idea of using Jup and trusting him with a note. He knew how extremely intelligent the orangutan was; it had been put to the test many times. Jup understood the word "corral," which had often been spoken in front of him, and, as the reader will remember, he had often driven the cart there with Pencroff. Day had not yet broken. The nimble orangutan would know how to slip through those woods unseen — and in any case the convicts would take him for one of their natural inhabitants.

Nab did not hesitate. He wrote the note, he tied it around Jup's neck, he led the ape to the door of Granite House, let a long rope unroll from it all the way to the ground, and then, several times over, he repeated these words:

"Jup! Jup! Corral! Corral!"

The animal understood, seized the rope, slid quickly down to the beach and disappeared into the darkness, without stirring the convicts' attention in the least.

"You did well, Nab," Cyrus Smith answered, "but perhaps you'd have done even better not to warn us!"

And as he said this, Cyrus Smith was thinking of Harbert, whose recovery the journey seemed to have put in such grave danger.

Nab finished his story. The convicts had not shown themselves on the beach. Not knowing how many people lived on the island, they might suppose that Granite House was defended by a sizable force. They must have remembered that during the attack on the brig they had been met by a heavy fire from both the lower rocks and the upper ones, and no doubt they did not want to expose themselves to it. But Prospect Plateau lay open to them, and the guns of Granite House could not sweep it. So there they gave free rein to their instinct for plunder — looting, burning, doing harm for harm's sake — and they withdrew only half an hour before the colonists arrived, believing them still shut up in the corral.

Nab had rushed out of his refuge. He had climbed back up onto the plateau, at the risk of catching a bullet, he had tried to put out the fire that was devouring the poultry-yard buildings, and he had fought the flames, but in vain, until the moment the cart appeared at the edge of the woods.

Such had been these grave events. The presence of the convicts was a permanent threat to the colonists of Lincoln Island, who had been so happy until then — and who could expect still greater misfortunes to come!

Gédéon Spilett stayed in Granite House with Harbert and Pencroff, while Cyrus Smith, with Nab beside him, went to judge for himself how great the disaster was.

It was lucky that the convicts had not come as far as the foot of Granite House. The workshops at the Chimneys would not have escaped destruction. But then again, that damage might have been easier to repair than the ruins piled up on Prospect Plateau!

Cyrus Smith and Nab made their way toward the Mercy and walked up its left bank without meeting any trace of the convicts' passage. On the other side of the river, in the thick of the woods, they saw no suspicious sign either.

Besides, in all probability, one of two things could be assumed. Either the convicts knew that the colonists had come back to Granite House — they might have seen them go by on the Corral Road — or else, after laying waste the plateau, they had plunged into Jacamar Wood, following the course of the Mercy, and knew nothing of the colonists' return.

In the first case, they would have gone back toward the corral, which was now undefended and held supplies that were precious to them.

In the second, they would have gone back to their camp to wait there for some chance to renew the attack.

So there would be good reason to strike first; but any undertaking meant to rid the island of them still depended on Harbert's condition. Cyrus Smith would need every one of his men, and no one could leave Granite House at that moment.

The engineer and Nab reached the plateau. It was a scene of desolation. The fields had been trampled. The ears of grain, which were just about to be harvested, lay on the ground. The other plantings had suffered no less. The kitchen garden was torn up. Fortunately Granite House held a reserve of seeds that would make it possible to repair the damage.

As for the mill, the poultry-yard buildings and the onaggas' stable, the fire had destroyed them all.

A few frightened animals were wandering across the plateau. The birds, which had taken refuge on the waters of the lake during the fire, were already coming back to their usual places and dabbling along the shore. Here everything would have to be done over again.

Cyrus Smith's face, paler than usual, showed an inner anger he was mastering only with difficulty, but he did not say a word. He took one last look at his ravaged fields and at the smoke still rising from the ruins, and then he went back to Granite House.

The days that followed were the saddest the colonists had yet spent on the island! Harbert was visibly growing weaker. It seemed that a more serious illness, the result of the deep upheaval his body had suffered, was threatening to break out, and Gédéon Spilett foresaw such a turn for the worse that he would be powerless to fight it!

And indeed Harbert lay in an almost continuous drowsiness, and a few signs of delirium began to show. Cooling herbal teas were the only remedies the colonists had. The fever was not yet very high, but soon it seemed bent on settling into regular attacks.

Gédéon Spilett recognized it on December 6. The poor boy's fingers, nose and ears turned extremely pale, and at first he was seized with light chills, with goose flesh, with shivering. His pulse was weak and irregular, his skin dry, his thirst intense. This stage was soon followed by a stage of heat: his face flushed, his skin reddened, his pulse quickened. Then came a heavy sweat, after which the fever seemed to drop. The attack had lasted about five hours.

Gédéon Spilett had not left Harbert's side. The boy now had an intermittent fever — that was only too certain — and this fever had to be cut short at all costs before it grew worse.

"And to cut it short," Gédéon Spilett said to Cyrus Smith, "we need a febrifuge — something that brings fever down."

"A febrifuge!" the engineer answered. "We have no cinchona bark and no quinine sulfate!"

"No," said Gédéon Spilett, "but there are willows on the shore of the lake, and willow bark can sometimes take the place of quinine."

"Then let's try it without losing a moment!" answered Cyrus Smith.

Willow bark, in fact, has rightly been regarded as a substitute for cinchona, like the horse chestnut, holly leaves, snakeroot and others. They obviously had to try it, even though it was not as good as cinchona, and they would have to use it in its natural state, since they had no means of extracting its alkaloid — that is, salicin.

Cyrus Smith went himself to cut a few pieces of bark from the trunk of a kind of black willow. He brought them back to Granite House, ground them to a powder, and that powder was given to Harbert that same evening.

The night passed without any serious incident. Harbert was somewhat delirious, but the fever did not come back in the night, and it did not come back the following day either.

Pencroff began to hope again. Gédéon Spilett said nothing. It might be that the attacks did not come every day — that the fever was tertian, in a word, returning every other day — and that it would come back the day after. So that day was awaited with the keenest anxiety.

It could be noticed, besides, that in the fever-free interval Harbert lay as if shattered, his head heavy and easily dizzy. And there was another symptom that frightened the reporter to the last degree: Harbert's liver was beginning to become congested, and soon a more violent delirium showed that his brain was being affected too.

Gédéon Spilett was appalled by this new complication. He took the engineer aside.

"It's a pernicious fever!" he told him.

"A pernicious fever!" cried Cyrus Smith. "You're mistaken, Spilett. A pernicious fever doesn't break out on its own. You have to have caught the germ of it!"

"I'm not mistaken," answered the reporter. "No doubt Harbert picked up that germ in the island's marshes, and that's enough. He's already had a first attack. If a second one comes, and if we can't prevent the third... he's lost!"

"But the willow bark?"

"It isn't enough," answered the reporter, "and a third attack of pernicious fever that isn't cut short with quinine is always fatal!"

Fortunately Pencroff had heard none of this conversation. It would have driven him mad.

It is easy to imagine the anxiety the engineer and the reporter went through during that day of December 7 and the night that followed.

Toward the middle of the day the second attack came. The crisis was terrible. Harbert felt that he was lost! He stretched out his arms to Cyrus Smith, to Spilett, to Pencroff! He did not want to die!... It was a heartbreaking scene. Pencroff had to be led away.

The attack lasted five hours. It was clear that Harbert would not survive a third.

The night was dreadful. In his delirium Harbert said things that broke his companions' hearts! He raved, he fought the convicts, he called for Ayrton! He pleaded with that mysterious being, that protector who had vanished now and whose image haunted him... then he fell back into a deep prostration that drained the life from him entirely... Several times Gédéon Spilett thought the poor boy was dead!

The next day, December 8, was nothing but one fainting spell after another. Harbert's wasted hands clutched at his sheets. They had given him fresh doses of the ground bark, but the reporter no longer expected anything from it.

"If we haven't given him a stronger febrifuge before tomorrow morning," said the reporter, "Harbert will be dead!"

Night came — the last night, no doubt, of this brave, good, intelligent boy, so far beyond his years, whom they all loved like their own son! The only remedy that existed against this terrible pernicious fever, the only specific that could defeat it, was not to be found on Lincoln Island!

During that night of December 8 to 9, Harbert was seized again by a more violent delirium. His liver was horribly congested, his brain was under attack, and by now it was impossible for him to recognize anyone.

Would he live until the morning, until that third attack that was bound to carry him off? It no longer seemed likely. His strength was exhausted, and between the crises he lay as if lifeless.

Toward three o'clock in the morning Harbert gave a terrifying cry. He seemed to writhe in a final convulsion. Nab, who was beside him, rushed in terror into the next room, where his companions were keeping watch!

At that moment Top barked in a strange way...

They all came back in at once and managed to hold down the dying boy, who was trying to throw himself out of bed, while Gédéon Spilett, taking his arm, felt his pulse slowly rising...

It was five o'clock in the morning. The rays of the rising sun were beginning to slip into the rooms of Granite House. A fine day was dawning, and that day would be poor Harbert's last!...

A ray of light slipped as far as the table that stood near the bed.

Suddenly Pencroff gave a cry and pointed to an object lying on that table...

It was a small oblong box, and on its lid were these words:

    Quinine Sulfate.
