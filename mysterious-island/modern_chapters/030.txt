Chapter 9: The Hydraulic Elevator

_Bad weather — The hydraulic elevator — Making window glass and glassware — The bread tree — Frequent visits to the corral — The flock grows — A question from the reporter — The exact position of Lincoln Island — Pencroff's proposal_

The weather changed during the first week of March. There had been a full moon at the start of the month, and the heat was still fierce. The air felt charged with electricity, and a spell of stormy weather, longer or shorter, was truly to be feared.

And sure enough, on the 2nd, thunder rumbled with tremendous violence. The wind was blowing from the east, and hail struck full on the front of Granite House, rattling against it like a volley of grapeshot. They had to shut the door and the window shutters tight, or everything inside the rooms would have been flooded.

When Pencroff saw those hailstones coming down, some of them as big as a pigeon's egg, he had only one thought: his wheat field was in the gravest danger.

He ran straight to his field, where the ears were already beginning to lift their little green heads, and he managed to protect his crop by spreading a big sheet of canvas over it. He was stoned in its place, but he did not complain.

This bad weather lasted a week, and all that time the thunder never stopped rolling in the depths of the sky. Between two storms you could still hear it growling dully somewhere beyond the horizon; then it would start again with fresh fury. The sky was streaked with lightning, and bolts struck several trees on the island, among them an enormous pine that stood near the lake at the edge of the forest. Two or three times, too, the lightning hit the beach, melting the sand and turning it to glass. When the engineer found these fulgurites — tubes of sand fused by lightning — they led him to think it might be possible to fit the windows with thick, strong panes of glass that could stand up to wind, rain and hail.

With no urgent work to do outdoors, the colonists made use of the bad weather to work inside Granite House, which was being fitted out more fully and more comfortably day by day. The engineer set up a lathe, which let him turn a few utensils for washing or for the kitchen, and above all buttons, which they had been sorely missing. A rack had been put up for the guns, which were kept with the greatest care, and the shelves and cupboards left nothing to be desired. They sawed, they planed, they filed, they turned, and all through that stretch of bad weather nothing could be heard but the squeal of tools and the hum of the lathe, answering the rumble of the thunder.

Master Jup had not been forgotten. He had a room of his own near the main storeroom, a sort of cabin with a bunk always full of good bedding, which suited him perfectly.

"With good old Jup there's never any back talk," Pencroff often said, "never a rude answer! What a servant, Nab! What a servant!"

"My pupil," Nab would answer, "and soon my equal!"

"Your superior, you mean," the sailor would shoot back, laughing, "because you talk, Nab, and he doesn't!"

It goes without saying that Jup now knew his duties thoroughly. He beat the clothes, he turned the spit, he swept the rooms, he waited at table, he stacked the firewood, and — a detail that delighted Pencroff — he never went to bed without first coming to tuck the worthy sailor in.

As for the health of the members of the colony, two-legged or two-handed, four-handed or four-legged, it left nothing to be desired. Living this open-air life, on this healthy soil, in this temperate zone, working with head and hands, they could not believe that sickness would ever touch them.

And indeed they were all marvelously well. Harbert had already grown two inches in a year. His face was taking shape and becoming more manly, and he promised to be a young man as fine in body as in character. Besides, he used every spare moment his manual work left him to learn. He read the few books that had been found in the chest, and beyond the practical lessons that came out of the very necessities of their situation, he had in the engineer a teacher for the sciences and in the reporter a teacher for languages, and both were glad to round out his education.

The engineer's fixed idea was to pass on to the boy everything he knew, to teach him by example as much as by word, and Harbert made the most of his teacher's lessons.

"If I die," Cyrus Smith thought, "he will take my place!"

The storm ended around March 9, but the sky stayed covered with clouds for the whole of that last month of summer. The atmosphere, violently shaken by all those electrical upheavals, could not get back its former clearness, and there was rain and fog almost every day, apart from three or four fine days that allowed all kinds of excursions.

Around this time the female onagga gave birth to a foal, a female like her mother, which thrived wonderfully. At the corral, likewise, the flock of mouflons increased, and several lambs were already bleating in the sheds, to the great delight of Nab and Harbert, who each had a favorite among the newborns.

They also tried domesticating the peccaries, and the experiment was a complete success. A sty was built near the poultry yard, and it soon held several young ones well on the way to becoming civilized — that is, to getting fat under Nab's care. Master Jup, whose job was to bring them their daily food — dishwater, kitchen scraps and so on — did his task conscientiously. Now and then, it is true, he amused himself at the expense of his little boarders by pulling their tails. But that was mischief, not malice: those little curly tails amused him like a toy, and his instincts were a child's.

One day that March, Pencroff, chatting with the engineer, reminded Cyrus Smith of a promise he had not yet had time to keep.

"You talked about a contraption that would do away with the long ladders at Granite House, Mr. Cyrus," he said. "Aren't you going to put it in someday?"

"You mean a sort of elevator?" answered Cyrus Smith.

"Let's call it an elevator, if you like," the sailor replied. "The name doesn't matter, as long as it takes us up to our house without wearing us out."

"Nothing could be easier, Pencroff. But is it really worth it?"

"Certainly, Mr. Cyrus. Now that we've given ourselves what we need, let's think a bit about comfort. For people it'll be a luxury, if you like; but for things it's a necessity! It's no fun climbing a long ladder when you're carrying a heavy load!"

"Well then, Pencroff, we'll try to make you happy," said Cyrus Smith.

"But you haven't got an engine to work it."

"We'll make one."

"A steam engine?"

"No, a water engine."

And in fact a natural force was right there at the engineer's disposal to drive his machine, and he could put it to use without much trouble.

All he had to do was increase the flow of the small branch channel taken off the lake, which supplied water to the inside of Granite House. The opening left among the stones and grass at the upper end of the old spillway was made bigger, which produced a strong fall of water at the bottom of the passage, and the overflow from it drained away down the inner well. Beneath this fall the engineer installed a drum fitted with paddles. Outside, it was linked to a wheel wound with a strong cable that carried a large basket. In this way, by means of a long rope that hung down to the ground and could be used to engage or disengage the water engine, a person could ride up in the basket all the way to the door of Granite House.

It was on March 17 that the elevator worked for the first time, to everyone's satisfaction. From then on all loads — wood, coal, provisions, and the colonists themselves — were hoisted up by this very simple system. It replaced the primitive ladder, which nobody thought of missing. Top showed himself particularly delighted with the improvement. He did not have, and could not have, Master Jup's knack for climbing rungs, and many a time he had had to make the ascent to Granite House on Nab's back, or even on the orangutan's.

Around this time, too, Cyrus Smith tried making glass, and first he had to adapt the old pottery kiln for this new purpose. That presented some fairly serious difficulties. But after several failed attempts he finally managed to set up a glassworks, which Gédéon Spilett and Harbert, the engineer's natural assistants, did not leave for several days.

As for the substances that go into glass, they are simply sand, chalk and soda (either the carbonate or the sulfate). Now, the shore supplied the sand; the lime supplied the chalk; the sea plants supplied the soda; the pyrites supplied the sulfuric acid; and the ground supplied the coal to heat the kiln to the necessary temperature. So Cyrus Smith had everything he needed to begin.

The tool that was hardest to make was the glassblower's "pipe": an iron tube five or six feet long, used to gather up on one of its ends the material that is kept in a molten state. But Pencroff managed to make this pipe by rolling a long, thin strip of iron the way a gun barrel is rolled, and it was soon ready for work.

On March 28 the kiln was fired up hot. A hundred parts of sand, thirty-five of chalk and forty of sulfate of soda, mixed with two or three parts of powdered charcoal, made up the batch, which was put into crucibles of fireclay. When the high heat of the kiln had turned it liquid, or rather into a thick paste, Cyrus Smith "gathered" a certain amount of this paste on the end of the pipe. He turned it over and over on a metal plate set out beforehand, so as to give it the right shape for blowing. Then he handed the pipe to Harbert and told him to blow into the other end.

"Like blowing soap bubbles?" the boy asked.

"Exactly," answered the engineer.

And Harbert, puffing out his cheeks, blew so long and so well into the pipe, taking care to keep turning it all the while, that his breath swelled the glassy mass. More molten material was added to the first, and soon the result was a bubble a foot across. Then Cyrus Smith took the pipe back from Harbert and, swinging it to and fro like a pendulum, drew the soft bubble out until it took the shape of a cylinder with cone-shaped ends.

So the blowing had produced a glass cylinder closed off by two rounded caps, like halves of a ball. These were easily cut off with a sharp iron dipped in cold water. Then, by the same method, the cylinder was split along its length. After a second heating had made it soft again, it was spread out on a plate and flattened with a wooden roller.

The first pane was made, and all they had to do was repeat the operation fifty times to have fifty panes. So the windows of Granite House were soon fitted with sheets of glass — not very clear, perhaps, but transparent enough.

As for glassware, tumblers and bottles, that was child's play. Besides, they took them just as they came off the end of the pipe. Pencroff had asked for the privilege of "blowing" in his turn, and he loved it. But he blew so hard that his products took on the most comical shapes, and he admired every one of them.

During one of the excursions made at this time, a new tree was discovered whose products added still more to the colony's food supply.

Cyrus Smith and Harbert, out hunting, had gone one day into the Far West forests, on the left bank of the Mercy, and as always the boy was asking the engineer a thousand questions, which he answered gladly. But hunting is like every other occupation here below: when you don't bring the proper zeal to it, there are plenty of reasons why you won't succeed. Now Cyrus Smith was no hunter, and on top of that, Harbert was talking chemistry and physics that day. So a good many kangaroos, capybaras and agoutis passed well within range and still got away from the boy's gun. As a result, the day was already well along and the two hunters were in a fair way to having made the trip for nothing, when Harbert stopped short and gave a shout of joy.

"Oh, Mr. Cyrus! Do you see that tree?"

And he pointed to what was a shrub rather than a tree, for it had only a single stem, covered in scaly bark, which bore leaves streaked with little parallel veins.

"And what is this tree that looks like a little palm?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"It's a Cycas revoluta — there's a picture of it in our dictionary of natural history!"

"But I don't see any fruit on this shrub."

"No, Mr. Cyrus," answered Harbert, "but its trunk holds a flour that nature gives us ready-ground."

"So it's the bread tree?"

"Yes! The bread tree."

"Well, my boy," said the engineer, "that's a precious discovery while we wait for our wheat harvest. Let's get to work — and heaven grant you're not mistaken!"

Harbert was not mistaken. He broke open the stem of a cycad. It was made of a glandular tissue and held a quantity of floury pith, crossed by bundles of woody fiber, which were separated by rings of the same material arranged one inside another. Mixed with this starch was a slimy juice with an unpleasant taste, but it would be easy to drive it out by pressing. This cellular substance made a real flour of superior quality, extremely nourishing — so much so that the laws of Japan once forbade its export.

Cyrus Smith and Harbert studied the part of the Far West where these cycads grew, took landmarks, and went back to Granite House, where they announced their discovery.

The next day the colonists went out to harvest, and Pencroff, more and more enthusiastic about his island, said to the engineer:

"Mr. Cyrus, do you believe there are castaway islands?"

"What do you mean by that, Pencroff?"

"Why, I mean islands made specially so that people can be shipwrecked on them properly, where poor devils can always manage to get by!"

"That's possible," said the engineer with a smile.

"It's certain, sir," answered Pencroff, "and it's just as certain that Lincoln Island is one of them!"

They came back to Granite House with an ample harvest of cycad stems. The engineer built a press to squeeze out the slimy juice mixed with the starch, and he got a good quantity of flour, which Nab's hands turned into cakes and puddings. It was not yet real wheat bread, but they were almost there.

At this time, too, the onagga, the goats and the ewes at the corral gave all the milk the colony needed every day. So the cart — or rather a sort of light two-wheeled trap that had replaced it — made frequent trips to the corral. And when it was Pencroff's turn to make the rounds, he took Jup along and let him drive, which Jup did with his usual intelligence, cracking his whip.

So everything was thriving, at the corral as well as at Granite House, and truly the colonists, apart from being far from their homeland, had nothing to complain about. Indeed, they had grown so used to this life, so at home on this island, that they could not have left its welcoming soil without regret!

And yet, so deeply is love of country rooted in the human heart that if some ship had unexpectedly come in sight of the island, the colonists would have signaled to it, drawn it in, and gone!... Meanwhile they lived this happy life, and they feared rather than wished for anything that might come along to interrupt it.

But who can boast of ever having pinned fortune down, and of being safe from her reverses?

Be that as it may, this Lincoln Island, where the colonists had now been living for more than a year, was often the subject of their conversation, and one day a remark was made that would later lead to serious consequences.

It was April 1, a Sunday — Easter Day — which Cyrus Smith and his companions had kept holy with rest and prayer. The day had been beautiful, as a day in October might be in the northern hemisphere.

Toward evening, after dinner, they were all gathered on the veranda at the edge of Prospect Plateau, watching night rise over the horizon. Nab had served a few cups of the infusion of elderberry seeds that stood in for coffee. They were talking about the island and its isolated position in the Pacific when Gédéon Spilett happened to say:

"My dear Cyrus, since you've had that sextant we found in the chest, have you taken our island's position again?"

"No," answered the engineer.

"But it might be a good idea to do it, with an instrument that's better than the one you used."

"What for?" said Pencroff. "The island's fine where it is!"

"No doubt," Gédéon Spilett went on, "but it may be that the flaws in your instruments affected the accuracy of your observations, and since it's easy to check them..."

"You're right, my dear Spilett," answered the engineer, "and I ought to have made that check sooner — although if I did make some mistake, it shouldn't be more than five degrees in longitude or latitude."

"Ah! Who knows?" the reporter went on. "Who knows whether we aren't a lot closer to an inhabited land than we think?"

"We'll know tomorrow," answered Cyrus Smith, "and if I hadn't had so much work that left me no free time, we'd know already."

"Good!" said Pencroff. "Mr. Cyrus is too good an observer to have gone wrong, and unless it's moved, the island is right where he put it!"

"We'll see."

So the next day the engineer used the sextant to make the observations needed to check the coordinates he had already obtained, and here is the result of his work.

His first observation had given him this position for Lincoln Island:

    Longitude west: from 150° to 155°
    Latitude south: from 30° to 35°

The second gave, exactly:

    Longitude west: 150° 30′
    Latitude south: 34° 57′

So, for all the imperfection of his instruments, Cyrus Smith had worked with such skill that his error had not gone beyond five degrees.

"Now," said Gédéon Spilett, "since we have an atlas as well as a sextant, let's see, my dear Cyrus, exactly where Lincoln Island sits in the Pacific."

Harbert went to get the atlas, which, as we know, had been published in France, so that its place names were in French.

The map of the Pacific was spread out, and the engineer, compasses in hand, got ready to fix the island's position on it.

Suddenly the compasses stopped in his hand, and he said:

"But there's already an island in this part of the Pacific!"

"An island?" cried Pencroff.

"Ours, surely?" said Gédéon Spilett.

"No," Cyrus Smith went on. "This island lies at 153° longitude and 37° 11′ latitude — that is, two and a half degrees farther west and two degrees farther south than Lincoln Island."

"And what island is it?" asked Harbert.

"Tabor Island."

"Is it a big island?"

"No, it's an islet lost in the Pacific, which perhaps nobody has ever visited!"

"Well, we'll visit it," said Pencroff.

"We will?"

"Yes, Mr. Cyrus. We'll build a decked boat, and I'll take charge of sailing her. How far are we from this Tabor Island?"

"About a hundred and fifty miles to the northeast," answered Cyrus Smith.

"A hundred and fifty miles! What's that?" answered Pencroff. "In forty-eight hours, with a good wind, it's done!"

"But what for?" asked the reporter.

"You never know. We'll have to see!"

And on that answer it was decided that a boat would be built, so that she could put to sea around next October, when the fine season came back.
