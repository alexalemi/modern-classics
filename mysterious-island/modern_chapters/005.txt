Chapter 6: The Castaways' Inventory

_The castaways' inventory — Nothing — The burnt linen — An excursion into the forest — The flora of the evergreens — The jacamar gets away — Tracks of wild beasts — The couroucous — The grouse — A strange kind of fishing_

The inventory of everything these castaways of the air possessed, thrown up on a coast that seemed to be uninhabited, will not take long to draw up.

They had nothing, apart from the clothes they had been wearing when disaster struck. One must mention, it is true, a notebook and a watch that Gédéon Spilett had kept, no doubt by oversight. But there was not a weapon, not a tool, not even a pocketknife. The passengers in the basket had thrown everything overboard to lighten the balloon.

The imaginary heroes of Daniel Defoe or of Wyss, who wrote The Swiss Family Robinson, and real castaways like Selkirk and Raynal, wrecked on Juan Fernández or in the Auckland Islands, were never so utterly destitute. Either they drew plentiful supplies from their stranded ship — seed, livestock, tools, ammunition — or else some piece of wreckage washed up on the shore that let them meet the first needs of life. They were never, at the start, completely unarmed in the face of nature. But here there was not a single instrument of any kind, not a single utensil. From nothing, they would have to arrive at everything!

And if only Cyrus Smith had been with them, if only the engineer could have put his practical knowledge and his inventive mind to work on their situation, perhaps all hope would not have been lost! Alas, they could no longer count on ever seeing Cyrus Smith again. The castaways had nothing to rely on but themselves, and that Providence which never abandons those whose faith is sincere.

But first of all, should they settle on this stretch of coast without trying to find out what continent it belonged to, whether it was inhabited, or whether it was only the shore of a desert island?

That was an important question, and one they needed to answer as soon as possible. On the answer would depend everything they did next. All the same, following Pencroff's advice, it seemed wise to wait a few days before setting out to explore. They had, in fact, to lay in food and find something more strengthening to eat than eggs and shellfish alone. The explorers might have to endure long, hard days with no shelter to rest their heads, and before anything else they had to get their strength back.

For the time being the Chimneys offered a good enough refuge. The fire was lit, and it would be easy to keep embers going. For the moment there was no shortage of shellfish and eggs among the rocks and on the beach. They would surely find some way to kill a few of the pigeons that flew by the hundreds along the crest of the plateau, even if it had to be with sticks or stones. Perhaps the trees of the nearby forest would give them fruit they could eat? And fresh water was right there. So it was agreed that they would stay at the Chimneys for a few days to get ready for an expedition, either along the coast or into the interior.

This plan suited Nab particularly well. As stubborn in his ideas as in his hunches, he was in no hurry to leave this part of the coast, the scene of the catastrophe. He did not believe — he refused to believe — that Cyrus Smith was lost. No, it did not seem possible to him that such a man could have come to so commonplace an end, carried off by a wave and drowned a few hundred paces from shore! As long as the sea had not thrown up the engineer's body, as long as he, Nab, had not seen his master's corpse with his own eyes and touched it with his own hands, he would not believe he was dead! And the idea took root deeper than ever in his stubborn heart. An illusion, perhaps, but an illusion that deserved respect, and the sailor had no wish to destroy it. For Pencroff there was no hope left: the engineer had truly perished in the waves. But with Nab there was no arguing. He was like the dog that cannot leave the spot where its master fell, and his grief was so great that he probably would not outlive him.

That morning, March 26, at first light, Nab had set off north along the coast again, back to the place where the sea had no doubt closed over the unfortunate Smith.

Breakfast that day consisted entirely of pigeon eggs and lithodomes. Harbert had found salt left by evaporation in the hollows of the rocks, and that mineral came in very handy.

When the meal was over, Pencroff asked the reporter whether he wanted to come with them into the forest, where he and Harbert were going to try their luck at hunting. But on second thought, someone had to stay behind to keep the fire going, and also in the very unlikely case that Nab needed help. So the reporter stayed.

"Off to the hunt, Harbert," said the sailor. "We'll find our ammunition along the way, and we'll cut our gun in the forest."

But just as they were leaving, Harbert pointed out that since they had no tinder, it might be wise to replace it with something else.

"With what?" asked Pencroff.

"Burnt linen," the boy answered. "In a pinch, it can do the job of tinder."

The sailor thought this very sensible advice. Its only drawback was that it meant sacrificing a piece of handkerchief. Still, it was worth it, and part of Pencroff's big checked handkerchief was soon reduced to a half-charred rag. This flammable stuff was stored in the central chamber, at the back of a little hollow in the rock, sheltered from all wind and damp.

It was now nine o'clock in the morning. The weather looked threatening, and the breeze was blowing from the southeast. Harbert and Pencroff rounded the corner of the Chimneys, not without a glance at the smoke curling up around a point of rock. Then they started up the left bank of the river.

When they reached the forest, Pencroff broke two stout branches off the first tree and turned them into clubs, and Harbert ground their tips to a point on a rock. Oh, what wouldn't he have given for a knife! Then the two hunters pushed on through the tall grass, following the bank. From the bend where the river turned toward the southwest, it narrowed little by little, and its banks formed a deep, steep-sided channel roofed over by the arching trees on either side. To keep from getting lost, Pencroff decided to follow the stream, which would always lead him back to where he had started. But the bank was not without its obstacles: here, trees whose supple branches bent down to the level of the current; there, creepers or thorns that had to be smashed through with a stick. Often Harbert would slip between the broken stumps as nimbly as a young cat and vanish into the undergrowth. But Pencroff would call him back at once and beg him not to wander off.

Meanwhile the sailor was paying close attention to the lay of the land and what it was like. On this left bank the ground was flat and rose very gradually toward the interior. In places it was damp, and then it took on a marshy look. You could sense a whole network of little trickles underneath, which must have been draining toward the river through some crack in the rock below. In places, too, a brook ran through the undergrowth, and it was easy to wade across. The opposite bank looked more rugged, and the valley, with the river running along its lowest line, showed its shape more clearly on that side. The hillside there, covered with trees rising in tiers, formed a screen that blocked the view. Walking on that right bank would have been hard, for the slopes dropped away steeply, and the trees, bent out over the water, held on only by the strength of their roots.

Needless to say, this forest, like the coast they had already covered, bore no trace of human beings. Pencroff noticed only the tracks of four-footed animals, fresh trails left by creatures whose species he could not make out. Quite certainly — and Harbert thought so too — some of them had been left by formidable wild beasts that they would no doubt have to reckon with. But nowhere was there the mark of an ax on a tree trunk, or the remains of a dead fire, or a human footprint. And perhaps they ought to be glad of that, for on this land in the middle of the Pacific, the presence of man might have been more to be feared than to be wished for.

Harbert and Pencroff, hardly talking, since the going was very rough, made only slow progress, and after an hour's walking they had covered barely a mile. So far the hunting had not paid off. A few birds were singing and fluttering among the branches, but they were very shy, as if man had instinctively taught them a well-founded fear. Among other birds, in a marshy part of the forest, Harbert pointed out one with a long, sharp beak that was built very much like a kingfisher. It differed from a kingfisher, though, in its rather coarse plumage, which had a metallic sheen.

"That must be a jacamar," said Harbert, trying to creep within good range of it.

"This would be a fine chance to taste jacamar," the sailor answered, "if that bird were in the mood to be roasted!"

At that moment a stone, thrown skillfully and hard by the boy, struck the bird where the wing joins the body. But the blow was not enough, for the jacamar ran off as fast as its legs would carry it and disappeared in an instant.

"What a clumsy fool I am!" cried Harbert.

"No, no, my boy!" the sailor answered. "That was a good throw, and plenty of people would have missed the bird altogether. Come on, don't be upset! We'll catch him another day!"

The exploration went on. The farther the hunters went, the more widely spaced and the more magnificent the trees became, but not one bore fruit they could eat. Pencroff looked in vain for some of those precious palm trees that serve so many purposes in daily life, and which have been found as far as the fortieth parallel in the northern hemisphere, but only as far as the thirty-fifth in the southern. This forest, however, was made up entirely of conifers: deodars, which Harbert had already recognized, Douglas firs like the ones that grow on the northwest coast of America, and splendid firs a hundred and fifty feet tall.

Just then a flock of small birds with pretty plumage and long, shimmering tails scattered among the branches, shedding their loosely attached feathers, which covered the ground with a fine down. Harbert picked up a few of the feathers and, after examining them, said:

"These are couroucous."

"I'd rather have a guinea fowl or a heath cock," answered Pencroff, "but anyway, are they good to eat?"

"They're good to eat, and their meat is actually very delicate," Harbert went on. "Besides, if I'm not mistaken, they're easy to get close to and knock down with a stick."

Slipping through the grass, the sailor and the boy reached the foot of a tree whose low branches were covered with the little birds. The couroucous were waiting there for the insects they feed on to fly past. You could see their feathered feet gripping tightly the middling shoots they were perched on.

The hunters then stood up and, swinging their sticks like scythes, mowed down whole rows of couroucous, which never thought of flying away and let themselves be knocked down like fools. A hundred of them already lay on the ground before the rest decided to escape.

"Good," said Pencroff. "Here's game made to measure for hunters like us! You could catch it by hand!"

The sailor strung the couroucous on a flexible switch, the way larks are strung for market, and the exploration went on. They could see that the stream curved slightly, so as to form a hook toward the south. But this detour probably did not go on for long, for the river must have had its source in the mountain and been fed by the melting of the snow that covered the sides of the central cone.

The particular purpose of this excursion, as we know, was to bring back to the residents of the Chimneys as much game as possible. It could not be said that this aim had been achieved so far. So the sailor kept up his search energetically, and grumbled whenever some animal, which he did not even have time to identify, bolted off through the tall grass. If only he had had the dog Top! But Top had disappeared at the same time as his master, and had probably perished with him!

Toward three o'clock in the afternoon, new flocks of birds were glimpsed through certain trees, pecking at their aromatic berries — junipers among them. Suddenly a real trumpet call rang through the forest. These strange, resounding fanfares came from the game birds known in the United States as grouse. Soon a few pairs came into view, with plumage mottled tawny and brown, and brown tails. Harbert could tell the males by the two pointed wings of feathers that stood up on their necks. Pencroff decided that it was absolutely essential to get hold of one of these birds, which were as big as a hen and whose meat is as good as a hazel grouse's. But it was difficult, for they would not let anyone near them. After several failed attempts, which did nothing but frighten the grouse, the sailor said to the boy:

"That settles it. Since we can't kill them on the wing, we'll have to try catching them with a line."

"Like a carp?" cried Harbert, very surprised at the suggestion.

"Like a carp," the sailor answered seriously.

In the grass Pencroff had found half a dozen grouse nests, each holding two or three eggs. He took great care not to touch these nests, since their owners were sure to come back to them. It was around them that he planned to set his lines — not snares, but real fishing lines with hooks. He led Harbert some distance from the nests, and there he prepared his peculiar tackle with all the care a disciple of Izaak Walton, the great writer on angling, would have brought to it. Harbert followed the work with an interest that is easy to understand, though he doubted it would succeed. The lines were made of thin creepers tied end to end, fifteen to twenty feet long. Big, very strong thorns with hooked points, taken from a bush of dwarf acacias, were tied to the ends of the creepers to serve as hooks. As for bait, fat red worms crawling on the ground did the job.

That done, Pencroff slipped through the grass, keeping cleverly out of sight, and laid the hooked ends of his lines near the grouse nests. Then he came back, took up the other ends, and hid with Harbert behind a big tree. Then both of them waited patiently. Harbert, it must be said, did not have much faith in the inventive Pencroff's success.

A good half hour went by, but, just as the sailor had predicted, several pairs of grouse came back to their nests. They hopped about, pecking at the ground, without the slightest suspicion that the hunters were there. The hunters, besides, had taken care to stay downwind of the birds.

There is no denying that at this moment the boy was intensely interested. He was holding his breath, and Pencroff, eyes wide, mouth open, lips pushed out as if he were about to taste a bite of grouse, was barely breathing at all.

Meanwhile the birds were strolling among the hooks without paying them much attention. Then Pencroff gave little tugs that jiggled the bait, as though the worms were still alive.

Without a doubt, the sailor felt a far stronger thrill at that moment than any angler does, since the angler cannot see his prey coming toward him through the water.

The tugs soon caught the birds' attention, and they began pecking at the hooks. Three grouse, very greedy no doubt, swallowed bait and hook together. Suddenly, with a sharp jerk, Pencroff set his hooks, and the beating of wings told him the birds were caught.

"Hurrah!" he shouted, rushing at the game, and he had it in hand in an instant.

Harbert had clapped his hands. It was the first time he had ever seen birds caught with a line. But the sailor, very modestly, assured him that this was not his first try, and that anyway the credit for the invention was not his.

"And in any case," he added, "in the situation we're in, we'd better expect to see plenty of other things like it!"

The grouse were tied together by the feet, and Pencroff, happy not to be going back empty-handed and seeing that the daylight was beginning to fade, thought it best to head for home.

The way back was plainly marked by the river; they had only to follow it downstream. And at about six o'clock, rather tired from their excursion, Harbert and Pencroff walked back into the Chimneys.
