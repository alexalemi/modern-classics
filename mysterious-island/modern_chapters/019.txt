Chapter 20: The Rainy Season

_The rainy season — The question of clothing — A seal hunt — Making candles — Work inside Granite House — The two little bridges — Back from a visit to the oyster bed — What Harbert finds in his pocket_

Winter truly began with the month of June, which in this hemisphere corresponds to December in the northern one. It opened with showers and squalls that followed one another without a break. The residents of Granite House could now appreciate the advantages of a home that no bad weather could touch. The shelter of the Chimneys would have been far too poor a refuge against the hardships of a winter, and there was reason to fear that the high tides, driven by winds from the open sea, might break into it again. Cyrus Smith even took a few precautions against that possibility, to protect as well as he could the forge and the furnaces that were set up there.

All through June, the time went into all sorts of work, which left room for both hunting and fishing, and the pantry's stores could be kept amply supplied. As soon as he had the leisure, Pencroff meant to set up traps, from which he expected great things. He had already made snares out of woody fibers, and not a day went by without the warren supplying its share of rodents. Nab spent nearly all his time salting or smoking meat, which kept him in excellent preserved provisions.

The question of clothing now came up for very serious discussion. The colonists had no clothes but the ones they had been wearing when the balloon threw them onto the island. Those clothes were warm and sturdy. They had taken the greatest care of them, and of their underclothes too, and they kept everything perfectly clean. But all of it would soon need replacing. Besides, if the winter turned out to be harsh, the colonists would suffer badly from the cold.

On this point, Cyrus Smith's ingenuity had come up short. He had had to see to the most urgent things first, to build the home and make sure of the food supply, and the cold might catch him before the question of clothing was solved. So they would just have to resign themselves to getting through this first winter without complaining too much. When the fine season came, they would go hunting in earnest for the mouflons whose presence had been noticed during the exploration of Mount Franklin, and once the wool was gathered, the engineer would surely know how to make warm, sturdy cloth out of it... How? He would think about that.

"Well, we'll just have to toast our shins by the fire in Granite House!" said Pencroff. "There's fuel everywhere, and no reason to go easy on it."

"Besides," said Gédéon Spilett, "Lincoln Island doesn't lie in a very high latitude, and the winters here probably aren't severe. Didn't you tell us, Cyrus, that this thirty-fifth parallel corresponds to the latitude of Spain in the other hemisphere?"

"Certainly," answered the engineer, "but some winters are very cold in Spain! Snow and ice, nothing missing, and Lincoln Island may be tested just as hard. Still, this is an island, and being an island, I hope its temperature will be milder."

"Why is that, Mr. Cyrus?" asked Harbert.

"Because the sea, my boy, can be thought of as an immense reservoir in which the heat of summer is stored up. When winter comes, the sea gives that heat back, and so the regions near the oceans enjoy a moderate average temperature — not as hot in summer, but not as cold in winter."

"We'll see," said Pencroff. "Personally, I don't intend to worry about how cold it will or won't be. What's certain is that the days are already short and the evenings long. Suppose we took up the question of lighting?"

"Nothing could be easier," said Cyrus Smith.

"To take up?" asked the sailor.

"To solve."

"And when do we start?"

"Tomorrow, with a seal hunt."

"To make tallow candles?"

"Tallow? For shame, Pencroff! Proper candles — stearin candles."

That was indeed the engineer's plan, and it was a workable one, since he had lime and sulfuric acid, and the amphibians on the islet would furnish the fat he needed to make them.

It was June 4. It was Pentecost Sunday, and everyone agreed to keep the holiday. All work stopped, and prayers rose toward heaven. But these prayers were now prayers of thanks. The colonists of Lincoln Island were no longer the wretched castaways who had been flung onto the islet. They no longer asked; they gave thanks.

The next day, June 5, in rather uncertain weather, they set out for the islet. Once again they had to wait for low tide to wade across the channel, and on that subject it was agreed that they would build a boat of some sort, however rough, which would make getting back and forth easier and would also let them travel up the Mercy during the great exploration of the island's southwest, which had been put off until the first fine days.

The seals were plentiful, and the hunters, armed with their iron-tipped spears, easily killed half a dozen. Nab and Pencroff skinned them and carried back to Granite House only their fat and their skins, the skins being meant for making sturdy shoes.

The result of this hunt was as follows: about three hundred pounds of fat, all of which was to go into making candles.

The process was extremely simple, and if it did not give perfect results, the candles were at least usable. Even if Cyrus Smith had had nothing but sulfuric acid at his disposal, he could have heated that acid with the neutral fat — in this case the seal fat — and separated out the glycerin. Then, from the new compound, he could easily have separated the olein, the margarin, and the stearin by using boiling water. But to keep the operation simple, he chose to saponify the fat with lime. In this way he obtained a lime soap, which sulfuric acid easily broke down: the acid made the lime settle out as calcium sulfate and set the fatty acids free.

Of these three acids — oleic, margaric, and stearic — the first, being liquid, was squeezed out under enough pressure. The other two together made up the very substance that would be molded into candles.

The whole operation took no more than twenty-four hours. After several trials, the wicks were made of plant fibers, and when they were dipped in the melted substance, they produced genuine stearin candles, molded by hand, which lacked nothing but bleaching and polishing. They did not, of course, have the advantage of wicks soaked in boric acid, which turn to glass little by little as they burn and so are consumed completely. But Cyrus Smith made a fine pair of snuffers, and these candles were greatly appreciated during the long evenings at Granite House.

All that month there was no shortage of work inside the new home. The joiners had plenty to do. The tools, which were very rough, were improved. More were added to them, too.

Among other things, scissors were made, and the colonists could at last cut their hair, and if not shave their beards, at least trim them as they pleased. Harbert had none, and Nab hardly any, but their companions were bristling with enough of it to justify making the scissors.

Making a handsaw — the kind called a panel saw — cost endless trouble, but in the end they had a tool that, worked hard enough, could cut through the fibers of wood. So they made tables, seats, and cupboards to furnish the main rooms, and bed frames whose only bedding was mattresses of eelgrass. The kitchen, with its shelves holding the earthenware utensils, its brick stove, and its stone sink for the washing up, looked very fine, and Nab went about his business there with great solemnity, as if he were working in a chemist's laboratory.

But the joiners soon had to give way to the carpenters. The new spillway, blasted open with explosives, made it necessary to build two little bridges, one on Prospect Plateau and the other down on the beach itself. Now, in fact, both the plateau and the beach were cut straight across by a stream that had to be crossed by anyone who wanted to reach the north of the island. To avoid it, the colonists would have had to make a long detour, going west until they were beyond the springs of Red Creek. The simplest thing, then, was to put two little bridges across, one on the plateau and one on the beach, each twenty to twenty-five feet long, whose entire framework was just a few tree trunks squared off with the ax. It took only a few days. Once the bridges were in place, Nab and Pencroff used them to go as far as the oyster bed that had been found off the dunes. They pulled along with them a sort of rough cart, which replaced the old hurdle, since that had really been too awkward, and they brought back a few thousand oysters. These quickly took to their new home among the rocks at the mouth of the Mercy, which made so many natural oyster pens. The oysters were of excellent quality, and the colonists ate them almost every day.

As you can see, Lincoln Island, although its inhabitants had explored only a very small part of it, already supplied almost all their needs. And it was likely that, searched down to its most hidden corners — all across the wooded country stretching from the Mercy to Reptile End — it would pour out new treasures.

Only one hardship still weighed on the colonists of Lincoln Island. They had no lack of meat, the nitrogen-rich food, nor of the vegetable foods that ought to balance it. The woody roots of the dragon trees, left to ferment, gave them a sour drink, a sort of beer far better than plain water. They had even made sugar, without sugarcane or beets, by collecting the sap that flows from "Acer saccharinum," a kind of maple, of the family Aceraceae, that thrives in every temperate zone, and of which the island had a great many. They made a very pleasant tea from the bee balm brought back from the warren. And they had plenty of salt, the only mineral that is part of the human diet... But they had no bread.

Perhaps, later on, the colonists could replace that food with some substitute, sago-palm flour or the starch of the breadfruit tree. And it was possible, in fact, that the southern forests might count some of those precious trees among their species, but so far none had been found.

Yet in this matter Providence was to come straight to the colonists' aid — in an infinitesimal amount, it is true, but still: Cyrus Smith, with all his intelligence and all his ingenuity, could never have produced what Harbert found one day, by the purest chance, in the lining of his jacket, which he was busy mending.

That day — it was pouring rain — the colonists were gathered in the great hall of Granite House when the boy suddenly cried out:

"Look, Mr. Cyrus! A grain of wheat!"

And he showed his companions a grain, one single grain, which had slipped from his torn pocket into the lining of his jacket.

The grain was there because Harbert, back in Richmond, had been in the habit of feeding some wood pigeons that Pencroff had given him.

"A grain of wheat?" the engineer said quickly.

"Yes, Mr. Cyrus, but just one, only one!"

"Well, my boy," cried Pencroff with a smile, "that gets us a long way, I must say! What could we possibly do with one grain of wheat?"

"We'll make bread out of it," answered Cyrus Smith.

"Bread, cakes, pies!" retorted the sailor. "Come on! The bread this grain of wheat gives us won't choke us anytime soon!"

Harbert, attaching very little importance to his discovery, was about to throw the grain away, but Cyrus Smith took it, examined it, saw that it was in good condition, and, looking the sailor straight in the eye, asked him calmly:

"Pencroff, do you know how many ears a grain of wheat can produce?"

"One, I suppose!" answered the sailor, surprised by the question.

"Ten, Pencroff. And do you know how many grains an ear carries?"

"No, I can't say I do."

"Eighty on average," said Cyrus Smith. "So if we plant this grain, at the first harvest we'll reap eight hundred grains. Those will produce six hundred and forty thousand at the second harvest, five hundred and twelve million at the third, and at the fourth more than four hundred billion grains. That's the proportion."

Cyrus Smith's companions listened without answering. The figures stunned them. And yet they were exact.

"Yes, my friends," the engineer went on. "Such are the arithmetical progressions of fruitful nature. And even so, what is this multiplication of the grain of wheat, whose ear carries only eight hundred grains, compared with the poppy plants that bear thirty-two thousand seeds, or the tobacco plants that produce three hundred and sixty thousand? Within a few years, if it weren't for the many causes of destruction that hold their fertility in check, those plants would overrun the whole earth."

But the engineer had not finished his little cross-examination.

"And now, Pencroff," he went on, "do you know how many bushels four hundred billion grains make?"

"No," answered the sailor, "but what I do know is that I'm nothing but a dunce!"

"Well, it would come to more than three million, at a hundred and thirty thousand grains to the bushel, Pencroff."

"Three million!" cried Pencroff.

"Three million."

"In four years?"

"In four years," answered Cyrus Smith, "and even in two, if, as I hope, we can get two harvests a year in this latitude."

To that, as was his habit, Pencroff could think of no reply but a tremendous hurrah.

"So, Harbert," the engineer added, "you've made a discovery of the greatest importance to us. Everything, my friends, everything can be of use to us in the situation we're in. Please don't forget it."

"No, Mr. Cyrus, no, we won't forget it," answered Pencroff. "And if I ever find one of those tobacco seeds that multiply by three hundred and sixty thousand, I promise you I won't throw it to the wind! And now, do you know what we have left to do?"

"We have to plant this grain," answered Harbert.

"Yes," added Gédéon Spilett, "and with all the respect it deserves, because it carries our future harvests inside it."

"If only it grows!" cried the sailor.

"It will grow," answered Cyrus Smith.

It was June 20. So the time was right for sowing that single, precious grain of wheat. At first there was talk of planting it in a pot. But on reflection they decided to trust nature more boldly and give it to the earth. That was done the very same day, and there is no need to add that every precaution was taken to make the operation succeed.

The weather having cleared a little, the colonists climbed to the heights above Granite House. There, on the plateau, they chose a spot well sheltered from the wind, where the noonday sun would pour down all its warmth. The spot was cleared, carefully weeded, and even dug over to drive out any insects or worms. They laid down a bed of good soil improved with a little lime. They put a fence around it. Then the grain was pressed down into the moist bed.

Didn't it seem as if these colonists were laying the cornerstone of a building? It reminded Pencroff of the day he had struck his one and only match, and of all the care he had taken over that operation. But this time the matter was more serious. For the castaways would always have managed to get fire, by one means or another, but no human power could ever give them back this grain of wheat if, by bad luck, it should die!
