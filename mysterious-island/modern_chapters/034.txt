Chapter 13: Tabor Island

_Departure decided — Hypotheses — Preparations — The three passengers — The first night — The second night — Tabor Island — Searching the beach — Searching the woods — Nobody — Animals — Plants — A dwelling — Deserted_

"A castaway!" cried Pencroff. "Marooned a few hundred miles from us, on this Tabor Island! Ah, Mr. Cyrus, you won't stand in the way of my voyage now!"

"No, Pencroff," answered Cyrus Smith, "and you'll leave as soon as possible."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow."

The engineer was holding the paper he had pulled out of the bottle. He studied it for a few moments, then spoke again.

"From this document, my friends," he said, "and from the very way it's written, we can conclude two things right away. First, the castaway on Tabor Island is a man with a fairly advanced knowledge of seamanship, since he gives the island's latitude and longitude, and they agree with the ones we found, to within a single minute. Second, he's English or American, since the document is written in English."

"That's perfectly logical," answered Gédéon Spilett, "and the presence of this castaway explains how the chest came to wash up on our shores. There was a shipwreck, because there's a castaway. As for him, whoever he is, it's lucky for him that Pencroff had the idea of building this boat and trying her out today of all days. One day later, and this bottle might have smashed on the reefs."

"Yes," said Harbert, "it's a lucky chance that the Bonadventure passed that way just when the bottle was still floating!"

"And that doesn't strike you as odd?" Cyrus Smith asked Pencroff.

"It strikes me as lucky, that's all," answered the sailor. "Do you see anything extraordinary in it, Mr. Cyrus? That bottle had to end up somewhere, and why not here as well as anywhere else?"

"Perhaps you're right, Pencroff," answered the engineer, "and yet..."

"But," Harbert pointed out, "nothing proves that the bottle has been floating on the sea for a long time, does it?"

"Nothing," answered Gédéon Spilett, "and in fact the document looks as if it was written recently. What do you think, Cyrus?"

"That's hard to check, and anyway, we'll find out!" answered Cyrus Smith.

While they were talking, Pencroff had not been idle. He had put the boat about, and the Bonadventure, running on a broad reach with every sail drawing, was racing toward Claw Cape. Everyone was thinking about the castaway on Tabor Island. Was there still time to save him? It was a great event in the life of the colonists! They themselves were only castaways, but it was to be feared that another man had not been as fortunate as they had, and their duty was to hurry to the help of misfortune.

They rounded Claw Cape, and at about four o'clock the Bonadventure dropped anchor at the mouth of the Mercy.

That same evening the details of the new expedition were settled. It seemed best that Pencroff and Harbert, who both knew how to handle a boat, should make the voyage by themselves. If they left the next day, October 11, they could arrive sometime on the 13th, because with the wind that was blowing it would take no more than forty-eight hours to make the crossing of a hundred and fifty miles. Add one day on the island and three or four days to come back, and they could count on being home on Lincoln Island by the 17th. The weather was fine, the barometer was rising steadily, without any jumps, and the wind seemed well settled. Every chance, then, was on the side of these good men, whom a duty to their fellow man was about to carry far from their island.

So it had been agreed that Cyrus Smith, Nab and Gédéon Spilett would stay at Granite House. But a protest was raised. Gédéon Spilett, who had by no means forgotten his trade as a reporter for the New York Herald, declared that he would swim there rather than miss a chance like this, and so he was allowed to join the voyage.

The evening was spent carrying aboard the Bonadventure some bedding, utensils, weapons, ammunition, a compass, and provisions for about a week. The loading was quickly done, and the colonists went back up to Granite House.

The next morning at five o'clock they said their goodbyes, not without some emotion on both sides. Pencroff spread his sails to the wind and headed for Claw Cape, which he had to round before setting a direct course to the southwest.

The Bonadventure was already a quarter of a mile from the coast when her passengers saw two men on the heights of Granite House waving goodbye to them. It was Cyrus Smith and Nab.

"Our friends!" cried Gédéon Spilett. "This is our first separation in fifteen months!..."

Pencroff, the reporter and Harbert waved one last goodbye, and Granite House soon disappeared behind the high rocks of the cape.

For the first hours of the day the Bonadventure stayed in constant sight of the southern coast of Lincoln Island, which soon looked like nothing more than a green basket with Mount Franklin rising out of it. Distance had shrunk the heights, and from out here the island hardly looked like a place to tempt a ship in to land.

They passed Reptile End at about one o'clock, but ten miles out to sea. At that distance it was no longer possible to make out anything of the western coast, which stretched as far as the shoulders of Mount Franklin, and three hours later the whole of Lincoln Island had sunk below the horizon.

The Bonadventure behaved perfectly. She rose easily to the waves and made rapid headway. Pencroff had rigged his gaff topsail, and with every stitch of canvas set he held a dead straight course, checked by the compass.

From time to time Harbert took a turn at the helm, and the boy's hand was so steady that the sailor could not fault him for a single swerve off course.

Gédéon Spilett chatted with one and then the other, and lent a hand with the sails when needed. Captain Pencroff was entirely satisfied with his crew, and talked of nothing less than rewarding them with "a tot of wine every watch"!

In the evening the crescent moon, which would not reach its first quarter until the 16th, showed for a while in the twilight and soon went out. The night was dark but full of stars, and the next day promised to be fine again.

As a precaution, Pencroff took in the gaff topsail. He did not want to risk being caught by some sudden gust with canvas set at the masthead. It was perhaps too much caution for so calm a night, but Pencroff was a prudent seaman, and no one could have blamed him for it.

The reporter slept for part of the night. Pencroff and Harbert relieved each other at the helm every two hours. The sailor trusted Harbert as he trusted himself, and the boy's coolness and good sense justified that trust. Pencroff gave him the course the way a captain gives it to his helmsman, and Harbert did not let the Bonadventure stray from it by a hair.

The night passed well, and the day of October 12 went by under the same conditions. The southwesterly course was kept strictly all that day, and unless the Bonadventure was being carried along by some unknown current, she ought to make landfall right on Tabor Island.

As for the sea the boat was crossing, it was completely empty. Now and then some large bird, an albatross or a frigate bird, passed within gunshot, and Gédéon Spilett wondered whether it was to one of those powerful fliers that he had entrusted his last dispatch to the New York Herald. These birds were the only living things that seemed to visit this stretch of ocean between Tabor Island and Lincoln Island.

"And yet," Harbert remarked, "this is the time of year when the whalers usually head for the southern part of the Pacific. Honestly, I don't believe there's a lonelier sea than this one!"

"It's not as empty as all that!" answered Pencroff.

"What do you mean?" asked the reporter.

"Well, we're on it, aren't we! Do you take our boat for a piece of driftwood and the three of us for porpoises?"

And Pencroff laughed at his own joke.

By evening, going by dead reckoning, they could assume that the Bonadventure had covered a hundred and twenty miles since leaving Lincoln Island — that is, in thirty-six hours, which gave a speed of three and a third miles an hour. The breeze was light and dying down. Still, they could hope that at daybreak the next day, if the reckoning was right and the course had been true, they would sight Tabor Island.

So neither Gédéon Spilett, nor Harbert, nor Pencroff slept that night of October 12 to 13. Waiting for the morning, they could not help feeling deeply stirred. There were so many uncertainties in the venture they had undertaken! Were they near Tabor Island? Was the island still inhabited by the castaway they were coming to help? Who was this man? Wouldn't his arrival bring some trouble into their little colony, so united until now? And besides, would he agree to trade one prison for another? All these questions, which would surely be answered the next day, kept them awake, and at the first glimmer of dawn they turned their eyes to every point of the western horizon in turn.

"Land!" Pencroff shouted at about six o'clock in the morning.

And since it was unthinkable that Pencroff could be mistaken, it was obvious that land was there.

Imagine the joy of the Bonadventure's little crew! In a few hours they would be on the island's shore!

Tabor Island, a sort of low coast barely rising out of the waves, was no more than fifteen miles away. The Bonadventure's bow, which was pointing a little to the south of the island, was turned straight toward it, and as the sun climbed in the east, a few summits stood out here and there.

"It's only an islet, much less important than Lincoln Island," Harbert remarked, "and it was probably raised up from the sea floor, like ours."

At eleven in the morning the Bonadventure was only two miles off, and Pencroff, looking for a channel to bring her in, was moving forward only with extreme caution over these unknown waters.

By now they could take in the whole islet at a glance. Clumps of green gum trees stood out on it, along with a few other large trees of the same kinds that grew on Lincoln Island. But — a rather astonishing thing — not a wisp of smoke rose to show that the islet was inhabited, and not a signal could be seen anywhere along the shore!

And yet the document had been definite: there was a castaway, and that castaway should have been on the lookout!

Meanwhile the Bonadventure was threading her way through the rather tricky channels that the reefs left between them, and Pencroff watched every twist and turn of them with the closest attention. He had put Harbert at the helm and stationed himself in the bow, studying the water, ready to lower the sail, whose halyard he held in his hand. Gédéon Spilett swept the whole shoreline with the spyglass without seeing a thing.

At last, at about noon, the Bonadventure ran her stem up onto a sandy beach. The anchor was dropped, the sails lowered, and the little boat's crew went ashore.

And there was no doubt that this really was Tabor Island, since, according to the most recent charts, there was no other island in that part of the Pacific between New Zealand and the American coast.

The boat was moored securely, so that the ebbing tide could not carry her off. Then Pencroff and his two companions, well armed, went up the beach to reach a kind of cone, two hundred and fifty to three hundred feet high, that rose half a mile away.

"From the top of that hill," said Gédéon Spilett, "we should be able to get a general idea of the islet, and that will make our search easier."

"It's doing here," answered Harbert, "just what Mr. Cyrus did first thing on Lincoln Island, when he climbed Mount Franklin."

"Exactly," answered the reporter, "and it's the best way to go about it!"

Talking as they went, the explorers moved forward along the edge of a meadow that ran right up to the foot of the cone. Flocks of rock pigeons and terns, like the ones on Lincoln Island, flew up in front of them. In the woods that bordered the meadow on the left they heard brushwood rustling and glimpsed grass stirring, signs of very shy animals. But so far nothing showed that the islet was inhabited.

When they reached the foot of the cone, Pencroff, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett climbed it in a few minutes and looked out to every point of the horizon.

They were indeed on an islet. It measured no more than six miles around, and its outline, with few capes or headlands jutting out and few coves or inlets cutting in, made a long oval. All around, the sea stretched to the edge of the sky, with nothing else at all. There was not a speck of land, not a sail in sight!

The islet was wooded all over, and it had none of the variety of Lincoln Island, which was barren and wild in one part and fertile and rich in the other. Here there was one even mass of green, with two or three low hills rising above it. A stream crossed the oval at a slant, flowing through a broad meadow and emptying into the sea on the western coast through a narrow mouth.

"It's a small estate," said Harbert.

"Yes," answered Pencroff, "it would have been a bit cramped for us!"

"And on top of that," answered the reporter, "it seems to be uninhabited."

"It does," answered Harbert. "Nothing here gives any sign of human beings."

"Let's go down," said Pencroff, "and look."

The sailor and his two companions went back to the shore, to the spot where they had left the Bonadventure. They had decided to go all the way around the islet on foot before venturing into the interior, so that not a single spot would escape their search.

The beach was easy to follow, and only in a few places was it cut by big rocks, which they could easily get around. The explorers headed south, scaring up large flocks of water birds and herds of seals, which threw themselves into the sea the moment they caught sight of the men, however far off.

"Those animals," the reporter pointed out, "aren't seeing human beings for the first time. They're afraid of them, so they know them."

An hour after setting out, the three of them had reached the southern tip of the islet, which ended in a sharp cape, and they turned north along the western coast. It was made of sand and rock as well, with thick woods behind.

Nowhere was there any trace of a dwelling, nowhere the print of a human foot, along the whole edge of the islet, which they had walked completely around after four hours.

It was extraordinary, to say the least, and they were bound to believe that Tabor Island was not inhabited, or was no longer. Perhaps, after all, the document was already several months or several years old, and in that case it was possible that the castaway had been taken home — or that he had died of hardship.

Pencroff, Gédéon Spilett and Harbert, trying out theories that were more or less likely, ate a quick dinner on board the Bonadventure, so that they could set off again and keep exploring until nightfall.

And so they did. At five in the afternoon they ventured into the woods.

Many animals fled at their approach — mainly, one could even say only, goats and pigs, which, as was easy to see, belonged to European breeds. No doubt some whaler had put them ashore on the island, where they had multiplied rapidly. Harbert promised himself that he would catch a pair or two alive to take back to Lincoln Island.

So there was no longer any doubt that men had visited this islet at some time or other. That became even clearer when, through the forest, they found trodden paths, tree trunks felled with an ax, and everywhere the mark of human work. But these trees, which were rotting away, had been cut down many years before. The ax cuts had grown a velvet of moss, and the grass grew long and thick across the paths, so that they were hard to make out.

"But," Gédéon Spilett pointed out, "that proves not only that men landed on this islet but that they lived on it for some time. Now, who were these men? How many of them were there? How many are left?"

"The document," said Harbert, "only mentions one castaway."

"Well, if he's still on the island," answered Pencroff, "there's no way we won't find him!"

So the exploration went on. The sailor and his companions naturally followed the path that cut diagonally across the islet, and so they came out beside the stream that ran down to the sea.

The animals of European stock and the bits of work done by human hands proved beyond question that people had come to this island before them. A number of specimens from the plant kingdom proved it no less. In some places, in the middle of clearings, it was plain that the ground had been planted with vegetables, probably quite a long time ago.

And what was Harbert's joy when he recognized potatoes, chicory, sorrel, carrots, cabbages and turnips! They had only to gather the seed to enrich the soil of Lincoln Island.

"Good! Fine!" answered Pencroff. "That'll suit Nab nicely, and us too. So even if we don't find the castaway, at least our voyage won't have been wasted, and God will have rewarded us!"

"No doubt," answered Gédéon Spilett. "But to judge by the state these plantings are in, I'm afraid the islet hasn't been inhabited for a long time."

"Yes," answered Harbert. "Anyone living here, whoever it was, would never have neglected such an important crop!"

"Right!" said Pencroff. "This castaway has gone!... That's what we have to suppose..."

"Then we have to accept that the document is already old?"

"Obviously."

"And that the bottle only reached Lincoln Island after floating on the sea for a long time?"

"Why not?" answered Pencroff. "But here comes the night," he added, "and I think we'd better stop searching for now."

"Let's go back on board, and we'll start again tomorrow," said the reporter.

That was the wisest course, and they were about to take it when Harbert pointed to a dark shapeless mass among the trees and cried:

"A house!"

At once all three of them made for the dwelling he had pointed out. In the last light of dusk they could see that it had been built of planks covered with thick tarred canvas.

The door was half shut. Pencroff pushed it open and strode quickly inside...

The house was empty!
