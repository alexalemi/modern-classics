Chapter 10: The Engineer's Invention

_An invention of the engineer's — The question that worries Cyrus Smith — Setting out for the mountain — The forest — Volcanic ground — The tragopans — The mouflons — The first plateau — Camp for the night — The summit of the cone_

A few moments later the three hunters were standing before a crackling fire. Cyrus Smith and the reporter were there. Pencroff looked from one to the other without a word, his capybara in his hand.

"Well, yes, my good fellow," cried the reporter. "Fire, real fire, that's going to roast this magnificent game to perfection, and we'll be feasting on it before long!"

"But who lit it?" asked Pencroff.

"The sun!"

Gédéon Spilett's answer was exactly right. It was the sun that had supplied the heat Pencroff was marveling at. The sailor could not believe his eyes, and he was so dumbfounded that it did not even occur to him to question the engineer.

"So you had a lens, sir?" Harbert asked Cyrus Smith.

"No, my boy," he answered, "but I made one."

And he showed them the device that had served him as a lens. It was simply the two glasses he had taken off the reporter's watch and his own. He had filled the space between them with water and sealed their edges together with a little clay, and so he had made himself a real lens. Focusing the sun's rays on a bit of very dry moss, it had set the moss burning.

The sailor studied the device, then looked at the engineer without saying a word. But his look said a great deal! If Cyrus Smith was not a god to him, he was certainly more than a man. At last Pencroff found his voice again, and he cried:

"Write that down, Mr. Spilett, write that down on your paper!"

"It's written down," answered the reporter.

Then, with Nab's help, the sailor set up the spit, and the capybara, properly cleaned, was soon roasting like an ordinary suckling pig in front of a bright, crackling flame.

The Chimneys had become livable again, not only because the passages were warming up from the fire, but because the partitions of stones and sand had been rebuilt.

As you can see, the engineer and his companion had made good use of the day. Cyrus Smith had almost entirely recovered his strength, and he had tested it by climbing to the plateau above. From there his eye, trained to judge heights and distances, had rested for a long time on the cone whose peak he meant to reach the next day. The mountain, about six miles off to the northwest, seemed to him to stand three thousand five hundred feet above sea level. So an observer posted on its summit would be able to sweep the horizon for a radius of at least fifty miles. It was therefore likely that Cyrus Smith would easily settle the question of "continent or island," which he put, not without reason, ahead of every other.

They had a proper supper. The capybara meat was declared excellent. The sargassum and the stone-pine nuts rounded out the meal, during which the engineer said little. He was absorbed in the next day's plans.

Once or twice Pencroff put forward a few ideas about what ought to be done, but Cyrus Smith, who was clearly a methodical mind, merely shook his head.

"Tomorrow," he kept saying, "we'll know where we stand, and we'll act accordingly."

When the meal was over, fresh armfuls of wood were thrown on the fire, and the occupants of the Chimneys, faithful Top included, fell into a deep sleep. Nothing disturbed that peaceful night, and the next morning, March 29, they woke fresh and rested, ready to set out on the expedition that was to decide their fate.

Everything was ready for the start. What was left of the capybara could feed Cyrus Smith and his companions for another twenty-four hours. Besides, they fully expected to find more food along the way. Since the glasses had been put back on the engineer's and the reporter's watches, Pencroff burned a little more of the linen that was to serve as tinder. As for flint, there would be no shortage of it in ground of volcanic origin like this.

It was half past seven in the morning when the explorers, armed with sticks, left the Chimneys. On Pencroff's advice, it seemed best to take the road they had already traveled through the forest, even if they came back by another way. It was also the most direct route to the mountain. So they turned the southern corner and followed the left bank of the river, leaving it at the point where it bent toward the southwest. They found the path they had already beaten under the evergreens, and at nine o'clock Cyrus Smith and his companions reached the western edge of the forest.

The ground, fairly level until then — marshy at first, then dry and sandy — now showed a gentle slope, rising from the shore toward the interior of the country. A few very shy animals had been glimpsed among the tall trees. Top was quick to flush them, but his master called him back at once, for this was no time to chase them. Later on, they would see. The engineer was not a man to be distracted from his one fixed idea. In fact, you would not have been wrong to say that he was not looking at the country at all, neither at its lay of the land nor at what grew and lived there. His only goal was the mountain he meant to climb, and he went straight for it.

At ten o'clock they halted for a few minutes. On leaving the forest, they had seen the whole system of the mountains spread before them. The mountain was made of two cones. The first, cut off flat at a height of about two thousand five hundred feet, was propped up by oddly shaped buttresses of rock, which seemed to branch out like the talons of an immense claw clamped down on the ground. Between these spurs lay as many narrow valleys, bristling with trees, whose last clumps climbed all the way up to the flat top of the first cone. The vegetation, however, seemed thinner on the side of the mountain that faced northeast, and there they could make out fairly deep streaks that must have been lava flows.

On the first cone sat a second one, slightly rounded at its top and tilted a little to one side. It looked like a huge round hat cocked over one ear. It seemed to be made of bare earth, pierced in many places by reddish rocks.

It was the summit of this second cone that they needed to reach, and the ridge of the spurs ought to offer the best route to it.

"We're on volcanic ground," Cyrus Smith had said, and his companions, following him, began to climb little by little along the back of one of the spurs. It wound its way up in a snaking line, which made it easier going, and ended at the first plateau.

The ground swelled in many places into lumps and blisters; volcanic forces had plainly thrown it into convulsions. Here and there lay stray boulders, plentiful fragments of basalt, pumice stones, and obsidian. In isolated clumps rose the same kind of conifers that, a few hundred feet lower, at the bottom of the narrow gorges, formed thick groves almost impenetrable to the sun.

During this first part of the climb, on the lower slopes, Harbert pointed out tracks that showed large animals had passed by recently — wild beasts, or something else.

"Those creatures may not hand their territory over to us without a fight," said Pencroff.

"Well," answered the reporter, who had already hunted tigers in India and lions in Africa, "we'll see about getting rid of them. But in the meantime, let's keep on our guard!"

All the while they were climbing, little by little. The way was long, made longer by detours and by obstacles that could not be crossed head-on. Sometimes, too, the ground suddenly dropped away, and they found themselves at the edge of deep crevasses that had to be gone around. Retracing their steps like this to find some passable track cost them time and effort. At noon, when the little band stopped for lunch at the foot of a large clump of firs, beside a small stream that went tumbling off in a cascade, they were still only halfway to the first plateau. From then on it seemed unlikely they would reach it before nightfall.

From that spot the sea horizon spread out more widely. But on the right their view was stopped by the sharp headland to the southeast, and they could not tell whether the coast turned back abruptly to join some land lying behind it. To the left, their line of sight gained a few miles to the north. But from the northwest onward, from where the explorers stood, it was cut off sharply by the ridge of a strangely shaped spur, which formed a kind of mighty buttress for the central cone. So they still could not guess the answer to the question Cyrus Smith wanted to settle.

At one o'clock they took up the climb again. They had to bear off toward the southwest and push once more into fairly thick undergrowth. There, under cover of the trees, several pairs of game birds of the pheasant family fluttered about. They were "tragopans," decorated with a fleshy wattle hanging down over the throat and with two thin, round horns set just behind the eyes. Among these pairs, each bird about the size of a rooster, the female was brown all over, while the male was resplendent in red plumage sprinkled with little white teardrops. Gédéon Spilett killed one of these tragopans with a stone thrown hard and true, and Pencroff, made ravenous by the open air, eyed it with more than a little longing.

After leaving this thicket, the climbers boosted one another up on their shoulders and scaled a very steep bank for a hundred feet. They reached a higher level, with few trees, where the ground began to look volcanic. Now they had to work back toward the east, zigzagging to make the slopes easier, for they had become very steep, and each of them had to choose carefully where he put his foot. Nab and Harbert led the way and Pencroff brought up the rear, with Cyrus and the reporter between them. The animals that lived on these heights — and there was no lack of tracks — had to belong to those sure-footed, nimble-backed breeds, the chamois or their Pyrenean cousins the izards. They saw a few of them, but that was not the name Pencroff gave them, for at one point he shouted:

"Sheep!"

Everyone had stopped fifty paces from half a dozen large animals with strong horns that curved back and flattened toward the tips, and with a woolly fleece hidden under long, silky, tawny hair.

These were not ordinary sheep, but a species widely found in the mountain regions of the temperate zones, which Harbert named as mouflons.

"Do they have legs of lamb and chops?" asked the sailor.

"Yes," answered Harbert.

"Well, then, they're sheep!" said Pencroff.

The animals stood motionless among the fragments of basalt and stared in astonishment, as though they were seeing two-legged humans for the first time. Then, their fear suddenly awakened, they vanished, bounding away over the rocks.

"So long!" Pencroff called after them, in a tone so comical that Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett, Harbert and Nab could not help laughing.

The climb went on. On some of the slopes they often noticed traces of lava, very oddly streaked. Little solfataras — vents that breathe out sulfurous steam — sometimes cut across the route the climbers were following, and they had to skirt around the edges. In a few places the sulfur had settled out as crystal crusts, amid the materials that usually come before an outpouring of lava: pozzolana, volcanic grit with irregular grains, heavily roasted, and whitish ash made of countless tiny crystals of feldspar.

As they drew near the first plateau, formed where the lower cone was cut off flat, the climb became very hard indeed. By about four o'clock they had passed the highest belt of trees. All that remained, here and there, were a few twisted, scrawny pines, which must have had a tough life to hold out against the great winds off the sea at that height. Luckily for the engineer and his companions, the weather was fine and the air still, for a strong breeze at an altitude of three thousand feet would have hampered their movements. The clearness of the air let them feel the purity of the sky overhead. A perfect calm reigned around them. They could no longer see the sun, which was now hidden by the vast screen of the upper cone. That screen blocked out the western half of the horizon, and its enormous shadow, stretching all the way to the shore, grew longer as the radiant sun sank on its daily course. A few vapors, more mist than cloud, were beginning to appear in the east, and the sun's rays tinted them with every color of the spectrum.

Only five hundred feet now separated the explorers from the plateau they wanted to reach to make camp for the night. But those five hundred feet grew into more than two miles because of the zigzags they had to make. The ground was practically giving way under their feet. The slopes were often so steep that they slid on the lava flows wherever the grooves, worn smooth by the weather, did not give them enough of a foothold. Evening was gradually coming on, and it was nearly night when Cyrus Smith and his companions, worn out by a seven-hour climb, arrived on the plateau of the first cone.

Now the business was to set up camp and get their strength back, first by eating supper and then by sleeping. This second level of the mountain rose from a base of rocks, and among them they easily found a sheltered spot. Fuel was not plentiful. Still, they could make a fire from the mosses and dry brush that bristled over parts of the plateau. While the sailor built his hearth on stones he set out for the purpose, Nab and Harbert busied themselves gathering fuel for it. They soon came back with their loads of brush. The flint and steel were struck, the burnt linen caught the sparks, and under Nab's breath a crackling fire grew up within a few moments in the shelter of the rocks.

This fire was meant only to fight off the rather cold night air. It was not used to cook the pheasant, which Nab was saving for the next day. The rest of the capybara and a few dozen stone-pine nuts made up the supper. It was not yet half past six, and everything was finished.

Cyrus Smith then had the idea of exploring, in the half-darkness, the wide circular shelf that held up the upper cone of the mountain. Before he took any rest, he wanted to know whether the base of that cone could be walked around, in case its sides proved too steep to climb all the way to the top. The question weighed on his mind, because it was possible that on the side toward which the hat tilted — that is, toward the north — the plateau would be impassable. And if the summit could not be reached on the one hand, and the base of the cone could not be circled on the other, there would be no way to examine the western part of the country, and the purpose of the climb would partly fail.

So the engineer, paying no attention to how tired he was, left Pencroff and Nab to arrange the sleeping quarters and Gédéon Spilett to write up the day's events, and began to follow the circular rim of the plateau, heading north. Harbert went with him.

The night was fine and still, and the darkness was not yet deep. Cyrus Smith and the boy walked side by side without speaking. In some places the plateau opened out wide in front of them, and they passed easily. In others, blocked by fallen rock, it left only a narrow trail where two people could not walk abreast. After twenty minutes of walking, Cyrus Smith and Harbert even had to stop. From that point on, the slopes of the two cones ran together. There was no longer any shoulder to separate the two parts of the mountain. Going around it on slopes that tilted at nearly seventy degrees became impossible.

But if the engineer and the boy had to give up following a circular path, they were given, in exchange, the chance to climb the cone directly.

For in front of them a deep gash opened in the mass of the mountain. It was the broken-out mouth of the upper crater — its spout, if you like — through which the molten matter had poured out in the days when the volcano was still active. Hardened lava and crusted cinders formed a kind of natural staircase, with broad, well-marked steps, which ought to make the way to the summit easier.

One glance was enough for Cyrus Smith to see how things lay, and without hesitating, with the boy behind him, he plunged into the enormous crevice in the gathering darkness.

There were still a thousand feet to climb. Would the inner slopes of the crater be passable? They would soon find out. The engineer would keep on climbing as long as nothing stopped him. Fortunately, these slopes, long and winding, made a wide spiral, like the thread of a screw, around the inside of the volcano, and that helped them gain height.

As for the volcano itself, there could be no doubt that it was completely extinct. Not a wisp of smoke escaped from its sides. Not a flame showed in its deep hollows. Not a rumble, not a murmur, not a tremor came out of that dark well, which perhaps sank all the way down to the bowels of the earth. Even the air inside the crater carried no trace of sulfurous fumes. This was more than a volcano asleep. It was a volcano dead and gone.

Cyrus Smith's attempt was bound to succeed. Little by little, as he and Harbert climbed the inner walls, they saw the crater widen above their heads. The circle of sky framed by the rim of the cone grew noticeably wider. With almost every step that Cyrus Smith and Harbert took, new stars came into view. The magnificent constellations of that southern sky were blazing. At the zenith shone the splendid Antares, in Scorpius, with its pure brilliance, and not far from it Beta Centauri, which is believed to be the star nearest the Earth. Then, as the crater opened out, there appeared Fomalhaut, in the Southern Fish, and the Southern Triangle, and at last, almost at the south pole of the heavens, the glittering Southern Cross, which takes the place of the North Star in the southern hemisphere.

It was nearly eight o'clock when Cyrus Smith and Harbert set foot on the upper crest of the mountain, at the summit of the cone.

By then it was completely dark, and their eyes could not see more than two miles in any direction. Did the sea surround this unknown land, or was this land joined, to the west, to some continent of the Pacific? It was still impossible to tell. Toward the west, a band of cloud lying sharply drawn along the horizon deepened the darkness, and the eye could not make out whether sky and water met there in one unbroken circle.

But at one point on that horizon a faint glow suddenly appeared, sinking slowly as the cloud rose toward the zenith.

It was the thin crescent of the moon, already about to set. But its light was enough to draw the line of the horizon clearly, now that it stood free of the cloud, and the engineer saw the moon's trembling image reflected for an instant on a surface of water.

Cyrus Smith took the boy's hand, and in a grave voice:

"An island!" he said, just as the crescent moon went out in the waves.
