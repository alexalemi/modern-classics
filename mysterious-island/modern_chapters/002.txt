Chapter 3: The One Who Is Missing

_Five in the evening — The one who is missing — Nab's despair — Searching to the north — The islet — A long night of anguish — The morning fog — Nab swims for it — A view of the land — Wading the channel_

The engineer had been carried off by a wave through the mesh of the net, which had given way. His dog had vanished too. The faithful animal had thrown himself into the sea of his own accord, to help his master.

"Come on!" cried the reporter.

And all four of them — Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, Pencroff and Nab — forgot how worn out and exhausted they were, and began to search.

Poor Nab was weeping with rage and despair at once, at the thought that he had lost everything he loved in the world.

Not two minutes had passed between the moment Cyrus Smith disappeared and the moment his companions reached land. So they could hope to get there in time to save him.

"Let's look for him! Let's look for him!" cried Nab.

"Yes, Nab," answered Gédéon Spilett, "and we'll find him!"

"Alive?"

"Alive!"

"Can he swim?" asked Pencroff.

"Yes!" answered Nab. "And besides, Top is with him!"

The sailor heard the roar of the sea and shook his head.

The engineer had vanished off the north part of the coast, about half a mile from the spot where the castaways had just come ashore. If he had managed to reach the nearest point of the shore, then that point had to lie half a mile away at most.

It was nearly six o'clock by then. The mist had just come down, and it made the night very dark. The castaways walked north along the east coast of this land onto which chance had thrown them — an unknown land, whose place on the globe they could not even guess. Underfoot was sandy ground, mixed with stones, that seemed to have no plant life of any kind. The ground was very uneven and rough, and in some places it seemed riddled with little potholes that made walking very hard. Every moment, big, heavy-winged birds burst out of these holes and fled in all directions, unseen in the dark. Others, quicker, rose in flocks and swept past like clouds. The sailor thought he recognized gulls and sea mews, whose shrill whistling competed with the roaring of the sea.

Now and then the castaways stopped, shouted at the top of their lungs, and listened for any answering call from the direction of the ocean. They reasoned, in fact, that if they had come anywhere near the place where the engineer might have come ashore, then the barking of the dog Top would have reached them, even if Cyrus Smith himself was in no condition to give a sign that he was alive. But no cry stood out above the rumble of the breakers and the rattle of the surf. Then the little band would set off again and search every least nook and cranny of the shore.

After twenty minutes of this, the four castaways were brought up short by a foaming fringe of waves. There was no more solid ground. They were standing at the tip of a sharp point, and the sea was breaking furiously against it.

"It's a headland," said the sailor. "We'll have to go back the way we came, keeping to the right, and that way we'll reach the mainland."

"But what if he's out there!" answered Nab, pointing to the ocean, where the enormous waves showed white in the darkness.

"Well then, let's call him!"

And all of them, joining their voices, gave a mighty shout, but nothing answered. They waited for a lull. They tried again. Still nothing.

So the castaways turned back, following the other side of the headland, over ground just as sandy and rocky as before. Pencroff noticed, though, that the shore here was steeper and that the ground was rising, and he guessed that it must join up, by a fairly long slope, with a high coast whose mass he could make out dimly in the dark. There were fewer birds on this part of the shore. The sea, too, was less rough and less noisy here, and it was striking how much the tossing of the waves had died down. They could barely hear the surf. No doubt this side of the headland formed a half-moon cove, sheltered by the sharp point from the swells rolling in from the open sea.

But following this direction meant walking south, away from the stretch of coast where Cyrus Smith might have landed. After a mile and a half the shoreline still showed no bend that would let them turn back north. Yet this headland, whose point they had rounded, surely had to be joined to the mainland somewhere. The castaways were at the end of their strength, but they kept on walking bravely, hoping at every moment to find some sharp turn that would put them back on their first heading.

Imagine their disappointment, then, when after walking about two miles they found themselves stopped once more by the sea, on a fairly high point made of slippery rocks.

"We're on an islet!" said Pencroff. "And we've walked it from one end to the other!"

The sailor was right. The castaways had been thrown, not onto a continent, not even onto an island, but onto an islet no more than two miles long, and obviously not very wide.

Was this barren islet, strewn with stones, without plants, the bleak refuge of a few seabirds, part of some larger archipelago? There was no telling. When the balloon's passengers had glimpsed land through the mist from their basket, they had not been able to make out how big it was. Even so, Pencroff, whose sailor's eyes were used to piercing the dark, thought he could just now make out some dim masses to the west that suggested a high coast.

But in this darkness there was no way to work out what sort of land the islet belonged to, a single island or a whole group of them. Nor could they get off it, since the sea surrounded it. So the search for the engineer had to wait until the next day — and he, alas, had not given a single cry to show where he was.

"Cyrus's silence doesn't prove anything," said the reporter. "He may have fainted, he may be hurt, he may just be unable to answer for the moment. But let's not give up hope."

Then the reporter suggested lighting a fire somewhere on the islet, as a signal to the engineer. But they searched in vain for wood or dry brush. Sand and stones — there was nothing else.

It is easy to understand how Nab must have grieved, and his companions too, for they had grown deeply attached to the fearless Cyrus Smith. It was all too clear that for now they were powerless to help him. They had to wait for daylight. Either the engineer had managed to save himself on his own and had already found shelter somewhere on the coast, or he was lost forever!

Those were long and painful hours to get through. The cold was sharp. The castaways suffered cruelly, but they hardly noticed it. They did not even think of resting for a moment. Forgetting themselves for the sake of their leader, hoping, determined to go on hoping, they walked back and forth across the barren islet, returning again and again to its northern tip, where they had to be nearest to the scene of the disaster. They listened, they shouted, they strained to catch some last call for help. Their voices must have carried a long way, because the air had grown fairly calm by then, and the noise of the sea was beginning to fall with the swell.

At one point one of Nab's cries even seemed to come back as an echo. Harbert pointed it out to Pencroff, adding:

"That would prove there's a coast fairly close to the west."

The sailor nodded. Besides, his eyes could not be deceiving him. If he had seen land, even a little, then land was there.

But that distant echo was the only answer Nab's cries drew, and all along the east side of the islet the vast emptiness stayed silent.

Meanwhile the sky was clearing little by little. Toward midnight a few stars came out, and if the engineer had been there beside his companions, he could have noticed that these were no longer the stars of the Northern Hemisphere. The North Star was nowhere to be seen on this new horizon. The constellations overhead were not the ones he was used to watching in the northern part of the New World, and the Southern Cross was blazing near the south pole of the sky.

The night went by. At about five in the morning on March 25, the upper sky took on a faint tinge of color. The horizon was still dark, but with the first light of day a thick fog rose from the sea, so that no one could see more than twenty paces or so. The fog unrolled in great heavy coils that drifted slowly along.

This was a setback. The castaways could make out nothing around them. While Nab and the reporter peered out over the ocean, the sailor and Harbert looked for the coast to the west. But not a scrap of land could be seen.

"Doesn't matter," said Pencroff. "If I can't see the coast, I can feel it. It's there... right there... as sure as we're not in Richmond anymore!"

But the fog would not last long. It was only the light haze of fair weather. A good strong sun was warming its upper layers, and the heat was filtering down to the surface of the islet.

Sure enough, at about half past six, three quarters of an hour after sunrise, the mist was growing thinner. It thickened up above but melted away below. Soon the whole islet came into view, as though it had just come down out of a cloud. Then the sea appeared, spreading out in a circle, endless to the east but bounded to the west by a high, steep coast.

Yes! The land was there. And there was safety — for the time being, at least. Between the islet and the coast lay a channel half a mile wide, and through it an extremely fast current was running noisily.

At once, though, one of the castaways, listening to nothing but his heart, plunged into the current without asking his companions' advice, without saying a single word. It was Nab. He was in a hurry to reach that coast and work his way north along it. No one could have held him back. Pencroff called after him, but it was no use. The reporter was getting ready to follow Nab.

Then Pencroff went up to him.

"You mean to cross this channel?" he asked.

"Yes," answered Gédéon Spilett.

"Well, wait, take my word for it," said the sailor. "Nab can manage to help his master on his own. If we went into that channel, we'd risk getting swept out to sea by the current — it's running hard as anything. Now, unless I'm mistaken, it's an ebb current. Look, the tide's going out over the sand. So let's be patient, and at low water we may find a place where we can wade across..."

"You're right," answered the reporter. "Let's split up as little as we can..."

Meanwhile Nab was fighting the current with all his strength. He was crossing it at a slant. They could see his dark shoulders come out of the water at every stroke. He was being carried sideways at tremendous speed, but he was gaining on the coast too. It took him more than half an hour to cross that half mile between the islet and the land, and he came ashore several thousand feet from the spot directly opposite the place he had started from. Nab climbed out at the foot of a high granite wall and gave himself a good shake. Then, running hard, he soon disappeared behind a point of rock that jutted out into the sea at about the level of the islet's northern tip.

Nab's companions had followed his daring attempt in anguish, and when he was out of sight they turned their eyes to the land where they were going to ask for refuge, eating a few of the shellfish that lay scattered across the sand as they looked. It was a meager meal, but at least it was a meal.

The coast opposite formed a broad bay. To the south it ended in a very sharp point, bare of any plants and very wild-looking. This point joined the shore in a rather fanciful curve and was braced against tall granite rocks. To the north, on the other hand, the bay widened out into a more rounded coastline that ran from southwest to northeast and ended in a slender cape. Between these two end points, which held the arc of the bay between them, the distance might have been eight miles. Half a mile from the shore, the islet took up a narrow strip of sea. It looked like an enormous whale — like a whale's carcass, greatly enlarged. At its widest it was no more than a quarter of a mile across.

Facing the islet, the shoreline began, in front, with a sandy beach strewn with blackish rocks, which were now coming back into view little by little as the tide went down. Behind it rose a sort of granite rampart, sheer as a wall and topped by an irregular ridge at least three hundred feet high. It ran on like this for three miles and ended suddenly on the right in a flat-cut face that looked as if it had been carved by human hands. On the left, by contrast, above the headland, this ragged sort of cliff broke up into prism-shaped fragments, made of jumbled rocks and fallen rubble, and sloped down in a long ramp that gradually blended into the rocks of the southern point.

On the high plateau along the top of the coast there was not a single tree. It was a bare tabletop, like Table Mountain above Cape Town at the Cape of Good Hope, only smaller. At least, that was how it looked from the islet. But there was no lack of green to the right, behind the flat-cut face. They could easily make out the dim mass of tall trees, crowded together and stretching on beyond the limits of sight. That greenery cheered the eye, which the harsh lines of the granite face had made so gloomy.

Finally, far in the background and above the plateau, to the northwest and at least seven miles off, a white summit was gleaming in the sunlight. It was a cap of snow, crowning some distant mountain.

So there was no saying yet whether this land was an island or part of a continent. But at the sight of the twisted, heaped-up rocks on the left, a geologist would not have hesitated to call them volcanic, for they were beyond question the work of underground fire.

Gédéon Spilett, Pencroff and Harbert studied this land closely. Perhaps they were going to live on it for many long years. Perhaps they would even die on it, if it did not lie on the route of passing ships!

"Well," asked Harbert, "what do you say, Pencroff?"

"Well," answered the sailor, "there's good and bad, like in everything. We'll see. But the ebb is starting to tell. In three hours we'll try the crossing, and once we're over there, we'll do our best to get ourselves out of this mess and find Mr. Smith!"

Pencroff had not been wrong in his forecast. Three hours later, at low tide, most of the sand that formed the bed of the channel had come uncovered. Between the islet and the coast there was nothing left but a narrow passage of water, which would surely be easy to get across.

And in fact, at about ten o'clock, Gédéon Spilett and his two companions stripped off their clothes, bundled them on their heads, and ventured into the channel, which was no more than five feet deep. Harbert, for whom the water would have been too deep, swam like a fish and came through splendidly. All three reached the opposite shore without trouble. There the sun quickly dried them, and they put back on the clothes they had kept out of the water, and they held a council.
