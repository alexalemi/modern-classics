Chapter 18: The Dakkar Crypt

_Each one's thoughts — The shipbuilding resumed — January 1, 1869 — A plume on the top of the volcano — First signs of an eruption — Ayrton and Cyrus Smith at the corral — Exploring the Dakkar Crypt — What Captain Nemo had told the engineer_

At daybreak the colonists made their way back in silence to the mouth of the cavern, which they named "the Dakkar Crypt" in memory of Captain Nemo. The tide was low now, and they could pass easily under the arch, where the water was lapping at the foot of its basalt pillar.

The sheet-iron boat was left there, placed so that the waves could not get at it. As an extra precaution, Pencroff, Nab and Ayrton hauled it up onto the little beach that ran along one side of the crypt, in a spot where it was in no danger at all.

The storm had ended with the night. The last rolls of thunder were dying away in the west. It was no longer raining, but the sky was still heavy with clouds. All in all, this month of October, the beginning of the southern spring, was not getting off to a promising start, and the wind kept jumping from one point of the compass to another, so that no one could count on settled weather.

On leaving the Dakkar Crypt, Cyrus Smith and his companions had set off back along the Corral Road. As they went, Nab and Harbert took care to free the wire the captain had strung between the corral and the crypt, since it might be useful later on.

The colonists said little as they walked. The events of that night of October 15 to 16 had struck them very deeply. The stranger whose influence had protected them so well, the man their imagination had turned into a genie — Captain Nemo — was gone. He and his Nautilus lay buried at the bottom of an abyss. It seemed to each of them that they were more alone than before. They had grown used, so to speak, to counting on that powerful help which they no longer had, and neither Gédéon Spilett nor even Cyrus Smith himself escaped the feeling. And so they all kept a deep silence as they followed the Corral Road.

At about nine in the morning the colonists were back at Granite House.

It had been firmly agreed that the building of the ship would be pushed forward as hard as possible, and Cyrus Smith gave it more of his time and care than ever. No one knew what the future held. And it would be a real safeguard for the colonists to have at their disposal a solid vessel, one that could keep the sea even in heavy weather, and big enough to attempt a voyage of some length if need be. Even if, once the vessel was finished, the colonists did not yet decide to leave Lincoln Island and make for one of the Polynesian archipelagos of the Pacific or the coast of New Zealand, they must at the very least go to Tabor Island as soon as possible and leave there the notice about Ayrton. It was an essential precaution in case the Scottish yacht should come back to these seas, and nothing was to be neglected on that score.

So the work was taken up again. Cyrus Smith, Pencroff and Ayrton, helped by Nab, Gédéon Spilett and Harbert whenever some other urgent job did not call them away, worked without a break. The new vessel had to be ready in five months — that is, by the beginning of March — if they wanted to visit Tabor Island before the equinoctial gales made the crossing impossible. So the carpenters did not lose a moment. They did not, for that matter, have to worry about making any rigging, since the Speedy's had been saved whole. Above all else, then, it was the hull of the ship that had to be finished.

The end of the year 1868 slipped by amid this important work, almost to the exclusion of everything else. After two and a half months the frames had been set in place and the first planks of the hull fitted. It was already clear that the plans Cyrus Smith had drawn were excellent and that the ship would handle well at sea. Pencroff threw himself into the work with a devouring energy, and he did not hesitate to grumble whenever one or another of them laid down the carpenter's ax for the hunter's gun. All the same, the stores of Granite House had to be kept up, with the coming winter in mind. No matter. The good sailor was not happy when there were hands missing from the shipyard. On those occasions, grumbling all the while, he did the work of six men — out of sheer temper.

The whole summer season was a bad one. For some days at a time the heat was crushing, and the air, saturated with electricity, would discharge itself only in violent storms that churned up the layers of the atmosphere. Hardly a day went by without the distant rolling of thunder. It was like a low but never-ending murmur, the kind heard in the equatorial regions of the globe.

January 1, 1869, was even marked by a storm of extreme violence, and lightning struck the island several times. Big trees were hit by the electric fluid and shattered, among them one of the enormous nettle trees that shaded the poultry yard at the southern end of the lake. Did this display in the sky have any connection with what was going on in the bowels of the earth? Was there some kind of link between the disturbances in the air and the disturbances deep inside the globe? Cyrus Smith was inclined to think so, for as these storms grew, the volcanic symptoms grew stronger too.

It was on January 3 that Harbert, who had gone up to Prospect Plateau at dawn to saddle one of the onaggas, saw an enormous plume unrolling from the top of the volcano.

Harbert at once alerted the colonists, who came straight away to look at the summit of Mount Franklin.

"Hey!" cried Pencroff. "That's not steam this time! Looks to me like the giant isn't satisfied with breathing anymore — he's smoking!"

The sailor's image described exactly the change that had taken place at the mouth of the volcano. For three months already the crater had been giving off steam, sometimes more, sometimes less, but it had come only from the minerals boiling inside. This time the steam had given way to a thick smoke, rising in a grayish column more than three hundred feet wide at its base, which spread out like an immense mushroom seven or eight hundred feet above the top of the mountain.

"The chimney's on fire," said Gédéon Spilett.

"And we can't put it out!" answered Harbert.

"Somebody ought to sweep out volcanoes," Nab remarked, apparently in the most serious way in the world.

"Good, Nab!" cried Pencroff. "Will you be the one to take on that sweeping job?"

And Pencroff let out a great roar of laughter.

Cyrus Smith was watching closely the thick smoke that Mount Franklin was throwing up, and he was even listening hard, as though he hoped to catch some distant rumbling. Then he came back to his companions, from whom he had moved a little way apart.

"You're right, my friends. An important change has taken place — there's no point in hiding it from ourselves. The volcanic matter isn't just boiling anymore. It has caught fire, and we are very certainly threatened with an eruption before long!"

"Well then, Mr. Smith, we'll watch the eruption," cried Pencroff, "and we'll applaud it if it's a good one! I don't see anything there for us to worry about!"

"No, Pencroff," answered Cyrus Smith, "because the old path of the lava is still open, and the way the crater is shaped, it has always poured its lava out toward the north until now. And yet..."

"And yet, since there's nothing to be gained from an eruption, it would be better if it didn't happen," said the reporter.

"Who knows?" answered the sailor. "Maybe there's some useful, valuable stuff in that volcano that it'll be kind enough to spit up, and we'll put it to good use!"

Cyrus Smith shook his head like a man who expected nothing good from something that had developed so suddenly. He did not take the consequences of an eruption as lightly as Pencroff did. Even if the lava, because of the way the crater faced, did not directly threaten the wooded and cultivated parts of the island, other complications could arise. Eruptions, in fact, are quite often accompanied by earthquakes. And an island like Lincoln Island, made of such different materials — basalt on one side, granite on the other, lava to the north, loose soil to the south — materials that could not, therefore, be solidly bound to one another, would run the risk of being broken apart. So even if the outpouring of volcanic matter was no very serious danger, any movement in the earth's framework that shook the island could lead to extremely grave consequences.

"It seems to me," said Ayrton, who had lain down so as to put his ear to the ground, "it seems to me I can hear a dull rumbling, like a wagon loaded with iron bars."

The colonists listened with the closest attention and could tell that Ayrton was not mistaken. Now and then the rumbling was mixed with underground roars that swelled into a kind of "rinforzando," as musicians say, and then died slowly away, as though some violent wind had blown through the depths of the globe. But no actual explosion could be heard yet. So they could conclude that the steam and smoke were finding a free way out through the central chimney, and that, since the safety valve was wide enough, nothing would break apart and no explosion was to be feared.

"Now look here!" said Pencroff then. "Aren't we going back to work? Mount Franklin can smoke, bawl, groan, spit out fire and flames as much as it pleases — that's no reason to do nothing! Come on, Ayrton, Nab, Harbert, Mr. Cyrus, Mr. Spilett — today everybody has to lend a hand! We're going to fit the wales, and a dozen arms won't be too many. Within two months I want our new Bonadventure — because we'll keep that name for her, won't we? — floating on the waters of Balloon Harbor! So, not an hour to lose!"

All the colonists, whose arms Pencroff was calling for, went down to the shipyard and set about fitting the wales — the thick planks that run around a ship like a belt and bind the frames of her skeleton firmly together. It was heavy, hard work, and every one of them had to take part.

So they worked steadily all that day of January 3, without worrying about the volcano, which in any case could not be seen from the beach below Granite House. But once or twice great shadows, veiling the sun as it traveled its daily arc across an extremely clear sky, showed that a thick cloud of smoke was passing between its disk and the island. The wind, blowing in from the sea, was carrying all these vapors off to the west. Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett noticed these passing darkenings perfectly well, and more than once they talked over the obvious progress the volcano was making, but the work did not stop. It was, besides, of the greatest importance from every point of view that the vessel should be finished as soon as possible. Whatever might happen, the colonists' safety would be all the better assured by it. Who knew whether this ship might not one day be their only refuge?

That evening, after supper, Cyrus Smith, Gédéon Spilett and Harbert climbed back up to Prospect Plateau. Night had already fallen, and the darkness should let them see whether flames, or red-hot matter thrown up by the volcano, were mixed with the steam and smoke piled up at the mouth of the crater.

"The crater's on fire!" cried Harbert, who, being nimbler than his companions, had reached the plateau first.

Mount Franklin, about six miles away, now looked like a gigantic torch, with a few sooty flames twisting at its top. So much smoke, so much cinder and perhaps ash was mixed with them that their glow, greatly dimmed, did not stand out sharply against the darkness of the night. But a kind of tawny light spread over the island and picked out, dimly, the wooded masses in the foreground. Immense whirling clouds darkened the heights of the sky, and through them a few stars twinkled.

"It's moving fast!" said the engineer.

"That's not surprising," answered the reporter. "The volcano has been waking up for some time already. You remember, Cyrus, that the first steam appeared at about the time we were searching the mountain's spurs for Captain Nemo's hiding place. That was around October 15, if I'm not mistaken?"

"Yes!" answered Harbert. "Two and a half months ago already!"

"So the underground fires have been smoldering for ten weeks," Gédéon Spilett went on, "and it's no wonder they're growing so violent now!"

"Don't you feel a kind of vibration in the ground?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"I do," answered Gédéon Spilett, "but from that to an earthquake..."

"I'm not saying we're threatened with an earthquake," answered Cyrus Smith, "and God keep us from one! No. These vibrations come from the seething of the central fire. The earth's crust is nothing more than the wall of a boiler, and you know that the wall of a boiler, under the pressure of the gases, vibrates like a sounding board. That's what is happening right now."

"What magnificent sprays of fire!" cried Harbert.

At that moment there burst from the crater a sort of fireworks display whose brilliance the steam could not dim. Thousands of glowing fragments and bright points of light were flung out in every direction. Some of them shot past the dome of smoke, pierced it in a swift streak, and left behind them a real dust of glowing sparks. This burst of fire was accompanied by explosion after explosion, like the ripping fire of a battery of machine guns.

After an hour on Prospect Plateau, Cyrus Smith, the reporter and the boy went back down to the beach and returned to Granite House. The engineer was thoughtful, even worried, so much so that Gédéon Spilett felt he should ask him whether he foresaw some danger close at hand, for which the eruption would be either the direct or the indirect cause.

"Yes and no," answered Cyrus Smith.

"All the same," the reporter went on, "the greatest disaster that could happen to us — wouldn't that be an earthquake that turned the island upside down? And I don't think that's to be feared, since the steam and the lava have found a free way out."

"And so," answered Cyrus Smith, "I'm not afraid of an earthquake in the usual sense of the word — the convulsions of the ground caused by the expansion of underground vapors. But other causes can bring about great disasters."

"Which ones, my dear Cyrus?"

"I don't quite know... I need to see... to go over the mountain... In a few days I'll be sure about it."

Gédéon Spilett did not press him, and soon, in spite of the volcano's explosions, which were growing louder and which the echoes of the island repeated, the occupants of Granite House were sound asleep.

Three days went by: January 4, 5 and 6. They kept working on the boat, and the engineer, without explaining himself any further, pushed the work along with all his might. Mount Franklin was now hooded in a dark, sinister-looking cloud, and along with its flames it was spewing out red-hot rocks, some of which fell back into the crater itself. That led Pencroff, who was determined to see only the funny side of the thing, to say:

"Look at that! The giant's playing cup-and-ball! The giant's juggling!"

And in fact the matter it spewed out was falling back into the abyss, and it did not seem that the lava, swollen by the pressure inside, had yet risen as high as the mouth of the crater. At any rate, the breach in the crater's rim to the northeast, which was partly visible, was not pouring any stream down the northern slope of the mountain.

And yet, however urgent the shipbuilding was, other tasks required the colonists' presence at various points on the island. First of all, someone had to go to the corral, where the flock of mouflons and goats was penned, and renew the animals' supply of fodder. So it was agreed that Ayrton would go there the next day, January 7. And since he could manage this job perfectly well alone — he was used to it — Pencroff and the others were a little surprised when they heard the engineer say to Ayrton:

"Since you're going to the corral tomorrow, I'll go with you."

"Hey, Mr. Cyrus!" cried the sailor. "Our working days are numbered, and if you go too, that's four arms fewer!"

"We'll be back the day after," answered Cyrus Smith, "but I need to go to the corral... I want to find out how far the eruption has gone."

"The eruption! The eruption!" answered Pencroff, looking none too pleased. "A fine important thing, this eruption — and one that doesn't worry me much!"

Whatever the sailor thought of it, the engineer's planned expedition was kept for the next day. Harbert would have liked very much to go with Cyrus Smith, but he did not want to annoy Pencroff by being away.

The next day, at sunrise, Cyrus Smith and Ayrton climbed into the cart drawn by the two onaggas, took the Corral Road and drove along it at a brisk trot.

Above the forest passed great clouds which the crater of Mount Franklin kept feeding with sooty matter. These clouds, rolling heavily through the air, were obviously made of mixed substances. It was not the volcano's smoke alone that made them so strangely thick and heavy. Cinders reduced to dust — such as powdered pozzolana — and grayish ashes as fine as the finest flour hung suspended in their dense coils. These ashes are so fine that they have sometimes been seen to stay in the air for whole months. After the eruption of 1783 in Iceland, for more than a year the atmosphere was so loaded with volcanic dust that the sun's rays could hardly get through.

But more often this powdered matter falls back down, and that is what happened on this occasion. Cyrus Smith and Ayrton had hardly reached the corral when a kind of blackish snow, like fine gunpowder, came down and instantly changed the look of the ground. Trees, meadows — everything vanished under a layer several inches thick. But very luckily the wind was blowing from the northeast, and most of the cloud went off to break up over the sea.

"Now that's strange, Mr. Smith," said Ayrton.

"It's serious," answered the engineer. "This pozzolana, this powdered pumice — all this mineral dust, in a word — shows how deep the disturbance goes in the lower layers of the volcano."

"But isn't there anything to be done?"

"Nothing, except to keep track of how it's progressing. So you see to the corral, Ayrton. Meanwhile I'll climb up past the sources of Red Creek and look at the state of the mountain on its northern slope. Then..."

"Then... Mr. Smith?"

"Then we'll pay a visit to the Dakkar Crypt... I want to see... Well, I'll come back for you in two hours."

Ayrton then went into the yard of the corral, and while he waited for the engineer to come back he busied himself with the mouflons and goats, which seemed rather uneasy at these first signs of an eruption.

Meanwhile Cyrus Smith had ventured out along the crest of the eastern spurs. He went around Red Creek and came to the place where he and his companions had found a sulfur spring on their first exploration.

How things had changed! Instead of a single column of smoke, he counted thirteen of them shooting out of the ground, as if some piston were driving them violently upward. It was plain that at this point on the globe the earth's crust was under a terrifying pressure. The air was saturated with sulfurous gas, hydrogen and carbonic acid, mixed with water vapor. Cyrus Smith could feel a trembling in the volcanic tuff that lay scattered over the plain — rock that was nothing but powdery ash which time had hardened into solid blocks — but he could not yet see any trace of new lava.

The engineer was able to confirm this more thoroughly when he looked over the whole northern side of Mount Franklin. Whirls of smoke and flame were pouring out of the crater, and a hail of cinders was falling on the ground, but no lava was flowing from the crater's throat — which proved that the level of the volcanic matter had not yet reached the upper opening of the central chimney.

"And I'd rather it had!" Cyrus Smith said to himself. "At least I'd be sure the lava had taken its usual path again. Who knows whether it won't pour out through some new mouth? But that's not where the danger lies! Captain Nemo foresaw it clearly! No! The danger isn't there!"

Cyrus Smith went on as far as the enormous causeway of lava whose far end framed the narrow Shark Gulf. So he was able to examine the old streaks the lava had left on that side well enough. He had no doubt at all that the last eruption went back a very long way.

Then he turned back, listening to the underground rumblings, which rolled on like continuous thunder, with sharp, ringing explosions standing out against it. At nine in the morning he was back at the corral.

Ayrton was waiting for him.

"The animals are seen to, Mr. Smith," said Ayrton.

"Good, Ayrton."

"They seem uneasy, Mr. Smith."

"Yes. Instinct is speaking in them, and instinct doesn't lie."

"Whenever you're ready..."

"Take a lantern and the flint and steel, Ayrton," answered the engineer, "and let's go."

Ayrton did as he was told. The onaggas had been unharnessed and were wandering about the corral. The gate was shut from the outside, and Cyrus Smith, walking ahead of Ayrton, took the narrow path that led west toward the coast.

The two of them walked over ground padded as if with cotton by the powdery matter that had fallen from the cloud. Not a single four-footed animal showed itself in the woods. Even the birds had fled. Every so often a passing breeze stirred up the layer of ash, and the two colonists, caught in a thick swirl of it, could no longer see each other. Then they took care to hold a handkerchief over their eyes and mouths, for they ran the risk of being blinded and choked.

Under these conditions Cyrus Smith and Ayrton could not walk fast. Besides, the air was heavy, as though part of its oxygen had been burned up and it had become unfit to breathe. Every hundred paces they had to stop and get their breath back. So it was after ten o'clock when the engineer and his companion reached the crest of that huge pile of basalt and porphyry rocks that formed the northwest coast of the island.

Ayrton and Cyrus Smith began to climb down this steep coast, following more or less the dreadful path that had led them to the Dakkar Crypt on that stormy night. In broad daylight the descent was less dangerous, and besides, the layer of ash that covered the polished rock let them plant their feet more firmly on its sloping surfaces.

They soon reached the ledge that ran along the shore at a height of about forty feet. Cyrus Smith remembered that this ledge sloped gently down to sea level. Although the tide was low just then, no beach was showing, and the waves, dirtied by the volcanic dust, were beating straight against the basalt of the shore.

Cyrus Smith and Ayrton found the opening of the Dakkar Crypt without difficulty, and they stopped beneath the last rock, which formed the lowest step of the ledge.

"The sheet-iron boat ought to be here?" said the engineer.

"It is, Mr. Smith," answered Ayrton, drawing toward him the light craft, which was sheltered under the curve of the arch.

"Let's get in, Ayrton."

The two colonists climbed into the boat. A gentle swell of the waves carried it farther in under the very low arch of the crypt, and there Ayrton struck a light with the flint and steel and lit the lantern. Then he took up the two oars, and the lantern was set on the bow so as to throw its light ahead, while Cyrus Smith took the tiller and steered into the darkness of the crypt.

The Nautilus was no longer there to set this gloomy cavern ablaze with its lights. Perhaps its electric glow, still fed by its powerful source, was spreading even now through the depths of the water, but no gleam rose out of the abyss where Captain Nemo lay at rest.

The lantern's light, weak as it was, still allowed the engineer to make his way forward, keeping to the right-hand wall of the crypt. A deathly silence reigned beneath that vault — in its front part, at least — for soon Cyrus Smith could clearly hear the rumblings coming up from the bowels of the mountain.

"It's the volcano," he said.

Soon, along with the noise, the chemical reactions gave themselves away by a sharp smell, and sulfurous fumes caught the engineer and his companion by the throat.

"That's what Captain Nemo feared!" murmured Cyrus Smith, and his face went a little pale. "But we have to go all the way to the end."

"Let's go!" answered Ayrton, who bent over his oars and drove the boat on toward the far end of the crypt.

Twenty-five minutes after passing through the opening, the boat reached the end wall and stopped.

Then Cyrus Smith, standing up on his seat, moved the lantern over the different parts of the wall that separated the crypt from the central chimney of the volcano. How thick was that wall? Was it a hundred feet, or ten? No one could have said. But the underground noises were too easy to hear for it to be very thick.

After exploring the wall along a horizontal line, the engineer fastened the lantern to the end of an oar and moved it over the basalt wall again, higher up.

There, through cracks that could barely be seen, through the badly fitted columns of basalt, an acrid smoke was seeping that poisoned the air of the cavern. Fractures streaked the wall, and a few of them, more sharply marked than the rest, ran down to within only two or three feet of the water in the crypt.

At first Cyrus Smith stayed lost in thought. Then he murmured these words again:

"Yes! The captain was right! That's where the danger lies, and a terrible danger!"

Ayrton said nothing, but at a sign from Cyrus Smith he took up his oars again, and half an hour later he and the engineer came out of the Dakkar Crypt.
