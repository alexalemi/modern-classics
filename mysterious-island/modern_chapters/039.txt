Chapter 18: The Electric Telegraph

_Conversation — Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett — An idea of the engineer's — The electric telegraph — The wires — The battery — The alphabet — Fine weather — The colony prospers — Photography — A snow effect — Two years on Lincoln Island_

"The poor man!" said Harbert. He had rushed to the door, but he came back after seeing Ayrton slide down the elevator rope and vanish into the darkness.

"He'll come back," said Cyrus Smith.

"Now look here, Mr. Cyrus," cried Pencroff, "what does this mean? What! It wasn't Ayrton who threw that bottle into the sea? Then who on earth was it?"

Certainly, if ever a question needed asking, it was that one!

"It was him," answered Nab. "Only, the poor man was already half out of his mind."

"Yes!" said Harbert. "He didn't know what he was doing anymore."

"That's the only way to explain it, my friends," Cyrus Smith answered quickly. "And now I understand how Ayrton could give the exact position of Tabor Island, since the very events that led up to his being abandoned there had taught it to him."

"All the same," Pencroff pointed out, "if he wasn't a brute yet when he wrote his document, and if it's seven or eight years since he threw it into the sea, how is it the paper wasn't spoiled by the damp?"

"That proves," answered Cyrus Smith, "that Ayrton lost his reason at a much more recent time than he believes."

"It must be so," answered Pencroff. "Otherwise the thing would be inexplicable."

"Inexplicable indeed," answered the engineer, who seemed unwilling to carry the conversation any further.

"But did Ayrton tell the truth?" asked the sailor.

"Yes," answered the reporter. "The story he told is true in every point. I remember very well that the newspapers reported Lord Glenarvan's attempt and the result he obtained."

"Ayrton told the truth," added Cyrus Smith. "Don't doubt it, Pencroff, because the truth was cruel enough to him. A man tells the truth when he accuses himself like that!"

The next day — December 21 — the colonists went down to the beach and climbed up to the plateau, but they found no Ayrton there. During the night Ayrton had gone off to his house at the corral, and the colonists judged it best not to trouble him with their company. Time would no doubt do what encouragement had not been able to do.

So Harbert, Pencroff and Nab went back to their usual occupations. As it happened, that same day the same work brought Cyrus Smith and the reporter together in the workshop at the Chimneys.

"Do you know, my dear Cyrus," said Gédéon Spilett, "that the explanation you gave yesterday about that bottle didn't satisfy me at all? How can anyone believe that the poor wretch could have written that document and thrown that bottle into the sea without keeping the slightest memory of it?"

"Which is why it wasn't he who threw it, my dear Spilett."

"Then you still believe..."

"I believe nothing, I know nothing!" Cyrus Smith answered, cutting the reporter off. "I'm content to file this incident among the ones I haven't been able to explain so far!"

"Really, Cyrus," said Gédéon Spilett, "these things are unbelievable! Your rescue, the chest stranded on the sand, Top's adventures, and now this bottle... Are we never going to get the answer to these riddles?"

"Yes!" the engineer answered sharply. "Yes, even if I have to search this island down to its very bowels!"

"Perhaps chance will give us the key to this mystery!"

"Chance, Spilett! I hardly believe in chance, any more than I believe in mysteries in this world. There's a cause for everything inexplicable that happens here, and I'll find that cause. But in the meantime, let's watch and work."

January came. The year 1867 was beginning. The summer work was pushed ahead steadily. In the days that followed, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett went over toward the corral and were able to see that Ayrton had taken possession of the home that had been prepared for him. He was looking after the large flock entrusted to his care, and he was going to spare his companions the trouble of coming to visit the corral every two or three days. All the same, so as not to leave Ayrton alone for too long, the colonists paid him visits fairly often.

Nor was it a matter of indifference — given certain suspicions that the engineer and Gédéon Spilett shared — that this part of the island should be kept under some sort of watch, and if anything happened, Ayrton would not fail to let the people at Granite House know.

Still, it was possible that something might happen suddenly and need to reach the engineer quickly. And quite apart from anything to do with the mystery of Lincoln Island, plenty of other things might happen that would call for the colonists to act at once: a ship passing in the offing within sight of the west coast, a wreck on the western approaches, a possible landing by pirates, and so on.

So Cyrus Smith decided to put the corral in instant communication with Granite House.

It was on January 10 that he told his companions about his plan.

"Now look here! How are you going to manage that, Mr. Cyrus?" asked Pencroff. "You aren't by any chance thinking of putting in a telegraph?"

"Exactly that," answered the engineer.

"Electric?" cried Harbert.

"Electric," answered Cyrus Smith. "We have everything we need to make a battery. The hardest part will be drawing out the iron wire, but with a drawplate I think we'll manage it."

"Well, after that," the sailor replied, "I'm not giving up hope of seeing us ride on a railroad one of these days!"

So they set to work, beginning with the hardest part — that is, making the wire — because if they failed at that, there would be no point in building the battery and the other equipment.

The iron of Lincoln Island, as we know, was of excellent quality, and so it was very well suited to being drawn out into wire. Cyrus Smith began by making a drawplate. This was a plate of steel pierced with cone-shaped holes of various sizes, which would bring the wire, one hole after another, down to the thinness he wanted. This piece of steel was tempered "to its full hardness," as metalworkers say, and then fixed immovably onto a frame driven solidly into the ground, only a few feet from the great waterfall, whose driving power the engineer was going to put to use once again.

That was where the fulling mill stood. It was not running at the time, but its main shaft, which turned with tremendous force, could be used to draw the wire by winding it around itself.

The operation was a delicate one and took a great deal of care. The iron was first prepared in long, thin rods whose ends had been filed down to a point. Each rod was pushed through the largest hole in the drawplate and pulled through by the main shaft, which wound it up over a length of twenty-five to thirty feet. Then it was unwound and fed in turn through the holes of smaller and smaller diameter! In the end the engineer had wires forty to fifty feet long, which could easily be joined together and strung across the five miles that separated the corral from the grounds of Granite House.

It took only a few days to finish this job. In fact, as soon as the machine was up and running, Cyrus Smith left his companions to work as wire-drawers and turned to making his battery.

What he needed, in this case, was a battery that would give a steady current. As everyone knows, the elements of modern batteries are usually made of retort carbon, zinc and copper. The engineer had no copper at all. In spite of all his searching, he had not found a trace of it on Lincoln Island, and he would have to do without. Retort carbon — that is, the hard graphite left in the retorts of gasworks after the hydrogen has been driven out of the coal — could have been produced, but it would have meant setting up special apparatus, which would have been a big job. As for zinc, you will remember that the chest found at Jetsam Point had been lined with a casing of that metal, which could not have been put to better use than this.

So Cyrus Smith, after thinking it over carefully, decided to build a very simple battery, close to the one Becquerel invented in 1820, in which zinc is the only metal used. As for the other substances, nitric acid and potash, he had all of them at hand.

Here, then, is how this battery was put together. Its effect was to come from the action of the acid and the potash on each other.

A number of glass bottles were made and filled with nitric acid. The engineer sealed each one with a stopper, and through the stopper ran a glass tube. The tube was closed at its lower end, which was meant to dip into the acid, by a plug of clay held in place with a piece of cloth. Into this tube, through its upper end, he then poured a solution of potash, which he had obtained beforehand by burning various plants to ash. In this way the acid and the potash could act on each other through the clay.

Next, Cyrus Smith took two strips of zinc. One was dipped into the nitric acid, the other into the potash solution. At once a current was set up, running from the strip in the bottle to the strip in the tube. When the two strips were connected by a metal wire, the strip in the tube became the positive pole of the apparatus and the strip in the bottle its negative pole. Each bottle, then, produced its own current, and all these currents together would be enough to bring about every effect needed for electric telegraphy.

Such was the ingenious and very simple apparatus that Cyrus Smith built — the apparatus that would let him set up telegraphic communication between Granite House and the corral.

It was on February 6 that they began planting the poles, fitted with glass insulators, that were to carry the wire along the Corral Road. A few days later the wire was strung, ready to carry the electric current at a speed of a hundred thousand kilometers a second, with the earth taking care of bringing it back to its starting point.

Two batteries had been made, one for Granite House and the other for the corral, because if the corral was to communicate with Granite House, it might be just as useful for Granite House to communicate with the corral.

As for the receiver and the transmitter, they were very simple. At both stations the wire was coiled around an electromagnet — that is, a piece of soft iron with a wire wound around it. When the connection was made between the two poles, the current set out from the positive pole, ran along the wire, passed through the electromagnet, which became a magnet for the time being, and returned through the ground to the negative pole. When the current was broken, the electromagnet stopped being a magnet at once. So all that was needed was to place a plate of soft iron in front of the electromagnet. While the current was flowing, the plate was pulled toward it; when the current was broken, the plate fell back. Once he had this movement of the plate, Cyrus Smith could very easily connect it to a needle set on a dial with the letters of the alphabet around its rim, and in that way the two stations could send messages to each other.

The whole thing was completely installed on February 12. That day Cyrus Smith sent the current through the wire, asked whether all was well at the corral, and a few moments later received a satisfactory reply from Ayrton.

Pencroff was beside himself with joy. Every morning and every evening he sent a telegram to the corral, and it never went unanswered.

This way of keeping in touch had two very real advantages. First, it let them make sure Ayrton was at the corral. Second, it kept him from being completely cut off. Besides, Cyrus Smith never let a week go by without going to see him, and Ayrton came to Granite House now and then, where he always found a warm welcome.

So the fine season went by in the usual work. The colony's resources, especially in vegetables and grain, grew from day to day, and the plants brought back from Tabor Island had done perfectly. Prospect Plateau looked very reassuring. The fourth wheat harvest had been splendid, and, as you can well imagine, nobody took it into his head to count whether the four hundred billion grains were all there at the harvest. Pencroff had had the idea of doing it, all the same. But Cyrus Smith told him that even if he managed to count three hundred grains a minute — that is, nine thousand an hour — it would take him about five thousand five hundred years to finish the job, and the good sailor decided he had better give it up.

The weather was magnificent, the temperature very hot during the day. But in the evening the sea breezes came in to cool the burning air and gave the people of Granite House fresh nights. There were a few thunderstorms, though. They did not last long, but they fell on Lincoln Island with extraordinary force. For several hours at a time the lightning never stopped setting the sky ablaze, and the thunder rolled without a pause.

By this time the little colony was extremely prosperous. The residents of the poultry yard were multiplying fast, and the colonists lived off the surplus, since it was becoming urgent to bring the population down to a more reasonable number. The pigs had already had young, and, as you can imagine, looking after these animals took up a large part of Nab's and Pencroff's time. The onaggas, which had produced two pretty foals, were ridden most often by Gédéon Spilett and Harbert, who had become an excellent rider under the reporter's coaching. They were also harnessed to the cart, to haul wood and coal to Granite House, or the various minerals the engineer was using.

Several reconnaissance trips were pushed, during this time, deep into the Far West forests. The explorers could venture there without fear of the heat, because the sun's rays barely got through the thick tangle of branches overhead. In this way they visited the whole left bank of the Mercy, which ran beside the road from the corral to the mouth of Fall River.

But on these excursions the colonists took care to be well armed, because they often met wild boars, very fierce and savage, which had to be fought in earnest.

During this season a fearful war was also waged on the jaguars. Gédéon Spilett had vowed a very special hatred against them, and his pupil Harbert backed him up well. Armed as they were, they hardly feared meeting one of these big cats. Harbert's daring was superb, and the reporter's coolness astonishing. And so some twenty magnificent skins already decorated the great hall of Granite House, and if things went on like this, the jaguars would soon be extinct on the island, which was exactly what the hunters were aiming for.

The engineer sometimes took part in the various reconnaissance trips into the unknown parts of the island, which he observed with close attention. In the thickest parts of those vast woods it was other tracks than animals' that he was looking for, but nothing suspicious ever met his eyes. Neither Top nor Jup, who went with him, gave any sign by the way they behaved that there was anything out of the ordinary. And yet, more than once again, the dog barked at the mouth of the well that the engineer had explored without result.

It was during this time that Gédéon Spilett, with Harbert's help, took several pictures of the most picturesque parts of the island, using the photographic outfit that had been found in the chest and had not been used until then.

The outfit, fitted with a powerful lens, was very complete. The substances needed for making photographs — collodion to prepare the glass plate, silver nitrate to make it sensitive to light, hyposulfate of soda to fix the image once it was taken, ammonium chloride to bathe the paper meant for the positive print, sodium acetate and gold chloride to soak that print in — nothing was missing. Even the papers were there, already treated with chloride. Before laying them in the frame on top of the negatives, all you had to do was soak them for a few minutes in silver nitrate diluted with water.

So in a short time the reporter and his assistant became skilled operators, and they got some quite fine prints of the scenery: the whole island, taken from Prospect Plateau, with Mount Franklin on the horizon; the mouth of the Mercy, so picturesquely framed by its high rocks; the clearing and the corral set against the first slopes of the mountain; the whole curious sweep of Claw Cape, of Jetsam Point, and so on.

The photographers did not forget to take portraits of everyone living on the island, without leaving anyone out.

"It fills the place up," said Pencroff.

And the sailor was delighted to see his likeness, faithfully reproduced, decorating the walls of Granite House, and he liked to stop in front of this exhibition just as he would have stopped at the finest shop windows on Broadway.

But, it must be said, the most successful portrait of all was beyond question Master Jup's. Master Jup had posed with a seriousness impossible to describe, and his picture was so lifelike it seemed about to speak!

"You'd think he was about to pull a face!" cried Pencroff.

And if Master Jup had not been pleased, he would have been very hard to please. But he was pleased, and he gazed at his picture with a sentimental air that let a slight touch of vanity show through.

The great heat of summer ended with the month of March. The weather was sometimes rainy, but the air was still warm. This month of March, which corresponds to September in northern latitudes, was not as fine as they might have hoped. Perhaps it meant an early, harsh winter.

One morning — the 21st — they might even have believed that the first snows had arrived. Harbert had gone early to one of the windows of Granite House, and he called out:

"Look! The islet's covered with snow!"

"Snow, at this time of year?" answered the reporter, who had joined the boy.

Their companions were soon beside them, and they could only agree on one thing: not only the islet but the whole beach at the foot of Granite House was covered with a white layer, spread evenly over the ground.

"It's snow all right!" said Pencroff.

"Or something very like it!" answered Nab.

"But the thermometer reads fifty-eight degrees!" Gédéon Spilett pointed out.

Cyrus Smith looked at the white sheet without giving an opinion, because he truly did not know how to explain this phenomenon, at that time of year and at such a temperature.

"A thousand devils!" cried Pencroff. "Our crops are going to freeze!"

And the sailor was getting ready to go down when the nimble Jup got ahead of him and slid all the way to the ground.

But the orangutan had not yet touched the ground when the enormous layer of snow rose up and scattered into the air in flakes so countless that they veiled the light of the sun for several minutes.

"Birds!" cried Harbert.

They were indeed flocks of seabirds with dazzling white plumage. They had come down by hundreds of thousands on the islet and the shore, and they vanished into the distance, leaving the colonists open-mouthed, as if they had watched a scene change in a fairy play, with summer taking the place of winter before their eyes. Unfortunately, the change had been so sudden that neither the reporter nor the boy managed to bring down one of these birds, and they could not tell what species they were.

A few days later it was March 26, and two years had passed since the castaways of the air had been thrown onto Lincoln Island!
