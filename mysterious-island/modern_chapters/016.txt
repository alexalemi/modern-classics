Chapter 17: The New Waterfall

_A visit to the lake — The telltale current — Cyrus Smith's plans — The dugong fat — What to do with pyritic shale — Iron sulfate — How glycerin is made — Soap — Saltpeter — Sulfuric acid — Nitric acid — The new waterfall_

The next day, May 7, Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett left Nab to make breakfast and climbed up to Prospect Plateau, while Harbert and Pencroff went up the river to lay in a fresh supply of wood.

The engineer and the reporter soon reached the little beach at the southern tip of the lake where the amphibian had been left stranded. Flocks of birds had already swooped down on that great mass of flesh, and they had to drive them off with stones, because Cyrus Smith wanted to keep the dugong's fat and use it for the needs of the colony. As for the animal's meat, it could not fail to make excellent food, since in certain parts of Malaysia it is kept specially for the tables of the local princes. But that was Nab's business.

At that moment Cyrus Smith had other things on his mind. The incident of the day before had not faded from his thoughts, and it went on troubling him. He would have liked to get to the bottom of that underwater fight, and to know what cousin of the mastodons, or what other sea monster, had given the dugong such a strange wound.

So there he stood on the shore of the lake, watching, observing. But nothing showed itself beneath the calm water, which sparkled in the first rays of the sun.

At the little beach where the dugong's body lay, the water was shallow. But from there on the bottom of the lake sloped gradually downward, and in the middle it was probably very deep. The lake could be thought of as a wide basin that had been filled by the waters of Red Creek.

"Well, Cyrus," said the reporter, "I don't see anything suspicious about this water, do you?"

"No, my dear Spilett," answered the engineer, "and I really don't know how to explain what happened yesterday!"

"I admit," Gédéon Spilett went on, "that the wound on that amphibian is strange, to say the least. And I can't explain either how Top came to be thrown out of the water so violently. You'd really think a powerful arm had flung him out like that, and that the same arm, holding a dagger, had then killed the dugong!"

"Yes," answered the engineer, who had become thoughtful. "There's something here I can't understand. But do you understand any better, my dear Spilett, how I myself was saved — how I could have been pulled out of the waves and carried to the dunes? No, you don't, do you? That's why I sense some mystery here, one we'll no doubt uncover someday. So let's keep watching, but let's not dwell on these odd incidents in front of our companions. Let's keep our observations to ourselves and get on with our work."

As we know, the engineer had not yet been able to find where the lake's overflow escaped. But since he had seen no sign that it ever flooded its banks, there had to be a spillway somewhere. And now, as it happened, Cyrus Smith was rather surprised to notice a fairly strong current that could be felt at that very spot. He threw in a few small pieces of wood and saw that they drifted toward the southern corner. He followed the current, walking along the bank, and came to the southernmost point of the lake.

There the water seemed to sink a little, as though it were suddenly vanishing into some crack in the ground.

Cyrus Smith listened, putting his ear down to the level of the lake, and he heard, quite distinctly, the sound of an underground waterfall.

"That's where it is," he said, getting up again. "That's where the water drains out. That's where, no doubt, it runs through a channel cut in the mass of granite to reach the sea — through some hollows we might be able to put to good use! Well, I'm going to find out!"

The engineer cut a long branch and stripped it of its leaves. By pushing it down into the water at the corner where the two banks met, he found that there was a wide hole opening just one foot below the surface. This hole was the mouth of the spillway they had searched for in vain until then. The current there was so strong that the branch was torn out of the engineer's hands and disappeared.

"There's no doubt about it now," Cyrus Smith said again. "That's the mouth of the spillway, and I'm going to uncover it."

"How?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"By lowering the level of the lake by three feet."

"And how will you lower it?"

"By opening another outlet for the water, a bigger one than this."

"Where, Cyrus?"

"On the part of the shore that comes closest to the coast."

"But that shore is granite!" the reporter pointed out.

"Well then," answered Cyrus Smith, "I'll blow up the granite, and the water, as it rushes out, will drop far enough to uncover this opening..."

"And it'll make a waterfall as it pours down onto the beach," the reporter added.

"A waterfall we'll put to use!" answered Cyrus. "Come on, come on!"

The engineer hurried his companion along, and the reporter's trust in Cyrus Smith was so complete that he did not doubt for a moment that the plan would succeed. And yet — that granite shore — how could they open it? Without gunpowder, and with crude tools, how could they break up that rock? Wasn't the engineer about to throw himself into a task beyond his strength?

When Cyrus Smith and the reporter got back to the Chimneys, they found Harbert and Pencroff busy unloading their timber raft.

"The woodcutters are just about done, Mr. Cyrus," said the sailor with a laugh, "and when you need masons..."

"Not masons — chemists," answered the engineer.

"Yes," added the reporter, "we're going to blow up the island..."

"Blow up the island!" cried Pencroff.

"Part of it, at least!" Gédéon Spilett replied.

"Listen to me, my friends," said the engineer.

And he told them what he had found. In his view, there had to be a hollow of some size inside the mass of granite that held up Prospect Plateau, and he meant to get into it. To do that, they first had to clear the opening through which the water was rushing, and that meant lowering the level of the lake by giving the water a wider way out. Hence the need to make an explosive substance that could cut a deep breach at another point on the shore. That was what Cyrus Smith was going to attempt, using the minerals that nature had put within his reach.

There is no need to say how eagerly they all, and Pencroff above all, greeted this plan. Using drastic measures, tearing open the granite, creating a waterfall — that suited the sailor perfectly! And he would be a chemist just as readily as he had been a mason or a bootmaker, since the engineer needed chemists. He would be anything anybody wanted — "even a teacher of dancing and good manners," he told Nab — if that were ever needed.

Nab and Pencroff were given the first job: removing the dugong's fat and preserving its meat, which was meant for the table. They set off at once, without even asking for any more explanation. Their trust in the engineer was absolute.

A few moments after they left, Cyrus Smith, Harbert, and Gédéon Spilett, dragging the hurdle behind them, went up the river toward the coal seam. There they would find plenty of that pyritic shale — slate-like rock full of iron pyrite — which does indeed turn up in the most recent of the rock layers geologists then called "transition" formations, and of which Cyrus Smith had already brought back a sample.

The whole day was spent hauling a good amount of this pyrite-bearing rock to the Chimneys. By evening they had several tons of it.

The next day, May 8, the engineer began his chemistry. This pyritic shale is made up mainly of carbon, silica, alumina, and iron sulfide, with the iron sulfide in excess. The task was to separate out the iron sulfide and turn it into iron sulfate as quickly as possible. Once they had the sulfate, they would extract sulfuric acid from it.

That was in fact the goal. Sulfuric acid is one of the most widely used of all chemical agents — so much so that a nation's industrial importance can be measured by how much of it the nation consumes. Later on this acid would be enormously useful to the colonists for making candles, tanning hides, and so on. But for the moment the engineer had another use in mind for it.

Behind the Chimneys, Cyrus Smith chose a spot and had the ground carefully leveled. On this ground he laid a heap of branches and chopped wood. On top of it went pieces of the pyritic shale, propped up against one another like the stones of an arch. Then the whole thing was covered with a thin layer of pyrite that had first been broken down to the size of walnuts.

When that was done, they set fire to the wood. Its heat passed to the shale, and the shale caught fire too, since it contained carbon and sulfur. Then fresh layers of crushed pyrite were added, built up into an enormous mound. They left a few vents in it, and covered its outside with earth and grass, just as if they were making charcoal by slowly burning a stack of wood.

Then they let the change take place. It would take no less than ten to twelve days for the iron sulfide to turn into iron sulfate, and the alumina into aluminum sulfate. Both of these substances dissolve in water. The rest — the silica, the burnt carbon, and the ashes — do not.

While this chemical work went on, Cyrus Smith had other jobs done. They put more than zeal into it. It was something close to fury.

Nab and Pencroff had removed the dugong's fat and collected it in big earthenware jars. From this fat the engineer wanted to separate one of its ingredients, glycerin, by saponifying it — that is, by turning it into soap. To get that result, all he had to do was treat it with soda or with lime. Either substance would attack the fat and form a soap, and in doing so would set the glycerin free — and glycerin was exactly what the engineer was after. He had no shortage of lime, as we know. But treating the fat with lime would give only lime soaps, which do not dissolve in water and so are useless. Treating it with soda, on the other hand, would give a soap that did dissolve, one that would come in handy for washing and cleaning around the house. So, being a practical man, Cyrus Smith naturally tried to get soda. Was that difficult? No, because the shore was thick with seaweed and sea plants: glasswort, ice plants, and all the rockweeds and other brown seaweeds that make up kelp and wrack. So they gathered a great quantity of these plants. First they dried them, then they burned them in pits in the open air. The fire was kept going for several days, so that the heat would climb high enough to melt the ashes. The result of all that burning was a solid, grayish mass, long known by the name of "natural soda."

With that in hand, the engineer treated the fat with the soda. This gave him, on one side, a soap that dissolved in water, and on the other, that neutral substance, glycerin.

But that was not all. With an eye to what he meant to prepare later, Cyrus Smith still needed another substance: potassium nitrate, better known as niter, or saltpeter.

Cyrus Smith could have made this substance by treating potassium carbonate — which is easy to extract from the ashes of plants — with nitric acid. But he had no nitric acid, and nitric acid was precisely what he wanted to end up with. So it was a vicious circle, one he could never have broken out of. Very luckily, this time nature was going to hand him the saltpeter, and all he would have to do was pick it up. Harbert found a bed of it in the north of the island, at the foot of Mount Franklin, and all that remained was to purify it.

These various jobs took about eight days. So they were finished before the iron sulfide had finished turning into iron sulfate. In the days that followed, the colonists had time to make fireproof pottery out of potter's clay and to build a brick furnace of a special design, which would be used to distill the iron sulfate once they had it. All this was finished around May 18, at about the same time the chemical change was complete. Gédéon Spilett, Harbert, Nab, and Pencroff, skillfully guided by the engineer, had become the handiest workmen in the world. Besides, of all teachers, necessity is the one people listen to most and the one who teaches best.

When the pile of pyrite had been completely burned down, what was left — iron sulfate, aluminum sulfate, silica, leftover carbon, and ashes — was put into a basin full of water. They stirred this mixture, let it settle, and then poured off the liquid. That gave them a clear liquid with the iron sulfate and aluminum sulfate dissolved in it, while the other materials stayed behind as solids, since they do not dissolve. Finally, once part of this liquid had evaporated, crystals of iron sulfate formed and settled out. The mother liquor — that is, the liquid that had not evaporated, which held the aluminum sulfate — was thrown away.

So Cyrus Smith now had a fairly large quantity of these iron sulfate crystals, and the next task was to get sulfuric acid out of them.

In industry, making sulfuric acid takes a costly installation. You need large factories, special equipment, platinum apparatus, and chambers lined with lead, which the acid cannot eat through, where the transformation takes place, and so on. The engineer had none of this equipment. But he knew that in Bohemia in particular, sulfuric acid is made by simpler methods, which even have the advantage of producing it at a higher concentration. That is how the acid known as Nordhausen acid is made.

To get his sulfuric acid, Cyrus Smith now had only one operation left to perform: to heat the iron sulfate crystals strongly in a closed vessel, so that the sulfuric acid would distill off as vapor. That vapor, when it condensed, would then give him the acid.

This was the job the fireproof pottery was for — the crystals were placed inside it — and the furnace, whose heat would distill the sulfuric acid. The operation was carried out perfectly, and on May 20, twelve days after he had begun, the engineer was the owner of the agent he expected to use later in so many different ways.

Now, why did he want this agent? Quite simply to produce nitric acid. And that was easy, since saltpeter, attacked by sulfuric acid, gave him exactly that acid by distillation.

But in the end, what was he going to use this nitric acid for? His companions still did not know, because he had not yet said the last word about his work.

The engineer was nearing his goal, though, and one last operation gave him the substance that had demanded all these preparations.

He took some nitric acid and brought it together with the glycerin, which he had first concentrated by evaporating it in a water bath — a pot set inside a larger pot of hot water. And even without using a freezing mixture to keep it cold, he obtained several pints of an oily, yellowish liquid.

Cyrus Smith had done this last operation alone, off by himself, far from the Chimneys, because there was a danger of explosion. When he brought a flask of the liquid back to his friends, all he said was:

"Here's nitroglycerin!"

It was indeed that terrible product, whose explosive power is perhaps ten times that of ordinary gunpowder, and which has already caused so many accidents! Since a way has been found to turn it into dynamite, though — that is, to mix it with a solid substance, clay or sugar, porous enough to hold it — the dangerous liquid has been used more safely. But dynamite was not yet known at the time when the colonists were at work on Lincoln Island.

"And this stuff is what's going to blow up our rocks?" said Pencroff, looking rather doubtful.

"Yes, my friend," answered the engineer. "And this nitroglycerin will have all the more effect because the granite is extremely hard and will put up that much more resistance to being blown apart."

"And when do we get to see it, Mr. Cyrus?"

"Tomorrow, as soon as we've dug a blasting hole," answered the engineer.

The next day — May 21 — at dawn, the miners went to a point of land that formed the eastern shore of Lake Grant, only five hundred paces from the coast. At that spot the plateau lay lower than the surface of the lake, and the water was held back only by its frame of granite. So it was obvious that if they broke this frame, the water would escape through the gap and form a stream. That stream would run down the sloping surface of the plateau and then plunge over onto the beach. As a result, the level of the whole lake would drop, and the mouth of the spillway would be uncovered — which was the final goal.

So it was the frame they had to break. Under the engineer's direction, Pencroff, armed with a pickax that he swung with skill and strength, attacked the outer face of the granite. The hole they had to drill started from a horizontal ledge on the shore, and it had to slant downward so as to reach a level well below the surface of the lake. That way, when the explosion pushed the rocks apart, the water would be able to pour out freely and drop far enough.

The work took a long time, because the engineer wanted a tremendous effect and meant to put no less than ten liters of nitroglycerin into the blast. But Pencroff, taking turns with Nab, did so well that by four o'clock in the afternoon the blasting hole was finished.

That left the question of how to set off the explosive. Normally nitroglycerin is fired by means of fulminate caps, which explode and so set off the main charge. It takes a shock to make it explode; if it is simply lit, it will burn without blowing up.

Cyrus Smith could certainly have made a cap. In place of fulminate, he could easily have made something like guncotton, since he had nitric acid on hand. Packed into a cartridge and pushed down into the nitroglycerin, this substance would have exploded when lit by a fuse and set off the blast.

But Cyrus Smith knew that nitroglycerin has the property of detonating when it is struck. So he decided to make use of this property, ready to try another method if this one failed.

The fact is that the blow of a hammer on a few drops of nitroglycerin spread on the surface of a hard stone is enough to set off an explosion. But whoever was there to strike the hammer blow would be a victim of the explosion. So Cyrus Smith came up with this idea. Above the blasting hole he set up a post, and from it, on a plant fiber, he hung a lump of iron weighing several pounds. A second long fiber, first soaked in sulfur, was tied by one end to the middle of the first, while its other end trailed along the ground for a distance of several feet from the hole. When this second fiber was lit, it would burn until the flame reached the first. The first fiber would catch fire in its turn and break, and the lump of iron would come crashing down onto the nitroglycerin.

So this apparatus was set up. Then the engineer sent his companions away, filled the blasting hole until the nitroglycerin came right up level with its mouth, and sprinkled a few drops of it on the surface of the rock, just under the hanging lump of iron.

That done, Cyrus Smith took the end of the sulfur-soaked fiber and lit it. Then he left the spot and went back to join his companions at the Chimneys.

The fiber was supposed to burn for twenty-five minutes, and sure enough, twenty-five minutes later, an explosion went off that no words could describe. The whole island seemed to shake on its foundations. A spray of stones shot up into the air as if a volcano had spewed it out. The blast of displaced air was so violent that the rocks of the Chimneys swayed. The colonists, though they were more than two miles from the blast, were knocked flat on the ground.

They got up again, climbed back up to the plateau, and ran toward the spot where the explosion must have torn open the lake's bank...

A triple hurrah burst from their chests! The granite frame had been split wide open! A rapid stream of water was pouring out of it. It raced foaming across the plateau, reached the edge, and plunged three hundred feet down onto the beach!
