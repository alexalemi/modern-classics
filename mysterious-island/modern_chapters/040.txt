Chapter 19: Memories of Home

_Memories of home — The chances ahead — A plan to survey the island's coasts — Departure on April 16 — Serpentine Peninsula seen from the sea — The basalt of the west coast — Bad weather — Night falls — A new incident_

Two years already! And for two years the colonists had had no contact with their fellow human beings! They had no news of the civilized world. They were lost on this island as completely as if they had been on some tiny asteroid of the solar system!

What was happening at home just then? The image of their country was always before their eyes — a country torn apart by civil war when they had left it, and one that the Southern rebellion was perhaps still drenching in blood! It was a great grief to them, and they often talked these things over, though never once doubting that the cause of the North must triumph, for the honor of the American Union.

In those two years not a single ship had passed within sight of the island — or at least not a single sail had been spotted. It was obvious that Lincoln Island lay outside the usual sea routes, and even that it was unknown — as the maps proved, besides — for even without a harbor, its fresh water ought to have drawn ships looking to refill their casks. But the sea around it was always empty, as far as the eye could reach, and the colonists could hardly count on anyone but themselves to get home.

There was, however, one chance of rescue, and that chance was discussed one day in the first week of April by the colonists, who had gathered in the great hall of Granite House.

They had, in fact, been talking about America, about the country where they were born and which they had so little hope of seeing again.

"There's no getting around it," said Gédéon Spilett. "We'll have only one way — just one — of leaving Lincoln Island, and that's to build a ship big enough to stay at sea for a few hundred miles. It seems to me that when you've built a sloop, you can perfectly well build a ship!"

"And when you've been to Tabor Island," Harbert added, "you can perfectly well go to the Tuamotus!"

"I don't say no," replied Pencroff, who always had the deciding voice on matters of the sea. "I don't say no — though going near and going far aren't quite the same thing! If our boat had been threatened by some nasty gale on the trip to Tabor Island, we knew there was a port not too far off one way or the other. But twelve hundred miles to cover is a good long stretch, and the nearest land is at least that far away!"

"If it came to it, Pencroff, wouldn't you try the venture?" asked the reporter.

"I'll try anything anyone likes, Mr. Spilett," the sailor answered, "and you know very well I'm not a man to back down!"

"Remember, too, that there's one more sailor among us now," Nab pointed out.

"Who's that?" asked Pencroff.

"Ayrton."

"That's true," said Harbert.

"If he'd agree to come!" Pencroff observed.

"Come now!" said the reporter. "Do you really think that if Lord Glenarvan's yacht had turned up at Tabor Island while Ayrton was still living there, he would have refused to leave?"

"You're forgetting, my friends," Cyrus Smith said then, "that Ayrton was no longer in his right mind during the last years he spent there. But that isn't the question. What we need to decide is whether we should count the Scottish ship's return among our chances of rescue. Now, Lord Glenarvan promised Ayrton he would come back for him on Tabor Island once he judged that his crimes had been sufficiently paid for, and I believe he will come back."

"Yes," said the reporter, "and I'd add that he'll come back soon, because it's been twelve years since Ayrton was marooned!"

"Well!" answered Pencroff. "I quite agree with you that the lord will come back, and soon too. But where will he put in? At Tabor Island, not at Lincoln Island."

"That's all the more certain," Harbert replied, "because Lincoln Island isn't even on the map."

"So, my friends," the engineer went on, "we must take the necessary steps to make sure that our presence, and Ayrton's, on Lincoln Island is made known on Tabor Island."

"Obviously," the reporter answered, "and nothing could be easier than to leave a notice in that hut which was the home of Captain Grant and of Ayrton — a notice giving our island's position, which Lord Glenarvan or his crew can't fail to find."

"It's a pity, in fact," the sailor observed, "that we forgot to take that precaution on our first trip to Tabor Island."

"And why would we have taken it?" Harbert replied. "We didn't know Ayrton's story at that point. We had no idea that someone was supposed to come looking for him one day, and by the time we did know the story, the season was too far along to let us go back to Tabor Island."

"Yes," said Cyrus Smith, "it was too late, and the crossing must wait until next spring."

"But what if the Scottish yacht comes before then?" said Pencroff.

"That's not likely," answered the engineer, "because Lord Glenarvan wouldn't choose the winter season to venture into these distant seas. Either he has already come back to Tabor Island since Ayrton has been with us — that is, in the last five months — and left again, or he won't come until later, and there will be time enough, as soon as the first fine days of October arrive, to go to Tabor Island and leave a notice there."

"You have to admit," said Nab, "it would be very bad luck if the Duncan had turned up in these seas only in the last few months!"

"I hope that isn't the case," replied Cyrus Smith, "and that Heaven hasn't taken away the best chance we have left!"

"I think," the reporter remarked, "that in any case we'll know where we stand once we've been back to Tabor Island, because if the Scots have returned there, they're bound to have left some traces of their visit."

"That's obvious," answered the engineer. "So then, my friends, since we have this chance of getting home, let us wait patiently, and if it's taken from us, we'll see then what we ought to do."

"In any case," said Pencroff, "it's understood that if we do leave Lincoln Island one way or another, it won't be because we're unhappy here!"

"No, Pencroff," answered the engineer. "It will be because here we are far from everything a man should hold dearest in the world — his family, his friends, the country where he was born!"

With matters settled that way, there was no more talk of building a ship big enough to risk the voyage, either north to the island groups or west to New Zealand, and they went about nothing but their usual tasks in preparation for a third winter at Granite House.

It was also decided, however, that before the bad weather came the boat would be used for a voyage around the island. The full survey of the coasts was not yet finished. The colonists had only a rough idea of the shoreline to the west and the north, from the mouth of Fall River all the way to the Mandible Capes, and no better idea of the narrow bay that cut in between those capes like the jaws of a shark.

The plan for this excursion was put forward by Pencroff, and Cyrus Smith gave it his full approval, because he wanted to see that whole part of his domain for himself.

The weather was changeable at the time, but the barometer was not making any sudden swings, so they could count on weather they could handle. As it happened, during the first week of April, after a sharp drop of the barometer, its rise again was announced by a strong wind from the west that lasted five or six days. Then the needle of the instrument settled again at a height of twenty-nine and nine-tenths inches, and conditions seemed right for the exploration.

The day of departure was set for April 16, and the Bonadventure, moored at Balloon Harbor, was stocked with supplies for a voyage that might last some time.

Cyrus Smith told Ayrton about the planned expedition and invited him to take part. But Ayrton preferred to stay on land, so it was decided that he would come to Granite House while his companions were away. Master Jup was to keep him company, and raised no objection whatever.

On the morning of April 16 all the colonists, with Top, were on board. The wind was blowing from the southwest, a fine breeze, and on leaving Balloon Harbor the Bonadventure had to tack back and forth to reach Reptile End. Of the ninety miles that made up the island's perimeter, the south coast accounted for some twenty, from the harbor to the headland. Hence the need to make those twenty miles sailing as close to the wind as she could, since the wind was dead ahead.

It took no less than the whole day to reach the headland, because when the boat left harbor she found only two more hours of ebb tide and then, against her, six hours of flood tide, which were very hard to make headway against. So night had fallen by the time they rounded the headland.

Pencroff then suggested to the engineer that they keep going at low speed, with two reefs in the sail. But Cyrus Smith preferred to anchor a few cable lengths offshore, so as to see this part of the coast again by daylight. It was even agreed that, since the point was a careful survey of the island's shoreline, they would not sail at night, and that when evening came they would drop anchor close to land, as long as the weather allowed.

So the night was spent at anchor in the lee of the headland, and since the wind had died down along with the mist, nothing broke the silence. The passengers, except the sailor, may have slept a little less well aboard the Bonadventure than they would have in their rooms at Granite House — but sleep they did.

The next day, April 17, Pencroff got under way at first light. On a broad reach, with the wind on the port side, he was able to run very close along the west coast.

The colonists knew this wooded and magnificent coast, since they had already walked along its edge, and yet it roused all their admiration once again. They hugged the land as closely as they could, slowing down so as to take everything in, and only taking care not to strike the tree trunks that floated here and there. Several times they even dropped anchor, and Gédéon Spilett took photographs of that superb shoreline.

Toward noon the Bonadventure reached the mouth of Fall River. Beyond it, on the right bank, the trees appeared again, but more thinly scattered, and three miles farther on they formed nothing but isolated clumps among the western spurs of the mountain, whose barren backbone stretched all the way to the shore.

What a contrast between the southern and the northern parts of this coast! The one was as wooded and green as the other was harsh and wild! It looked like one of those "iron coasts," as they are called in some countries, and its tortured structure seemed to show that a true crystallization had suddenly taken place in basalt that was still boiling in the geological ages. It was a terrible-looking heap, which would have struck terror into the colonists at the outset if chance had cast them up on this part of the island! When they were on the summit of Mount Franklin, they had not been able to make out how deeply sinister this shore looked, because they had seen it from too high above. But seen from the sea, this coastline had a strangeness to it whose equal could perhaps be found in no other corner of the world.

The Bonadventure passed along this coast, keeping half a mile off. It was easy to see that it was made of blocks of every size, from twenty feet to three hundred feet high, and of every shape: cylindrical like towers, prismatic like steeples, pyramidal like obelisks, conical like factory chimneys. An ice field in the polar seas could not have been piled up more fantastically in its sublime horror! Here, bridges flung from one rock to the next; there, arches set out like those of a cathedral nave, so deep the eye could not reach their end; in one place, broad hollows whose vaulted roofs looked like great monuments; in another, a real crowd of points, of little pyramids, of spires, more than any Gothic cathedral has ever had. All the whims of nature, more varied even than those of the imagination, shaped this grandiose shoreline, which ran on for eight or nine miles.

Cyrus Smith and his companions gazed at it with a surprise that came close to stupefaction. But if they were struck dumb, Top was not shy about letting out barks that were echoed back a thousand times by the basalt wall. The engineer even noticed that these barks had something odd about them, like the ones the dog gave at the mouth of the well in Granite House.

"Let's go in closer," he said.

And the Bonadventure came and skimmed as near as she could to the rocks of the shore. Perhaps there was some cave there that ought to be explored? But Cyrus Smith saw nothing — not a cavern, not a crevice that could have served as a hiding place for any creature at all, for the foot of the rocks was washed by the surf itself. Soon Top stopped barking, and the boat drew off again to a few cable lengths from the shore.

In the northwestern part of the island the shore became flat and sandy once more. A few scattered trees stood out above a low, marshy stretch of land that the colonists had glimpsed before, and, in violent contrast with the other, so lifeless coast, life now showed itself in myriads of water birds.

That evening the Bonadventure anchored in a slight inlet of the shore on the north side of the island, close to land, since the water was so deep there. The night passed peacefully, because the breeze died away, so to speak, with the last glimmers of daylight, and rose again only with the first tints of dawn.

As it was easy to get ashore that morning, the colony's official hunters — that is, Harbert and Gédéon Spilett — went off for a two-hour walk and came back with several strings of ducks and snipe. Top had done wonders, and not a single bird had been lost, thanks to his zeal and skill.

At eight in the morning the Bonadventure got under way and ran along very fast, heading north toward North Mandible Cape, for the wind was right behind her and the breeze was tending to freshen.

"Anyhow," said Pencroff, "I wouldn't be surprised if some westerly gale was brewing. Yesterday the sun set on a very red horizon, and this morning here are 'cats' tails,' which mean no good."

These cats' tails were wispy cirrus clouds scattered across the zenith, and they are never less than five thousand feet above sea level. They looked like light tufts of cotton wool, and their presence usually announces some coming disturbance in the elements.

"Well then," said Cyrus Smith, "let's carry as much sail as we can and go and look for shelter in Shark Gulf. I think the Bonadventure will be safe there."

"Exactly," Pencroff answered, "and besides, the north coast is nothing but dunes, not very interesting to look at."

"I wouldn't be sorry," the engineer added, "to spend not only the night but tomorrow as well in that bay. It deserves to be explored carefully."

"I think we'll be forced to, whether we like it or not," answered Pencroff, "because the horizon's starting to look threatening out to the west. Look how dirty it's getting!"

"In any case, we have a good wind to reach Mandible Cape," the reporter remarked.

"A very good wind," replied the sailor. "But to get into the gulf we'll have to tack, and I'd very much like to be able to see clearly in waters I don't know!"

"Waters that must be strewn with reefs," Harbert added, "if we judge by what we've seen on the south shore of Shark Gulf."

"Pencroff," Cyrus Smith said then, "do whatever you think best. We leave it to you."

"Don't worry, Mr. Cyrus," the sailor answered. "I won't take any risks I don't have to! I'd sooner have a knife in my belly than a rock in my Bonadventure's!"

What Pencroff called the belly was the part of his boat's hull that lay underwater, and he cared more about it than about his own skin!

"What time is it?" asked Pencroff.

"Ten o'clock," Gédéon Spilett answered.

"And how far do we have to go to the cape, Mr. Cyrus?"

"About fifteen miles," the engineer replied.

"That's a matter of two and a half hours," said the sailor, "and we'll be abreast of the cape between noon and one o'clock. Unfortunately, the tide will turn just then, and the ebb will be running out of the gulf. So I'm very much afraid it'll be hard to get in, with both wind and sea against us."

"All the more so because it's full moon today," Harbert observed, "and these April tides are very strong."

"Well, Pencroff," asked Cyrus Smith, "can't you anchor off the tip of the cape?"

"Anchor close to land, with bad weather on the way!" cried the sailor. "Are you serious, Mr. Cyrus? That would be running ourselves aground on purpose!"

"Then what will you do?"

"I'll try to stay out at sea until the flood tide — that is, until seven o'clock this evening — and if there's still a little daylight, I'll try to get into the gulf. If not, we'll stay out tacking back and forth all night, and go in tomorrow at sunrise."

"As I told you, Pencroff, we leave it to you," replied Cyrus Smith.

"Ah!" said Pencroff. "If only there were a lighthouse on this coast, it'd make things easier for sailors!"

"Yes," answered Harbert, "and this time we won't have an obliging engineer to light a fire to guide us into port!"

"Why, that's right, my dear Cyrus," said Gédéon Spilett. "We've never thanked you. But honestly, without that fire we could never have reached..."

"A fire...?" asked Cyrus Smith, greatly astonished at the reporter's words.

"What we mean, Mr. Cyrus," answered Pencroff, "is that we were in real trouble aboard the Bonadventure during the last hours before we got back, and we'd have passed the island on its leeward side if you hadn't taken the precaution of lighting a fire on the night of October 19 to 20, on the plateau above Granite House."

"Yes, yes!... That was a lucky idea I had!" the engineer replied.

"And this time," the sailor added, "unless Ayrton thinks of it, there'll be nobody to do us that little favor!"

"No! Nobody!" answered Cyrus Smith.

And a few moments later, finding himself alone with the reporter in the bow of the boat, the engineer leaned close to his ear and said:

"If there is one thing certain in this world, Spilett, it is that I never lit a fire on the night of October 19 to 20 — not on the plateau above Granite House, and not anywhere else on the island!"
