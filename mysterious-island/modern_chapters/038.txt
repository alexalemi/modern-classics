Chapter 17: Abandoned on Tabor Island

_Still keeping apart — The stranger's request — The farm set up at the corral — Twelve years ago! — The quartermaster of the Britannia — Abandoned on Tabor Island — Cyrus Smith's hand — The mysterious document_

Those last words bore out what the colonists had sensed. There was some dark past in this unhappy man's life, atoned for perhaps in the eyes of men, but one his conscience had not yet absolved him of. In any case, the guilty man felt remorse, he repented, and his new friends would have shaken the hand they asked of him warmly — but he did not feel worthy to hold it out to honest people! Still, after the scene with the jaguar he did not go back to the forest, and from that day on he never again left the grounds of Granite House.

What was the mystery of this man's life? Would the stranger speak one day? Only the future would tell. In any case, they all agreed that his secret would never be asked of him, and that they would live with him as if they suspected nothing.

For some days, then, their life together went on as it had before. Cyrus Smith and Gédéon Spilett worked side by side, chemists one day, physicists the next. The reporter left the engineer only to go hunting with Harbert, for it would not have been wise to let the boy roam the forest alone, and they had to stay on their guard. As for Nab and Pencroff — one day at the stables or the poultry yard, another at the corral, not to mention the work at Granite House — they were never short of things to do.

The stranger worked apart from the others. He had gone back to his usual way of living: he did not come to meals, he slept under the trees of the plateau, he never mixed with his companions. It really seemed as though the company of the men who had saved him was more than he could bear!

"But then," Pencroff would point out, "why did he ask other people for help? Why did he throw that document into the sea?"

"He'll tell us," Cyrus Smith always answered.

"When?"

"Sooner, perhaps, than you think, Pencroff."

And in fact the day of confession was near.

On December 10, a week after his return to Granite House, Cyrus Smith saw the stranger coming toward him. In a calm voice and a humble tone, the man said:

"Sir, I have something to ask of you."

"Go ahead," replied the engineer. "But first, let me ask you something."

At these words the stranger flushed and was on the point of turning away. Cyrus Smith understood what was going on in the guilty man's soul: no doubt he was afraid the engineer meant to question him about his past!

Cyrus Smith held him back with his hand.

"Comrade," he said, "we're not just your companions here. We're your friends. I wanted to tell you that. And now I'm listening."

The stranger passed his hand over his eyes. A kind of trembling had come over him, and for some moments he could not get a word out.

"Sir," he said at last, "I've come to ask you a favor."

"What is it?"

"Four or five miles from here, at the foot of the mountain, you have a corral for your livestock. Those animals need looking after. Will you let me live out there with them?"

For some moments Cyrus Smith looked at the unfortunate man with deep compassion. Then:

"My friend," he said, "the corral has nothing but stables, hardly fit for the animals..."

"It will be good enough for me, sir."

"My friend," Cyrus Smith went on, "we'll never stand in your way in anything. You'd like to live at the corral. Very well. You'll always be welcome at Granite House, of course. But since you want to live at the corral, we'll make the arrangements needed for you to be properly settled there."

"Any way at all, I'll be fine there."

"My friend," answered Cyrus Smith, who was leaning on that warm word on purpose, "you'll let us be the judges of what we ought to do about that!"

"Thank you, sir," the stranger answered, and he withdrew.

The engineer at once told his companions about the request that had been made to him, and they decided to build a wooden house at the corral and make it as comfortable as they could.

That very day the colonists went to the corral with the tools they would need, and before the week was out the house was ready to receive its occupant. It had been put up about twenty feet from the stables, and from there it would be easy to keep an eye on the flock of mouflons, which by then numbered more than eighty head. A few pieces of furniture were made — a bunk, a table, a bench, a cupboard, a chest — and weapons, ammunition and tools were carried out to the corral.

The stranger, for his part, had not gone to look at his new home. He had let the colonists work on it without him while he kept busy on the plateau, no doubt wanting to put the finishing touches to his own task. And in fact, thanks to him, all the fields had been plowed and were ready to be sown as soon as the time came.

The house at the corral was finished on December 20. The engineer told the stranger that his new home was ready for him, and the man replied that he would go and sleep there that very evening.

That evening the colonists were gathered in the great hall of Granite House. It was eight o'clock — the hour when their companion was to leave them. Not wanting to embarrass him by forcing on him, with their presence, a farewell that might have cost him dearly, they had left him alone and gone back up to Granite House.

They had been talking in the great hall for a few minutes when there came a light knock at the door. Almost at once the stranger came in, and without any preamble he said:

"Gentlemen, before I leave you, it's right that you should know my story. Here it is."

These simple words made a very deep impression on Cyrus Smith and his companions.

The engineer had risen to his feet.

"We ask nothing of you, my friend," he said. "It's your right to keep silent..."

"It's my duty to speak."

"Then sit down."

"I'll stay standing."

"We're ready to hear you," replied Cyrus Smith.

The stranger stood in a corner of the room, half sheltered by the shadows. He was bareheaded, his arms folded across his chest, and it was in that posture that, in a low, muffled voice, speaking like someone who is forcing himself to speak, he told the following story. His listeners did not interrupt him once.

"On December 20, 1854, a steam yacht, the Duncan, belonging to a Scottish laird, Lord Glenarvan, dropped anchor off Cape Bernouilli, on the west coast of Australia, at the latitude of the thirty-seventh parallel. On board the yacht were Lord Glenarvan, his wife, a major in the British army, a French geographer, a girl and a boy. These last two were the children of Captain Grant, whose ship, the Britannia, had been lost with all hands a year before. The Duncan was commanded by Captain John Mangles and manned by a crew of fifteen.

"This is why the yacht was off the coast of Australia at that time.

"Six months earlier, a bottle holding a document written in English, German and French had been found in the Irish Sea, picked up by the Duncan. The gist of the document was that there were still three survivors of the wreck of the Britannia — Captain Grant and two of his men — and that they had found refuge on a piece of land. The document gave its latitude, but the longitude had been washed out by the sea water and could no longer be read.

"That latitude was 37° 11′ south. So, with the longitude unknown, anyone who followed the thirty-seventh parallel across continents and seas was certain to reach the land where Captain Grant and his two companions were living.

"The British Admiralty hesitated to undertake the search, so Lord Glenarvan resolved to try everything to find the captain. Mary and Robert Grant had been put in touch with him. The Duncan was fitted out for a long voyage, and Lord Glenarvan's family and the captain's children wanted to take part in it. Leaving Glasgow, the Duncan headed into the Atlantic, rounded the Strait of Magellan, and sailed up the Pacific as far as Patagonia, where, by a first reading of the document, it could be supposed that Captain Grant was a prisoner of the native people.

"The Duncan landed her passengers on the west coast of Patagonia and sailed off to pick them up again on the east coast, at Cape Corrientes.

"Lord Glenarvan crossed Patagonia, following the thirty-seventh parallel, and, having found no trace of the captain, he took ship again on November 13 to carry on his search across the ocean.

"After visiting the islands of Tristan da Cunha and Amsterdam, which lay on his course, without success, the Duncan arrived, as I said, at Cape Bernouilli on the Australian coast on December 20, 1854.

"Lord Glenarvan meant to cross Australia as he had crossed America, and he went ashore. A few miles from the coast there was a farm belonging to an Irishman, who offered the travelers his hospitality. Lord Glenarvan told the Irishman the reasons that had brought him to those parts, and asked him whether he knew of an English three-master, the Britannia, being lost on the west coast of Australia within the last two years.

"The Irishman had never heard of any such wreck. But, to the great surprise of everyone there, one of the Irishman's farmhands broke in and said:

"'My lord, praise God and thank Him. If Captain Grant is still alive, he is alive on Australian soil.'

"'Who are you?' asked Lord Glenarvan.

"'A Scot like yourself, my lord,' the man answered, 'and I'm one of Captain Grant's companions — one of the castaways of the Britannia.'

"This man's name was Ayrton. He was indeed the quartermaster of the Britannia, as his papers showed. But he had been separated from Captain Grant at the moment the ship broke up on the reefs, and until then he had believed that his captain had died with the whole crew, and that he, Ayrton, was the only survivor of the Britannia.

"'Only,' he added, 'it wasn't on the west coast but on the east coast of Australia that the Britannia was lost. And if Captain Grant is still alive, as his document says, he's a prisoner of the Aboriginal people, and it's on the other coast that you have to look for him.'

"As he said this, the man's voice was frank and his gaze was steady. No one could doubt what he said. The Irishman, who had employed him for more than a year, vouched for him. Lord Glenarvan believed in the man's honesty, and on his advice he decided to cross Australia by following the thirty-seventh parallel. Lord Glenarvan, his wife, the two children, the major, the Frenchman, Captain Mangles and a few sailors would make up the little party, with Ayrton as their guide, while the Duncan, under the command of her first mate, Tom Austin, would go on to Melbourne and wait there for Lord Glenarvan's instructions.

"They set off on December 23, 1854.

"It is time to say that this Ayrton was a traitor. He had indeed been the quartermaster of the Britannia. But after quarrels with his captain he had tried to stir up the crew to mutiny and seize the ship, and on April 8, 1852, Captain Grant had put him ashore on the west coast of Australia and sailed off and left him there — which was no more than justice.

"So this wretch knew nothing of the wreck of the Britannia. He had only just learned of it from Glenarvan's account! Since he had been marooned, he had become, under the name of Ben Joyce, the leader of a gang of escaped convicts. And if he insisted so brazenly that the wreck had happened on the east coast, if he urged Lord Glenarvan to set off in that direction, it was because he hoped to separate him from his ship, seize the Duncan, and turn the yacht into a pirate ship of the Pacific."

Here the stranger broke off for a moment. His voice was trembling, but he went on in these words:

"The expedition set off and made its way across Australia. Naturally it was an unlucky one, since Ayrton — or Ben Joyce, whichever you like to call him — was leading it, with his band of convicts sometimes going on ahead of it, sometimes following behind, for they had been told of the blow that was to be struck.

"Meanwhile the Duncan had been sent to Melbourne for repairs. The thing, then, was to get Lord Glenarvan to order her to leave Melbourne and sail to the east coast of Australia, where it would be easy to seize her. Ayrton led the expedition fairly close to that coast, into the middle of vast forests where there was nothing at all to live on. Then he got himself a letter, which he had taken it upon himself to carry to the Duncan's first mate — a letter ordering the yacht to sail at once to the east coast, to Twofold Bay, a few days' journey from where the expedition had halted. That was where Ayrton had arranged to meet his accomplices.

"Just as the letter was about to be handed to him, the traitor was unmasked, and there was nothing left for him but to run. But that letter, which was to deliver the Duncan into his hands, he had to have at any cost. Ayrton managed to get hold of it, and two days later he reached Melbourne.

"Until then the criminal had succeeded in his vile plans. He would be able to bring the Duncan to Twofold Bay, where it would be easy for the convicts to take her, and once her crew had been massacred, Ben Joyce would be master of those seas... God was to stop him just as his evil designs were coming to their end.

"Ayrton reached Melbourne and handed the letter to the first mate, Tom Austin, who read it and set sail at once. But imagine Ayrton's disappointment and rage when, the day after they sailed, he learned that the mate was taking the ship not to the east coast of Australia, to Twofold Bay, but to the east coast of New Zealand! He tried to stop it. Austin showed him the letter!... And in fact, by a providential mistake on the part of the French geographer who had written that letter, the east coast of New Zealand was the destination it named.

"All of Ayrton's plans were falling apart! He tried to stir up a mutiny. They locked him up. And so he was carried off to the coast of New Zealand, with no idea anymore what would become of his accomplices, or what would become of Lord Glenarvan.

"The Duncan stayed cruising off that coast until March 3. That day Ayrton heard the boom of guns. It was the Duncan's carronades firing, and soon Lord Glenarvan and all his party came on board.

"Here is what had happened.

"After a thousand hardships and a thousand dangers, Lord Glenarvan had managed to finish his journey and reach the east coast of Australia, at Twofold Bay. No Duncan! He telegraphed to Melbourne. The answer came back: 'Duncan sailed on the 18th of this month for unknown destination.'

"Lord Glenarvan could think only one thing: that the honest yacht had fallen into the hands of Ben Joyce and had become a pirate ship!

"Even so, Lord Glenarvan would not give up. He was a fearless and generous man. He took passage on a merchant ship, had himself carried to the west coast of New Zealand, and crossed it along the thirty-seventh parallel without finding any trace of Captain Grant. But on the other coast, to his great surprise, and by the will of Heaven, he found the Duncan again, under the command of the first mate, who had been waiting for him for five weeks!

"It was March 3, 1855. Lord Glenarvan was aboard the Duncan, then, but so was Ayrton. He was brought before Lord Glenarvan, who wanted to get out of him everything the bandit might know about Captain Grant. Ayrton refused to speak. Lord Glenarvan then told him that at the first port they put in to, he would be handed over to the British authorities. Ayrton said nothing.

"The Duncan took up her course along the thirty-seventh parallel again. Meanwhile Lady Glenarvan set herself to break down the bandit's resistance. At last her influence won out, and Ayrton, in exchange for whatever he could tell, proposed that Lord Glenarvan maroon him on one of the islands of the Pacific instead of handing him over to the British authorities. Lord Glenarvan, determined to do anything to learn what had become of Captain Grant, agreed.

"Ayrton then told the whole story of his life, and it was established that he knew nothing at all after the day Captain Grant had put him ashore on the Australian coast.

"Nevertheless, Lord Glenarvan kept the promise he had made. The Duncan went on her way and came to Tabor Island. That was where Ayrton was to be left — and it was there too that, by a real miracle, they found Captain Grant and his two men, on that very thirty-seventh parallel. So the convict was to take their place on that deserted islet, and these are the words Lord Glenarvan spoke as Ayrton left the yacht:

"'Here, Ayrton, you will be far from any land, with no possible way of reaching other men. You will not be able to escape from this islet where the Duncan leaves you. You will be alone, under the eye of a God who reads the very depths of men's hearts. But you will not be lost, or forgotten, as Captain Grant was. Unworthy as you are to be remembered by men, men will remember you. I know where you are, Ayrton, and I know where to find you. I will never forget it!'

"And the Duncan set sail and was soon gone from sight.

"It was March 18, 1855.

"Ayrton was alone, but he had no shortage of ammunition, weapons, tools or seeds. At his disposal — his, the convict's — was the house built by honest Captain Grant. He had only to let himself live, and to atone in solitude for the crimes he had committed.

"Gentlemen, he repented. He was ashamed of his crimes, and he was very unhappy! He told himself that if men came back one day to look for him on that islet, he must be worthy of going back among them! How he suffered, the wretch! How he worked to remake himself by work! How he prayed to be made new by prayer!

"For two years, three years, it went on that way. But Ayrton, worn down by solitude, always watching for some ship to appear on the horizon of his island, always asking himself whether the time of his atonement would soon be over, suffered as no one has ever suffered! Ah, how hard that solitude is for a soul eaten away by remorse!

"But no doubt Heaven did not think him punished enough, the unhappy man, for little by little he felt himself becoming a wild man! Little by little he felt himself sinking to the level of a brute! He cannot tell you whether it was after two years of being abandoned or four, but in the end he became the wretched creature you found!

"I don't need to tell you, gentlemen, that Ayrton, or Ben Joyce, and I are one and the same!"

Cyrus Smith and his companions had risen to their feet when the story ended. It is hard to say how deeply they were moved! So much misery, so much pain and despair laid bare in front of them!

"Ayrton," Cyrus Smith said then, "you were a great criminal, but Heaven must surely find that you've atoned for your crimes! It has proved it by bringing you back among your fellow men. Ayrton, you are forgiven! And now, will you be our companion?"

Ayrton had stepped back.

"Here is my hand!" said the engineer.

Ayrton rushed to take the hand Cyrus Smith held out to him, and great tears ran from his eyes.

"Will you live with us?" asked Cyrus Smith.

"Mr. Smith, give me a little more time," Ayrton answered. "Let me stay alone in the house at the corral!"

"As you wish, Ayrton," replied Cyrus Smith.

Ayrton was about to leave when the engineer put one last question to him.

"One more word, my friend. If you meant to live alone, why did you throw into the sea the document that put us on your track?"

"A document?" answered Ayrton, who seemed not to know what he was being asked about.

"Yes, the document sealed in a bottle that we found, which gave the exact position of Tabor Island!"

Ayrton passed his hand across his forehead. Then, after thinking:

"I never threw any document into the sea!" he answered.

"Never?" cried Pencroff.

"Never!"

And Ayrton bowed, went back to the door, and was gone.
