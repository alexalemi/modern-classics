Chapter 2: The Chest on the Beach

_First trial of the canoe — Wreckage on the shore — The tow — Jetsam Point — Inventory of the chest: tools, weapons, instruments, clothing, books, utensils — What Pencroff misses — The Gospel — A verse from the holy book_

By October 29 the bark canoe was entirely finished. Pencroff had kept his promise, and in five days a sort of canoe had been built, its hull ribbed with flexible rods of crejimba wood. A bench in the stern, a second bench amidships to hold the sides apart, a third bench in the bow, a gunwale to carry the tholepins for two oars, and a sculling oar for steering made up the whole of the little boat, which was twelve feet long and weighed under two hundred pounds. As for the launching, it was extremely simple. The light canoe was carried down onto the sand at the water's edge in front of Granite House, and the rising tide lifted it. Pencroff jumped straight in, worked it along with the sculling oar, and was able to confirm that it was entirely fit for the use they meant to make of it.

"Hurrah!" cried the sailor, who did not scorn to celebrate his own triumph that way. "With this you could go all the way around..."

"The world?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

"No, the island. A few stones for ballast, a mast up in the bow, and a scrap of sail that Mr. Smith will make us one of these days, and we'll go a long way! Well, Mr. Cyrus, and you, Mr. Spilett, and you, Harbert, and you, Nab, aren't you coming to try out our new ship? What the devil! We've got to see whether she can carry all five of us!"

It was, in fact, a test worth making. With a stroke of the sculling oar Pencroff brought the boat back to the shore through a narrow passage between the rocks, and it was agreed that they would try out the canoe that very day by following the shore as far as the first point, where the rocks of the south ended.

Just as they were getting in, Nab exclaimed:

"But your ship's taking on quite a bit of water, Pencroff!"

"That's nothing, Nab," replied the sailor. "The wood has to swell tight! In two days you won't see a drop, and our canoe will have no more water in her belly than a drunkard has in his stomach. All aboard!"

So they all got in, and Pencroff pushed off. The weather was magnificent, and the sea as calm as if its waters had been held within the narrow shores of a lake. The canoe could face it as safely as if it had been going up the quiet current of the Mercy.

Nab took one of the two oars and Harbert the other, while Pencroff stayed in the stern to steer with the sculling oar.

The sailor first crossed the channel and ran close along the southern tip of the islet. A light breeze was blowing from the south. There was no swell, either in the channel or out at sea. A few long undulations, which the canoe hardly felt because it was heavily loaded, swelled the surface of the sea at regular intervals. They went out about half a mile from the coast, far enough to take in the whole sweep of Mount Franklin.

Then Pencroff came about and headed back toward the mouth of the river. The canoe now followed the shore, which curved round to the farthest point of land and hid the whole low, marshy plain of Shelduck Marsh.

Measured around the curve of the coast, that point was about three miles from the Mercy. The colonists decided to go as far as its tip and to round it only far enough to get a quick look at the coast as far as Claw Cape.

So the boat followed the shoreline two cable lengths out at most, steering clear of the reefs scattered all along that stretch of coast, which the rising tide was beginning to cover. The cliff kept getting lower from the river mouth to the point. It was a heap of granite blocks flung about at random, very different from the great wall that formed Prospect Plateau, and extremely wild to look at. It was as though an enormous cartload of rocks had been dumped there. Not a scrap of vegetation grew on that sharp spur, which ran out two miles in front of the forest, and the point looked rather like a giant's arm reaching out of a green sleeve.

Driven by its two oars, the boat moved along easily. Gédéon Spilett, a pencil in one hand and his notebook in the other, sketched the coast in broad strokes. Nab, Pencroff, and Harbert chatted as they looked over this part of their domain, which was new to them, and as the canoe went farther south the two Mandible Capes seemed to shift their places and close Union Bay in more tightly.

As for Cyrus Smith, he did not talk. He watched. And from the wariness in his eyes it always seemed as though he were studying some strange country.

Meanwhile, after three quarters of an hour on the water, the canoe had nearly reached the tip of the point, and Pencroff was getting ready to round it, when Harbert stood up and pointed to a black spot.

"What's that I see over there on the beach?" he said.

Every eye turned toward the place he pointed to.

"There's something there, all right," said the reporter. "It looks like a piece of wreckage half buried in the sand."

"Ah!" cried Pencroff. "I see what it is!"

"What?" asked Nab.

"Barrels! Barrels — and they may be full!" answered the sailor.

"To the shore, Pencroff!" said Cyrus Smith.

A few strokes of the oars, and the canoe grounded at the head of a little cove. Its passengers jumped out onto the beach.

Pencroff had not been mistaken. Two barrels lay there, half sunk in the sand, but still firmly lashed to a large chest. Held up by them, the chest had floated along until it ran aground on the shore.

"So there's been a shipwreck somewhere near the island?" asked Harbert.

"Obviously," replied Gédéon Spilett.

"But what's in this chest?" cried Pencroff, with an impatience that was only natural. "What's in this chest? It's shut tight, and nothing to break the lid open with! Well, then, we'll do it with stones..."

And the sailor, lifting a heavy rock, was about to smash in one side of the chest when the engineer stopped him.

"Pencroff," he said, "can you hold back your impatience for just one hour?"

"But, Mr. Cyrus, think of it! Maybe everything we're missing is in there!"

"We'll find out, Pencroff," replied the engineer. "But take my word for it, don't smash this chest. It may be useful to us. Let's take it to Granite House, where we can open it more easily without breaking it. It's all fitted out for the trip. Since it floated this far, it will surely float as far as the mouth of the river."

"You're right, Mr. Cyrus, and I was wrong," replied the sailor. "But a man isn't always master of himself!"

The engineer's advice was sound. For one thing, the canoe could not have held whatever the chest probably contained. It must be heavy, since it had needed two empty barrels to "buoy it up," as sailors say. So it was better to tow it just as it was to the shore below Granite House.

And now, where had this wreckage come from? That was an important question. Cyrus Smith and his companions looked carefully all around them and walked along the shore for several hundred paces. No other debris turned up. They searched the sea as well. Harbert and Nab climbed a tall rock, but the horizon was empty. Nothing in sight — neither a disabled ship nor a vessel under sail.

Still, there had been a shipwreck. There was no doubt about that. Could this incident be connected with the incident of the lead pellet? Had strangers perhaps landed somewhere else on the island? Could they still be there? But the colonists naturally reflected that these strangers could not be Malay pirates, because the wreckage had obviously come from either America or Europe.

They all went back to the chest. It measured five feet long by three feet wide. It was made of oak, very carefully closed, and covered with a thick hide held in place with copper nails. The two big casks, tightly sealed but clearly empty from the sound they gave when struck, were bound to its sides with stout ropes, tied in knots that Pencroff easily recognized as "sailor's knots." The chest seemed to be perfectly preserved, which was explained by the fact that it had run aground on a sandy beach and not on reefs. Looking it over carefully, one could even be sure that it had not been in the sea for long, and that it had only recently arrived on this shore. No water seemed to have got inside, and the things it held must be undamaged.

It was clear that the chest had been thrown overboard from a disabled ship running toward the island. In the hope that it would reach the coast, where they could find it again later, some of the people on board had taken the precaution of lightening it with a float.

"We'll tow this wreckage to Granite House," said the engineer, "and we'll make an inventory of what's in it. Then, if we find any survivors of this shipwreck on the island — supposing there was one — we'll give it back to its owners. If we don't find anyone..."

"We'll keep it for ourselves!" cried Pencroff. "But, for God's sake, what on earth can be in there?"

The tide was already starting to reach the wreckage, which would certainly float at high water. One of the ropes holding the barrels was partly unwound and used as a towline to tie the float to the boat. Then Pencroff and Nab dug away the sand with their oars to make it easier to move the chest. Soon the boat, towing the chest behind it, began to round the point, which was given the name Jetsam Point. The tow was heavy, and the barrels were barely enough to hold the chest above the water. So the sailor feared at every moment that it would break loose and sink to the bottom. Fortunately his fears did not come true, and an hour and a half after setting out — it had taken all that time to cover the three miles — the canoe came alongside the shore in front of Granite House.

Canoe and wreckage were then hauled up onto the sand, and since the sea was already going out, they were soon left high and dry. Nab had gone to fetch tools to force the chest open while damaging it as little as possible, and they set about the inventory. Pencroff made no attempt to hide that he was extremely excited.

The sailor began by untying the two barrels, which were in very good shape and could be put to use, it goes without saying. Then the locks were forced with a crowbar, and the lid fell open at once.

A second casing, of zinc, lined the inside of the chest. Plainly it had been arranged so that the things it held would be safe from damp under any circumstances.

"Ah!" cried Nab. "What if it's canned food in there!"

"I certainly hope not," replied the reporter.

"If only there were..." said the sailor under his breath.

"Were what?" asked Nab, who had heard him.

"Nothing!"

The zinc casing was cut open across its whole width and folded back over the sides of the chest, and little by little all sorts of very different things were taken out and set down on the sand. At each new object Pencroff let out new hurrahs, Harbert clapped his hands, and Nab danced for joy. There were books in there that would have driven Harbert wild with delight, and cooking utensils that Nab would have covered with kisses!

All in all, the colonists had every reason to be extremely pleased, for the chest held tools, weapons, instruments, clothing, and books. Here is the exact list, as Gédéon Spilett entered it in his notebook:

    Tools:        3 knives with several blades
                  2 woodcutter's axes
                  2 carpenter's hatchets
                  3 planes
                  2 adzes
                  1 twibill (a double-bladed mortising axe)
                  6 cold chisels
                  2 files
                  3 hammers
                  3 gimlets
                  2 augers
                  10 bags of nails and screws
                  3 saws of different sizes
                  2 boxes of needles

    Weapons:      2 flintlock guns
                  2 percussion-cap guns
                  2 center-fire carbines
                  5 cutlasses
                  4 boarding sabers
                  2 kegs of gunpowder, holding twenty-five pounds each
                  12 boxes of percussion caps

    Instruments:  1 sextant
                  1 pair of binoculars
                  1 telescope
                  1 case of drawing compasses
                  1 pocket compass
                  1 Fahrenheit thermometer
                  1 aneroid barometer
                  1 box holding a complete photographic outfit: lens,
                    plates, chemicals, etc.

    Clothing:     2 dozen shirts of an unusual fabric that looked like
                    wool but was plainly of vegetable origin
                  3 dozen stockings of the same fabric

    Utensils:     1 iron kettle
                  6 tin-lined copper saucepans
                  3 iron platters
                  10 sets of aluminum forks and spoons
                  2 teakettles
                  1 small portable stove
                  6 table knives

    Books:        1 Bible with the Old and New Testaments
                  1 atlas
                  1 dictionary of the various Polynesian languages
                  1 dictionary of natural science, in six volumes
                  3 reams of blank paper
                  2 ledgers with blank pages

"You have to admit," said the reporter, once the inventory was finished, "that the owner of this chest was a practical man! Tools, weapons, instruments, clothes, utensils, books — nothing's missing! You'd really think he'd been expecting to be shipwrecked and had got ready for it ahead of time!"

"Nothing's missing, indeed," murmured Cyrus Smith thoughtfully.

"And one thing's certain," added Harbert. "The ship that carried this chest and its owner wasn't a Malay pirate!"

"Unless," said Pencroff, "the owner had been taken prisoner by pirates..."

"That won't do," replied the reporter. "It's more likely that an American or European ship was driven into these waters, and that some passengers, wanting to save at least the bare necessities, packed this chest this way and threw it into the sea."

"Do you think so, Mr. Cyrus?" asked Harbert.

"Yes, my boy," replied the engineer. "That could be how it happened. It's possible that at the moment of a shipwreck, or in expectation of one, someone gathered various essential things into this chest, hoping to find them again somewhere on the coast..."

"Even the photography box!" the sailor remarked, looking rather unconvinced.

"As for that apparatus," replied Cyrus Smith, "I don't really see the use of it. A fuller supply of clothing or more ammunition would have served us better — or any other castaways, for that matter!"

"But isn't there some mark on these instruments, these tools, these books — some address — that could tell us where they came from?" asked Gédéon Spilett.

That remained to be seen. So every object was carefully examined, especially the books, the instruments, and the weapons. Contrary to the usual practice, neither the weapons nor the instruments bore a maker's mark. Besides, they were in perfect condition and did not seem ever to have been used. The same was true of the tools and the utensils: everything was new. That proved, in short, that these things had not been grabbed at random and tossed into the chest. On the contrary, they had been chosen with forethought and packed with care. The second casing of metal pointed the same way. It had kept them safe from all damp, and it could never have been soldered in a moment of haste.

As for the dictionaries of natural science and of the Polynesian languages, both were in English, but neither carried a publisher's name or a date of publication.

The same went for the Bible, printed in English — a quarto volume, remarkable as a piece of printing, which seemed to have been leafed through often.

As for the atlas, it was a magnificent work, with maps of the whole world and several maps of the globe drawn on Mercator's projection. Its place names were in French — but it too carried neither a date of publication nor a publisher's name.

So there was nothing on any of these things to show where they had come from, and therefore nothing to suggest the nationality of the ship that must have passed through these waters not long before. But wherever the chest had come from, it made the colonists of Lincoln Island rich. Until then they had made everything themselves by transforming the raw products of nature, and they had pulled through by their own intelligence. But didn't it seem as though Providence had wanted to reward them by sending them, just now, these products of human industry? And so, with one accord, they lifted up their thanks to Heaven.

Still, one of them was not entirely satisfied. That was Pencroff. It appeared that the chest did not contain something he cared about enormously. As the objects came out of it, his hurrahs grew fainter and fainter, and once the inventory was done he was heard to mutter:

"That's all fine and good, but you'll see — there won't be anything in this box for me!"

Which led Nab to say to him:

"Well now, friend Pencroff, what were you expecting?"

"Half a pound of tobacco!" Pencroff replied in all seriousness. "Then my happiness would have been complete!"

Nobody could help laughing at the sailor's remark.

But one consequence of finding the wreckage was that a serious exploration of the island was now more necessary than ever. So it was agreed that the next day, at first light, they would set out up the Mercy and make their way to the west coast. If any castaways had landed somewhere on that coast, they might well be without any resources, and help had to be brought to them without delay.

During the rest of that day the various objects were carried up to Granite House and arranged in an orderly way in the great hall.

That day — October 29 — happened to be a Sunday, and before going to bed Harbert asked the engineer whether he would read them some passage from the Gospel.

"Gladly," replied Cyrus Smith.

He took up the holy book and was about to open it when Pencroff stopped him.

"Mr. Cyrus," he said, "I'm superstitious. Open it at random and read us the first verse your eyes fall on. We'll see if it fits our situation."

Cyrus Smith smiled at the sailor's notion, and to humor him he opened the Gospel exactly where a bookmark lay between the pages.

At once his eyes were caught by a red cross, made in pencil, that had been placed beside verse 8 of chapter 7 of the Gospel of Saint Matthew.

And he read the verse, which went like this:

    Everyone who asks receives, and whoever seeks finds.
