Lucius Annaeus Seneca was born in Spain around the end of the first century BC, made his career at Rome as a writer and a senator, was banished to Corsica by one emperor and recalled to tutor the next. That next one was Nero, and for the first years of the reign Seneca effectively ran the government with him. He withdrew from court around 62, having acquired one of the largest fortunes in the empire. Three years later Nero ordered him to kill himself on a charge of conspiracy, and he did.

The letters were written in those last years, to Lucilius, a friend who was governing Sicily. There are a hundred and twenty-four of them and this edition has all of them, where most editions print a selection of twenty or thirty. They amount to a course in Stoic practice delivered a letter at a time, and the form is the reason they are still readable. Each one starts from something that actually happened — the mail ships from Alexandria coming into the bay, a rented room over a public bathhouse where somebody is being plucked, a bad journey, an illness, a walk round a villa gone to seed — and then turns. The early ones close by handing Lucilius a quotation for the day, usually from Epicurus, whose school was the Stoics' rival, and Seneca keeps up a running joke about borrowing from the other side.

The obvious objection to the man is one he raises himself. Someone writing about the contempt of money while owning gardens, estates and a fortune is open to a charge, and he answers it directly: he is not a wise man yet, he is not setting up as a doctor while sick himself, and the two of them are lying in the same hospital ward and passing the medicine across. How well that answers is left to the reader here, as it was there.

Letter 47 is the one most often anthologised and deserves its place. Seneca tells Lucilius that the slaves at his table are men, that the master who cannot be spoken to freely by his own household is the one living in fear, and that fortune has the same power over both of them. He owned slaves. Letters 70 and 77 argue that a person may rightly choose the time of their own death, which was orthodox Stoic doctrine and which he acted on; they are given here as the argument he made, without comment.

Of the three Stoics whose work survives in quantity, one was born a slave, one was an emperor, and this one was a minister of state with a great deal to lose. Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius are both in this collection, and Seneca is the only one of the three writing to somebody else.
