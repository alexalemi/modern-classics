Chapter 14: Degrees of Wrong

A wrong is greater the greater the injustice it comes from. That is why the smallest wrongs are sometimes the greatest. Take the charge that the orator Callistratus brought against his rival Melanopus: that he had cheated the temple-builders, the officials in charge of building the temples, out of three sacred half-obols, a trifling sum of coins. With just acts it is the other way round. The reason is that the greater wrong is there in the smaller one as a possibility: a man who would steal three sacred half-obols would do any wrong at all. So sometimes a wrong is judged greater in this way, and sometimes by the size of the harm it does.

A wrong is also greater when there is no punishment equal to it, and every punishment falls short. It is greater when there is no remedy for it, because a remedy is hard, or impossible. It is greater when the victim cannot get justice through the courts, since then it cannot be put right; for a trial and a punishment are themselves a kind of remedy.

It is greater if the victim, after being wronged, has punished himself severely, since then the man who did it deserves an even greater punishment. So when the orator Sophocles (not the poet) spoke on behalf of Euctemon, who had cut his own throat after the outrage done to him, he said he would not set the penalty any lower than the victim had set it for himself.

It is greater if a man is the only one to have done it, or the first, or one of only a few. It is a great wrong, too, to commit the same offence many times. And a wrong is great if it has made people look for and find new ways to prevent and to punish it. At Argos, for example, a man is punished if a new law had to be made because of him, and so are the men on whose account the prison had to be built.

A more brutal wrong is greater. So is one planned in advance. So is one that fills the people who hear of it with fear rather than with pity. And there are rhetorical points of this sort to make: that the accused has swept aside or broken many things at once, such as oaths, handshakes, pledges of good faith, and the rights of marriage between two peoples. For that is a heap of many wrongs on top of one another.

A wrong is greater if it is done in the very place where wrongdoers are punished, which is what false witnesses do: where would a man not do wrong, if he will do it even in a court of law? It is greater when it carries the most shame. It is greater if it is done to someone who has done the wrongdoer a good turn, since then he does more than one wrong: he does harm, and he fails to do good in return.

A wrong is greater if it breaks the unwritten rules of justice, for it is the better man who is just without being forced to be, and the written laws use force while the unwritten ones do not. Looked at another way, it is greater if it breaks the written laws, since a man who will do the wrongs that are frightening and carry penalties will certainly do the ones that carry none.

So much, then, for greater and lesser wrongs.
