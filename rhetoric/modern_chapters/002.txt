Chapter 3: The Three Kinds of Speech

There are three kinds of rhetoric, neither more nor less, because there are three kinds of people who listen to speeches. Every speech is made up of three things: the speaker, the subject he speaks about, and the person he speaks to. And the purpose of the speech is directed at this last one, I mean the listener.

The listener must be either a spectator or a judge, and if a judge, then a judge either of what has happened or of what is going to happen. The member of the assembly judges what is going to happen; the juror judges what has happened; and the spectator judges the speaker's skill.

So there must be three kinds of rhetorical speech: political speech, the kind usually called deliberative, which is made to the assembly and advises for or against; courtroom speech; and ceremonial speech, the speech made for an occasion or a show, which praises or blames.

Advice either urges people to do something or urges them not to. Everyone who gives advice, whether privately to one person or publicly to the people in the assembly, always does one or the other. A lawsuit is either an accusation or a defence, since the people on either side of a dispute must do one or the other. Ceremonial speech is either praise or blame.

Each of these has its own time. For the person giving political advice it is the future: whether he urges or dissuades, he advises about things that are still to come. For the person in a lawsuit it is the past, since it is always about things that have been done that one side accuses and the other defends. For ceremonial speech the chief time is the present, since everyone who praises or blames does so on the basis of how things stand now; but speakers of this kind also often make use of the past, by reminding the audience of it, and of the future, by guessing at it in advance.

Each of these kinds also has a different goal, and since there are three kinds there are three goals. For the person giving political advice, the goal is what is advantageous or harmful. The one who urges a course of action recommends it as better, and the one who dissuades warns against it as worse, and he brings in everything else as an extra that bears on this: whether the course is just or unjust, noble or shameful. For people in a lawsuit, the goal is what is just and unjust, and they too bring in everything else as an extra that bears on it. For people who praise and blame, the goal is what is noble and what is shameful, and they too refer everything else back to that.

Here is a sign that each kind has the goal I have given it. On the other points the speakers sometimes do not argue at all. The man on trial will sometimes not dispute that the thing happened, or that he did harm, but he will never admit that he did wrong, since otherwise there would be no need for a trial. In the same way people giving political advice will often give up every other point, but they will never admit that what they are advising is not to our advantage, or that what they are warning us against is actually useful. Yet very often they do not care in the least about showing that it is not unjust to enslave their neighbours, even neighbours who have done them no wrong. In the same way people who praise or blame do not ask whether a man's actions were advantageous or harmful to him. On the contrary, they often count it in his praise that he did something noble with no thought for his own interest. So they praise Achilles because he went to the help of his friend Patroclus, knowing that he himself must die for it when he could have lived. For Achilles such a death was the nobler thing, while staying alive would have been the advantageous one.

It is clear from what has been said that the speaker must first have ready a stock of premises on these subjects. Sure signs, what is likely, and signs are the premises of rhetoric. A syllogism in general is made out of premises, and the enthymeme is a syllogism made out of the premises just mentioned.

Next, what is impossible cannot be done and cannot have been done; only what is possible can. Likewise, nothing that never happened can have been done, and nothing that never will happen can be done in the future. So the person giving political advice, the person in a lawsuit and the ceremonial speaker must all have premises ready about what is possible and what is impossible, about whether something has happened or not, and about whether something will happen or not.

Further, everyone, whether praising or blaming, urging or dissuading, accusing or defending, tries to show not only the things we have mentioned, but also that the good or the bad, the noble or the shameful, the just or the unjust in question is great or small. They do this either by speaking of the things in themselves or by setting them side by side and comparing them. So it is clear that the speaker must also have premises ready about greatness and smallness, and about the greater and the less, both in general and in each particular case: which good is greater or less, for instance, or which wrong, or which just act; and the same with the rest.

We have now said what the subjects are about which the speaker must, of necessity, have his premises ready. After this we must take each kind separately and set out its subjects: first what political advice is about, next what ceremonial speeches are about, and third what lawsuits are about.
