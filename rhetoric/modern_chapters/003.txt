Chapter 4: What Political Speeches Are About

First, then, we must grasp what kinds of good and bad things the person giving political advice advises about. He does not advise about everything, but only about things that may either happen or not happen.

About whatever necessarily is or will be, or cannot possibly be or come about, there is no advising.

Nor, in fact, is there advice about everything that may or may not happen. Among such things there are some goods that come about by nature and some that come by chance, and there is no point in giving advice about those. Clearly, advice is about the things we deliberate about, and those are the things that are naturally up to us, whose starting point lies in our own power. We go on looking into a course of action only until we have found out whether it is possible or impossible for us to do.

Now, to count up and sort into kinds, one by one and exactly, all the matters that people usually do business about in public, and further to define them, as far as possible, as they truly are, is not what we should be attempting at present. That is not the work of the art of rhetoric, but of an art that is wiser and closer to the truth; and rhetoric has already been given far more to study than properly belongs to it.

What we said earlier is true: rhetoric is made up of the science of logic, which I call analytics, and of the part of politics that deals with character, and it resembles dialectic in some ways and the arguments of the sophists, the paid teachers of clever talk, in others.

But the more anyone tries to make dialectic or rhetoric into sciences, rather than the abilities they are, the more he will blot out their nature without noticing it. He will be building them over into sciences of certain definite subjects, and not simply of arguments, and crossing into those sciences as he does so.

All the same, let us say even now as much about these matters as is worth sorting out, while still leaving room for the science of politics to look into them.

The subjects people deliberate about, and on which those who give political advice address the assembly, are pretty well five in number, if we take the most important ones. They are finance, war and peace, the defence of the country, imports and exports, and the making of laws.

So, on finance, the person who means to give advice should know what the city's sources of income are and how large they are, so that if any has been left out it may be added, and if any is too small it may be increased. He should also know all the city's expenses, so that if any is unnecessary it may be cut, and if any is too large it may be made smaller. For people grow richer not only by adding to what they already have but also by cutting down what they spend. And it is not enough to take in these things from experience of one's own city's affairs. To give advice on them a person must also be well informed about what has been found out elsewhere.

On war and peace, he should know the strength of the city: how great it is already and how great it could become, what kind of force it has now and what could be added to it, and also what wars it has fought and how it fought them. He must know these things not only about his own city but also about its neighbours, and above all about those with whom war is to be expected. The point is that the city should keep the peace with those stronger than itself, and keep in its own hands the choice of whether to go to war with those who are weaker. He must also know whether their forces are like or unlike ours, since here too one side may have the advantage or come off worse. And for this purpose he must have studied not only his own city's wars but also other people's wars, and how they turned out, because like causes naturally produce like results.

On the defence of the country, he must not be ignorant of how it is defended. He should know the size of the garrison, what kind of force it is, and where the guard-posts are, which is impossible unless he knows the country, so that if the garrison is too small it may be added to, if any part of it is unnecessary it may be withdrawn, and the places that matter may be watched more closely.

Next, on the food supply, he should know how much it costs to feed the city, and what kind of food it needs, both what is grown at home and what is imported; and which goods the city needs to export and which it needs to import, and from whom, so that treaties and trade agreements can be made with those people. For there are two kinds of people towards whom the citizens must be kept from giving any cause of complaint: those who are stronger than they are, and those who are useful to them in trade.

For the city's safety a speaker must be able to understand all these things, and not least he must have an understanding of the making of laws, since the city's security lies in its laws. So he must know how many forms of constitution there are, what is good for each, and what naturally destroys each of them, both the things that belong to that constitution itself and the things opposed to it. By being destroyed by things that belong to itself I mean this. Apart from the best constitution, all the others are destroyed both by being slackened and by being tightened too far, like the string of a lyre. Democracy, for instance, becomes weaker not only when it is slackened, until in the end it turns into an oligarchy, the rule of the few; it also becomes weaker when it is tightened too hard. It is like a hooked nose or a snub nose. When the curve is relaxed, the nose moves towards the ordinary shape in between; but when it becomes extremely hooked or extremely snub, it ends up so far gone that it does not even look like a nose.

For making laws it is useful not only to understand which constitution is good for us by looking at our own past, but also to know the constitutions of other peoples and which kinds suit which kinds of people. So it is clear that, for the making of laws, accounts of travels around the world are useful, since from them we can learn the laws of other nations; and for political debate, the histories written by people who record what men have done. But all this is the work of politics, not of rhetoric.

These, then, are the most important subjects on which the person who means to give political advice must have his premises ready. Now let us go back and say what we must draw on to urge people towards a course of action, or to warn them against it, both on these subjects and on the others.
