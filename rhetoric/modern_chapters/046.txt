Chapter 6: Dignity

Here are the things that give a style weight and dignity. First, use a description instead of a name: do not say "circle", but "a plane figure every point of which is the same distance from the centre". For brevity, do the opposite and use the name instead of the description.

If something is ugly or indecent, then when the ugliness lies in the description, use the name, and when it lies in the name, use the description.

Make things clear by metaphors and by descriptive epithets, but take care to avoid what sounds like poetry.

Make one thing into many, as the poets do. Though there is only one harbour, they still say

	to the harbours of Achaea

and, of a single writing tablet,

	here are the many-leaved folds of the tablet.

Do not join two words under one article, but give each its own. Greek can say *tês gunaikos tês hêmeteras*, "the wife, the one of ours", with "the" twice, and this is the weightier form. For brevity, do the opposite: *tês hêmeteras gunaikos*, "our wife".

Use a connecting word. For brevity, leave it out, but without leaving the thoughts disconnected: "having gone and having talked with him" is the fuller form, "having gone, I talked with him" the shorter.

The method of the poet Antimachus is useful too: describing a thing by what it does not have. He does this with the hill of Teumessus:

	There is a little hill, swept by the wind;

and then he goes on to praise it for all the things it is not, since in this way a description can be swelled without limit. The method works for good things and bad alike, by saying what a thing is not, whichever way happens to be useful. This is where the poets get words like "stringless" and "lyreless" music, *achordon* and *aluron*: they build epithets out of what a thing lacks. This is well thought of when it is used in metaphors by proportion, as in saying that the sound of the trumpet is a lyreless music, a song without the lyre that songs are usually played on.
