Chapter 12: Written and Spoken Style

We must not forget that each kind of speech has its own style. The style of writing is not the same as the style of debate, and within debate, the style of political speech is not the same as the style of the courts. A speaker needs to know both writing and debate. Knowing how to debate means knowing how to speak good Greek. Knowing how to write means not being forced to keep silent when you want to pass something on to other people, which is what happens to those who do not know how to write.

The written style is the most exact. The style of debate is the one best suited to delivery, and it comes in two kinds: one that expresses character and one that expresses emotion. That is why actors go looking for plays written this way, and poets go looking for actors who can play them. Yet poets written to be read are popular too, and people carry their books about: Chaeremon, for example, the tragic poet, who is as exact as a professional speechwriter, and Licymnius among the writers of dithyrambs, the choral songs to Dionysus. Put side by side, the speeches of the writers seem thin when they are heard in a debate, while the speeches of the orators, however well they were delivered, seem amateurish when you hold them in your hands and read them. The reason is that in debate what suits delivery is what fits. So when the delivery is taken away, things written for delivery cannot do their work and look foolish. Leaving out the connecting words, for example, and saying the same thing over and over again are rightly condemned in writing, but not in debate, and the orators use them, because they lend themselves to delivery.

When you repeat the same thing, though, you must change the way you say it, and this, as it were, clears the road for the delivery: "This is the man who robbed you. This is the man who deceived you. This is the man who in the end tried to betray you." This is what the actor Philemon used to do in Anaxandrides' play The Old Men's Battle, whenever he said "Rhadamanthus and Palamedes", and with the word "I" in the prologue of The Pious. If you do not act out lines like these, you become the man in the proverb who is carrying a beam: stiff as a plank.

The same goes for leaving out the connecting words: "I came, I met him, I begged him." These have to be acted out, and not spoken in one and the same character and tone of voice, as if they were a single thing. Leaving out the connecting words has a special effect of its own, too: many things seem to have been said in the same space of time. The word "and" turns many things into one, so if it is taken out, the opposite will plainly happen, and one thing will become many. So it makes things seem bigger: "I came, I talked with him, I pleaded with him" (it sounds like a great deal), "he looked down on everything I said." Homer is after the same effect in the lines

	Nireus, again, from Syme,
	Nireus, son of Aglaia,
	Nireus, the most handsome man.

If a great deal is said about someone, his name is bound to be mentioned many times; so if his name is mentioned many times, it seems as if a great deal has been said about him. By this piece of false reasoning Homer has made Nireus seem important, although he mentions him in this one passage only, and he has made him memorable, although he never says a word about him anywhere again.

The style of political speech is exactly like scene-painting, which is made to be looked at from a distance: the bigger the crowd, the further away the viewer stands. That is why fine detail is wasted in both, and even looks worse. The style of the courts is more exact, and more exact still before a single judge. There is the least room there for rhetoric, since it is easier to see at a glance what belongs to the case and what lies outside it; the struggle of debate is absent, and so the judgement is clean. That is why the same orators do not win applause in all these kinds. Where delivery counts for most, there exactness counts for least; and that is wherever the voice matters, and above all where a big voice matters.

The style of ceremonial speech is the most like writing, since its purpose is to be read; next to it comes the style of the courts.

To add the further distinction that style must be pleasant and magnificent is a waste of effort. Why those, rather than self-controlled, or generous, or any other virtue of character? What has been said already will plainly make it pleasant, if we have defined the virtue of style correctly. Why else must it be clear, and not mean, but fitting? If it babbles on, it is not clear, and it is not clear if it is too brief either; plainly what fits is the middle way. And what has been said will make the style pleasant if there is a good mixture of the familiar and the unfamiliar, and rhythm, and the persuasiveness that comes from being fitting.

That is all we have to say about style, both what is common to every kind of speech and what is special to each. It remains to speak of arrangement.
