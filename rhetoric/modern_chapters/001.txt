Chapter 2: The Means of Persuasion

Let rhetoric, then, be defined as the ability to see, in any given case, whatever means of persuasion it offers. That is the task of no other art. Every other art teaches and persuades about its own subject matter: medicine about what makes people healthy or sick, geometry about the properties of shapes and sizes, arithmetic about numbers, and the same with all the rest of the arts and sciences. Rhetoric, by contrast, seems able to see what is persuasive about more or less any subject that is put in front of it. That is why we say that, as an art, it does not have its rules fixed to any one special class of things.

Of the proofs, some are outside the art and some are supplied by the art itself. By proofs outside the art I mean all those that we did not provide ourselves but that were there already: witnesses, evidence given under torture by slaves, written contracts, and anything of that kind. By proofs the art itself supplies I mean all those that can be built by method and by our own efforts. So the first kind we only have to use, but the second kind we have to find.

Of the proofs that are provided by the speech, there are three kinds. The first lies in the speaker's character; the second in putting the listener into a certain frame of mind; the third in the argument itself, in so far as it proves something, or seems to.

A speaker persuades through his character when the speech is delivered in such a way as to make him seem worth believing. We believe good and fair-minded people more, and sooner, about everything in general; and where there is no exact knowledge but room for two opinions, we believe them completely. But this too must come about through the speech itself, not through what people already thought of the speaker before he began. Some writers of handbooks leave the speaker's goodness out of the art altogether, as though it did nothing to make him persuasive. We do not. On the contrary, character is, you might almost say, the most powerful of the proofs.

A speaker persuades through his listeners when the speech moves them to emotion. We do not give the same verdict when we are grieved as when we are glad, or when we love as when we hate. And this, as we said, is the one thing that today's writers of handbooks try to deal with. We shall explain these things one by one when we come to discuss the emotions.

Finally, people are persuaded through the argument itself when we show that something is true, or seems true, from whatever is persuasive about the particular matter in hand.

Since these are the three means by which proofs come about, it is clear that to master them a person must be able to reason, able to study characters and the virtues, and, third, able to study the emotions: what each emotion is, what it is like, what causes it and how it arises. So it turns out that rhetoric is a kind of offshoot of dialectic, and also of the study of character, which it is fair to call politics. That is why rhetoric slips on the costume of politics, like an actor putting on a mask, and so do the people who lay claim to it, partly through lack of education, partly through boastfulness, and partly for other all too human reasons. In fact rhetoric is a branch of dialectic and resembles it, as we said at the start. Neither of them is a science of how things stand in any one definite subject; both are simply abilities to supply arguments.

About their abilities, then, and how they are related to each other, enough has been said. Now for the proofs that work by proving something, or seeming to. In dialectic there is induction, which argues from particular cases; there is the syllogism; and there is the apparent syllogism, which only looks like one. It is the same in rhetoric. The example is an induction, the enthymeme is a syllogism, and the apparent enthymeme is an apparent syllogism. I call the enthymeme a rhetorical syllogism, and the example a rhetorical induction. Everyone who produces proofs by proving something does it either by giving examples or by using enthymemes, and by nothing besides. So, since in general anything whatever must be proved either by syllogism or by induction (and this is clear to us from the Analytics, my books on logic), it follows that each of these two in rhetoric must be the same as one of those two in logic: the example as induction, the enthymeme as syllogism.

What the difference is between an example and an enthymeme is clear from the Topics, where syllogism and induction have already been discussed. When something is shown to be so on the basis of many similar cases, that is called induction in dialectic and an example in rhetoric. When certain things are granted, and something else follows from them, beyond them, simply because they are so, whether always or usually, that is called a syllogism in dialectic and an enthymeme in rhetoric.

It is clear, too, that each of the two kinds of rhetorical speaking has its own merit. What was said in the Methodics, a work of mine on method, holds good here as well. Some speeches are rich in examples and others in enthymemes, and in the same way some speakers are fond of examples and others of enthymemes. Speeches that rely on examples are no less persuasive, but the ones that rely on enthymemes get the louder applause. The reason for this, and how to use each of them, we shall discuss later. For now, let us define these proofs themselves more clearly.

What is persuasive is persuasive to somebody. Some things are persuasive and believable straight away, in themselves; others because they seem to be proved by things that are persuasive and believable. Now no art looks at the individual case. Medicine does not ask what is healthy for Socrates or for Callias, but what is healthy for a person of this kind or for people of that kind. That is the part that belongs to an art, whereas individual cases are endless and cannot be known scientifically. In the same way rhetoric will not study what seems believable to this or that individual, to Socrates or to Hippias, but what seems believable to people of a certain kind. Dialectic does the same. It does not reason from any premises that happen to come along, for even people talking nonsense have certain things that seem true to them. Dialectic reasons from questions that need argument, and rhetoric from the matters people are already in the habit of deliberating about.

Rhetoric's work, then, is concerned with the kind of things we deliberate about but have no art or system for, and it is done in front of listeners who cannot take in an argument with many steps, or follow a chain of reasoning from far back. And we deliberate only about things that seem capable of turning out either way. Nobody deliberates about things that could not have been, could not be, or could not become otherwise, if he believes that is how they are, since there would be nothing to gain by it.

It is possible to reason and draw conclusions in two ways: from premises that have already been reached by reasoning, or from premises that have not, but that need reasoning because they are not what people generally believe. The first way is bound to be hard to follow because of its length, since the judge is assumed to be a simple person. The second is bound to be unpersuasive, because it starts from things that are neither agreed nor generally believed. So the enthymeme and the example must deal with things that can, for the most part, be otherwise than they are. The example is an induction and the enthymeme a syllogism, and the enthymeme is built from few premises, often fewer than the ones a full syllogism needs. If one of the premises is well known, there is no need to state it, because the listener adds it himself. Suppose you want to show that Dorieus, the famous athlete from Rhodes, has won a contest whose prize is a crown. It is enough to say that he has won at the Olympic Games. There is no need to add that the prize at Olympia is a crown, because everybody knows it.

Only a few of the premises from which rhetorical syllogisms are made are necessary. Most of the things we judge and consider could be otherwise than they are. People deliberate and think hard about what they are going to do, and all human actions are of that kind: practically none of them happens out of necessity. And what happens usually, or only possibly, can be reached by reasoning only from other premises of the same kind, just as what is necessary can be reached only from what is necessary; this too is clear to us from the Analytics. It is clear, then, that of the premises enthymemes are drawn from, a few will be necessary but most will hold only usually. And since enthymemes are drawn from what is likely and from signs, each of these must correspond to one of those two kinds of premise.

What is likely is what happens for the most part. It is not simply that, as some people define it, but what happens for the most part among things that could be otherwise; and it stands to the thing it makes likely as the general stands to the particular.

Of signs, some stand as a particular stands to the general, others as the general stands to a particular. A sign that is necessary is called a sure sign; a sign that is not necessary has no special name to mark the difference.

By necessary signs I mean those from which a valid syllogism can be made. That is why this kind of sign is called a *tekmêrion*, a sure sign. When people think that what they have said cannot be refuted, they think they are offering a *tekmêrion*, something proved and finished, since in the old language *tekmar*, the root of the word, means the same as *peras*, a limit or end.

Here is how a sign stands as a particular to the general. Suppose someone said that it is a sign that wise people are just, because Socrates was wise and just. This is a sign, but it can be refuted even if what is said about Socrates is true, because no valid syllogism can be made from it. But suppose someone said that it is a sign that a man is ill because he has a fever, or that a woman has given birth because she has milk. That sign is necessary. It is the only kind of sign that is a sure sign, because it is the only kind that, if the fact is true, cannot be refuted. Here, finally, is how a sign stands as the general to a particular. Suppose someone said that it is a sign that a man has a fever because he is breathing fast. This too can be refuted, even if it is true that he is breathing fast, since it is possible to breathe hard without having a fever.

So we have now said what the likely is, what a sign and a sure sign are, and how they differ. But these things, and the reason why some of them cannot make a valid syllogism while others can, are laid out more clearly in the Analytics.

We have said that the example is an induction, and what kind of material it works on. It is not the relation of part to whole, nor of whole to part, nor of whole to whole, but of part to part, like to like. When two cases come under the same general kind, but one is better known than the other, the better-known one is an example. Take the argument that Dionysius of Syracuse is plotting to make himself tyrant, because he is asking for a bodyguard. Pisistratus, earlier, when he was plotting a tyranny in Athens, asked for a bodyguard, got one, and made himself tyrant; so did Theagenes in Megara. All these, and any others the listeners know of, become examples for the case of Dionysius, about whom they do not yet know whether this is why he is asking. All of them fall under the same general rule: someone plotting a tyranny asks for a bodyguard.

That, then, is what the proofs that seem to prove things are made from.

But there is a very great difference between enthymemes, one that has escaped almost everybody, and it is the same difference that exists among syllogisms in the method of dialectic. Some enthymemes belong to rhetoric, just as some syllogisms belong to the method of dialectic; others belong to other arts and abilities, some of which already exist while others have not yet been worked out. That is why they slip past the listeners unnoticed; and the more properly a speaker gets hold of them, the further he moves away from rhetoric and dialectic without realising it. What I mean will be clearer if I say it at greater length.

By dialectical and rhetorical syllogisms I mean those that deal with what we call topics, the stock lines of argument. These are the common ones, which apply alike to questions of justice, of nature, of politics, and of many other subjects that differ in kind. An example is the line of argument from more and less: it will be no easier to build a syllogism or an enthymeme from it about justice than about nature, or about anything else, even though these subjects differ in kind. Special arguments, on the other hand, are those drawn from premises that belong to one particular species and kind of thing. There are premises about nature, for instance, from which you cannot build an enthymeme or a syllogism about ethics, and premises about ethics from which you cannot build one about nature; and the same holds in every field. The common lines of argument will not make anyone knowledgeable about any particular kind of thing, because they are not about any particular subject. But with the special ones, the better a person chooses his premises, the more he will find, without noticing, that he has made a science other than dialectic and rhetoric. For if he hits upon first principles, what he is doing will no longer be dialectic or rhetoric, but the science whose first principles he has got hold of.

Most enthymemes are drawn from these special and particular kinds, and fewer from the common ones. So here, as in the Topics, we must distinguish the kinds of enthymeme and the lines of argument they are to be taken from. By kinds I mean the premises that belong to each particular class of things; by lines of argument, the ones common to all alike. Let us talk about the kinds first. But first of all let us pin down the kinds of rhetoric, so that once we have divided them and know how many there are, we can take up the elements and premises of each one separately.
