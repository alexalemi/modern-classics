Chapter 11: Setting Things Before the Eyes

We have said, then, that wit comes from metaphor by proportion and from setting things before the eyes. Now we must say what we mean by "before the eyes", and what you have to do to make it happen.

By setting things before the eyes I mean using words that show things in action. For example, to call a good man "four-square", as the poet Simonides did, is a metaphor, since a good man and a square are both complete; but it does not show anything in action. On the other hand, "in the full flower of his prime" does show actuality, and so does Isocrates' "you, like a sacred animal left to range free", said to Philip of Macedon, since an animal dedicated to a god could wander where it pleased. And in the line

	Then the Greeks, darting forward on their feet,

the word "darting" is both actuality and metaphor, because it tells you they moved fast.

Homer does this often, using metaphor to make lifeless things alive. In every case what wins him applause is that he shows the thing in action, as in these lines:

	Back down to the plain rolled the shameless stone,

and

	the arrow flew,

and

	eager to fly on,

and

	they stood in the earth, longing to glut themselves with flesh,

and

	the spear-point sped raging through his chest.

In all of these, because the things are made alive, they seem to be in action: being shameless, raging and the rest are all forms of action. Homer fastened these words on by means of metaphor by proportion. The first line is about Sisyphus in the underworld, who must roll a stone up a hill for ever only to have it roll back down, and the stone is to Sisyphus what a shameless man is to the man he treats shamelessly.

He does the same with lifeless things in his famous similes. Of the waves of the sea he says:

	Arching, crested with foam, some in front and others behind them.

He makes everything move and live, and actuality is movement.

Metaphors should be drawn, as I said before, from things that are related to the subject but not obviously so. In philosophy too, it takes a sharp eye to see the likeness in things that are far apart. Archytas, for instance, said that an arbitrator and an altar were the same thing, because the man who has been wronged runs to both for refuge. Or you might say that an anchor and a pot-hook are the same, since both are the same sort of thing, a hook that holds something steady, and differ only in that one holds from below and the other from above. And to say that cities have been "levelled" finds the same thing in things far apart: the equality of a flat surface, and equality in power.

Most witty sayings come from metaphor and from catching the hearer out besides. It becomes clearer to him that he has learned something when it turns out the opposite of what he expected, and his mind seems to say, "How true! And I got it wrong." Witty sayings of the pithy kind come from not meaning what you seem to say, like the saying of Stesichorus that the Locrians' cicadas would have to sing from the ground: he meant that an invader would cut down their trees. Well-made riddles are pleasant for the same reason, since there is learning in them, and they are put as metaphors. So is what Theodorus calls "saying something new". This happens when the thing said is unexpected and does not, as he puts it, fit with what you thought before, the way comedians twist a word into something else. Jokes made by changing a letter do the same, because they catch you out. It happens in verse, too, for the line does not end as the listener expected:

	And on he strode, and underneath his feet he wore his chilblains,

where the listener thought he was going to say "sandals". But the point of this kind of joke has to be clear the moment it is said.

Jokes made by changing a letter work not by meaning what the word says but by twisting the word. When Theodorus said to Nicon the lyre-player, *thraxei se*, "it will rattle you", the phrase sounds like *Thrax ei su*, "you're a Thracian". He pretends to say one thing and catches you out, because he is saying something else. So the joke is pleasant to someone who sees it, but if you do not take Nicon to be a Thracian, it will not seem witty at all.

The same goes for "you want to sack him", where the word for "sack", *persai*, sounds like the word for Persians. Both kinds of joke must be said in the right way. Witty sayings of the other kind work like this too. You might tell the Athenians that their empire of the sea was not the beginning of their troubles, since they profited from it; or you might say, as Isocrates did, that the empire was the beginning of the city's troubles. The joke is that the Greek word *archê* means both "empire" and "beginning". Either way, what nobody expected to hear is said, and it is recognised as true. To say that the empire is an empire would be nothing clever; but that is not what he says. He means the word the other way. And in the first saying, the *archê* that is denied is not the one that was named, but the other.

In all these cases, the saying is good when the speaker applies the word fittingly, whether by a double meaning or by a metaphor. Take "Bearable is unbearable", said of a man called Anaschetos, a name that means Bearable. It denies what the name says, but fittingly, if he is an unpleasant man. Or take the line

	Do not be more of a stranger than a stranger should.

"Not more of a stranger than you should" comes to the same as "the stranger must not be a stranger for ever", and here too the word is taken over from another sense, since a stranger is both a foreigner visiting the city and a man who keeps his distance. It is the same with the much-praised line of Anaxandrides:

	It is fine to die before you do anything that deserves death.

This is the same as saying "it is worth dying when you do not deserve to die", or "it is worth dying when you are not worthy of death", or "when you are doing nothing worthy of death".

The form of the style in these sayings is the same. But the more briefly and the more antithetically such a thing is said, the more applause it wins. The reason is that the antithesis makes us learn more, and the brevity makes us learn faster.

If what is said is to be true and not superficial, it must always have one of two things added: either it is aimed at the person it is said about, or it is said correctly. You can have one of these without the other. "A man should die having done no wrong" is not witty. "The worthy man should marry the worthy woman" is not witty either. It is witty only when it has both at once: "it is worth dying when you are not worthy of death". And the more of these qualities it has, the wittier it seems: if, for example, the words are a metaphor, and a metaphor of the right sort, and there is antithesis and balance between the clauses, and actuality as well.

Similes too, as I said earlier, are in a way metaphors, the ones that win applause. For they are always put in two terms, like the metaphor by proportion. We say, for instance, that the shield is "the drinking cup of Ares", the god of war, and that the bow is "a lyre without strings". Said like this, the phrase is not simple; to call the bow a lyre, or the shield a cup, is simple.

This is also how people make comparisons: a pipe-player to a monkey, because of the way he hunches over his pipes, or a short-sighted man to a lamp with rain dripping on it, because both screw themselves up, the man his eyes and the lamp its flame.

A simile is good when there is a metaphor in it. You can compare a shield to the drinking cup of Ares, a ruin to the rags of a house, or say that Niceratus is "a Philoctetes bitten by Pratys". That was the comparison Thrasymachus made when he saw Niceratus after Pratys had beaten him in a contest of reciting Homer, still with his hair uncut and filthy, like Philoctetes left alone on his island after the snake bit him. This is where poets fail worst if they do it badly, and win most applause if they do it well: I mean when they make the second half answer the first, as in

	He wears his legs as curly as the parsley,
	like Philammon the boxer sparring with the punchbag.

All things of this sort are similes, and similes, as has been said many times, are metaphors.

Proverbs, too, are metaphors from one particular to another. If someone brings something into his house expecting to profit from it, and then is harmed by it, people say he is "like the Carpathian with the hare". The man from Carpathus brought a pair of hares to his island, and they bred until they had eaten all the crops. Both men have suffered the thing the proverb describes. That is roughly all that needs saying about where witty sayings come from and why they are witty.

The exaggerations that win applause are metaphors as well. Of a man with a black eye, for example, you might say, "you would have taken him for a basket of mulberries", since a black eye is purplish, but "a whole basket" is the exaggeration. And the phrase "like this or that" is an exaggeration that differs only in its wording. So

	like Philammon sparring with the punchbag

becomes "you would have taken him for Philammon fighting the punchbag", and

	wearing his legs as curly as parsley

becomes "you would have thought he had not legs but parsley, they are so curly".

Exaggeration belongs to the young, because it shows vehemence. That is why people use it most when they are angry, like Achilles refusing Agamemnon's gifts:

	Not if he gave me as many things as there are grains of sand and dust.
	The daughter of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, I will not marry,
	not if she rivalled golden Aphrodite in her beauty
	and Athena in her handiwork.

So it is unfitting for an older man to use it. The orators of Athens use it more than anyone.
