Chapter 9: The Periodic Style

We have now said that style must have a good rhythm and not be without rhythm, and which rhythms give it one, and how they must be arranged. Next: a style must be one of two kinds. Either it is a running style, held together only by its connecting words, like the preludes of dithyrambs, the choral songs for Dionysus, which run on freely without any matching stanzas; or it is a compact style, turned back on itself, like the stanzas of the old poets, where each answers the one before in the same shape.

The running style is the old one: "This is the setting out of the inquiry of Herodotus of Thurii", which opens a history whose sentences go on adding clause to clause. Everyone used to write this way, though not many do now. By a running style I mean one that has no end in itself, and stops only when the matter being told has come to its end. It is unpleasant because it has no limit, since everybody wants to see the end ahead. That is why runners gasp and collapse only at the turning post: while they could see the finish in front of them, they did not tire.

That, then, is the running style. The compact style is the one built of periods. By a period I mean a sentence that has a beginning and an end in itself, and a length that can be taken in at a glance. A style like this is pleasant and easy to learn. It is pleasant because it is the opposite of the unlimited kind, and because the listener always feels he has got hold of something and that something has been finished for him, whereas it is unpleasant to be unable to see ahead or to get anywhere. It is easy to learn because it is easy to remember, and that is because the periodic style has number, which is the easiest thing of all to remember. This is why everyone remembers verse better than loose prose: verse has a number by which it is measured.

But the period must also be complete in its sense, and not be cut off in the middle, as happens in these iambic lines of Sophocles:

	This land is Calydon; of Pelops' country,

If you pause at the end of the line, you can take it the wrong way round, since the division makes it sound as if Calydon were in the Peloponnese, Pelops' country. In fact "of Pelops' country" belongs to the line that follows, which places Calydon's plains across the strait from it.

A period is either made of clauses or simple. A period made of clauses is complete, is divided into its parts, and can be said in one breath; and it is divided not just anywhere, the way the line about Calydon was, but as a whole. A clause is one of the two parts of such a period. By a simple period I mean one that has only one clause.

Neither the clauses nor the periods should be stunted or too long. One that is too short often makes the listener stumble. He is still pressing forward towards the measure whose limit he already has in his head, and when the speaker stops and he is pulled back, he is bound to stumble at the sudden halt. Periods that are too long leave him behind, like people out walking together who go on past the usual turning point and leave their companions behind. In the same way, long periods become a speech in themselves and are like a dithyrambic prelude. The result is what Democritus of Chios, the musician, mocked in Melanippides, the dithyramb poet, for writing long preludes instead of matching stanzas. He took two lines of Hesiod, "A man does harm to himself in doing harm to another, and bad advice is worst for the one who gave it", and swapped the prelude, *anabolê*, into the place of the advice, *boulê*:

	A man does harm to himself who does harm to another,
	and a long prelude is worst for the one who composed it.

The same can be said of speakers whose clauses go on too long. But when the clauses are much too short, the result is not a period at all, and it drags the listener along headlong.

A style made of clauses is either divided or antithetical. The divided kind is like this, from the opening of Isocrates' Festival Speech: "I have often wondered at those who first gathered the great festivals and set up the athletic contests." The two clauses sit side by side, but neither is set against the other. In the antithetical kind, each clause has one opposite set against another, or the same word is yoked to both opposites. For example, Isocrates says of the Athenians who sent out colonists: "They helped both kinds, both those who stayed behind and those who went with them. To the ones they gained more land than they had at home; to the others they left enough land at home." Here staying behind and going are opposites, and so are enough and more. Again: "both those who need money and those who want to enjoy it", where enjoying is set against getting. Again:

"In these matters it often happens that sensible men fail and foolish men succeed."

"At once they were judged worthy of the prize for valour, and not long afterwards they won the command of the sea."

Of Xerxes: "to sail across the mainland and march across the sea, by bridging the Hellespont and digging through Athos."

"And that men who were citizens by nature should be robbed of their city by law."

"For some of them died miserably, and others were saved shamefully."

"In private to use barbarians as household slaves, but in public to look on while many of their allies were made slaves."

"Either to have it while they live, or to leave it behind when they die."

And what somebody said in court against Pitholaus and Lycophron, the brothers who had murdered Alexander, the tyrant of Pherae: "These men sold you when they were at home; now they have come to you, they have bought you." Every one of these does what we have described.

This kind of style is pleasant because opposites are the easiest things to understand, and easier still when they are put side by side; and also because it is like a syllogism, since a refutation is precisely a bringing together of opposites.

That, then, is antithesis. There are two more figures. *Parisôsis*, "evenness", is when the two clauses are equal in length. *Paromoiôsis*, "likeness of sound", is when each of the two clauses has similar sounds at its extremities. These must come either at the beginning or at the end. At the beginning, it is always whole words that sound alike. At the end, it is the last syllables, or two forms of the same word, or the same word itself. At the beginning, for example, in a line from comedy: "For he got from him a field, a fallow field", where the Greek sets *agron*, "field", beside *argon*, "fallow", two words made of the same letters. Or in Homer:

	they could be moved by gifts and be won over by words,

where the two halves open on the matching words *dôrêtoi*, "moved by gifts", and *parrêtoi*, "won over". At the end: "You would have thought he had not fathered a child, but become a child himself", where the two clauses end on the same *paidion*, "child", and on verbs with the same ending, *tetokenai* and *gegonenai*; and "in the greatest of worries and the smallest of hopes", where the clauses end *phrontisi* and *elpisin*, "worries" and "hopes", in matching sounds. Two forms of the same word: "Does he think he deserves a statue of bronze, when he is not worth a bronze coin?", where *chalkous*, "of bronze", answers *chalkou*, "a bronze coin". The same word itself: "While he lived you spoke badly of him, and now he is dead you write badly of him", where both clauses end on *kakôs*, "badly". A single syllable: "What terrible thing would have happened to you, if you had seen a man idle?", where *deinon*, "terrible", and *argon*, "idle", end on the same *-on*. It is possible for one sentence to have all of these at once: to be an antithesis, to have equal clauses, and to have like endings, *homoioteleuton*. The ways of beginning a period have been listed pretty fully in the Theodectea.

There are also false antitheses, which sound like a contrast while both halves say the same thing, so that nothing is set against anything, like this line of Epicharmus, the comic poet:

	At one time I was in their house, at another I was at their place.
