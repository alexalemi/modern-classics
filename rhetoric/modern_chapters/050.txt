Chapter 10: Wit and Metaphor

Now that these points are settled, we must say where wit comes from: the smart sayings that win applause. Actually producing them is a matter of natural talent or of practice; showing what they are is the business of this inquiry.

So let us describe them and count them up, and let this be our starting point. Learning something easily is naturally pleasant to everyone. Words mean something. It follows that the words which teach us something are the most pleasant words there are. Now, rare words are unknown to us, and ordinary words we know already; so it is metaphor above all that does this. When Homer has Odysseus, disguised as an old beggar, call his old age "stubble", he makes us learn and understand something through the class the two things share: both have lost their bloom. We see the old man and the cut field at once, and we see what they have in common.

The similes of the poets do the same thing, which is why a well-made simile comes across as witty. For a simile, as I said earlier, is a metaphor that differs only in the way it is put: it adds the word "like". That is why it is less pleasant. It is longer, and it does not say that this thing is that thing, so the mind does not go looking for how it is.

It follows that the style and the enthymemes that are witty are the ones that make us learn quickly. That is why superficial enthymemes are not popular (by superficial I mean the ones that are plain to everyone and need no searching out). Nor are the ones that we fail to understand even once they are said. What is popular is the kind we understand the moment it is spoken, even though we did not know it before, or the kind the mind catches up with a moment later. From these we get a kind of learning; from the other two we get none at all.

As far as the thought of what is said is concerned, then, these are the enthymemes that win applause. As far as the style is concerned, a saying succeeds by its shape, if it is put as an antithesis, two opposites set against each other. An example is: "counting the peace that everyone else shared as a war against their own private interests". Here war is set against peace.

It succeeds by its words if they contain a metaphor, and one that is neither far-fetched, since that is hard to take in at a glance, nor superficial, since that makes no impression on us at all. It succeeds, again, if it sets things before the eyes, since we ought to see things being done rather than things that are going to be done. So we should aim at three things: metaphor, antithesis and actuality, which means showing things in action.

There are four kinds of metaphor: from the general class to the particular, from the particular to the general, from one particular to another, and by proportion, where A is to B as C is to D. The kind that wins most applause is the one by proportion. Pericles, for example, said that the young men who had died in the war had vanished from the city "as if the spring had been taken out of the year". The spring is to the year what the young are to a city: the best part of it, and the part that holds its future. Leptines, speaking about the Spartans, said he would not stand by and watch Greece become one-eyed, since Athens and Sparta were her two eyes. When Chares, the Athenian general, was eager to have his accounts for the Olynthian war examined (every Athenian official had to submit to an audit of this kind when his term was over), his fellow general Cephisodotus was furious, and said that Chares was trying to hand in his accounts while he had the people by the throat and was choking it. On another occasion Cephisodotus urged the Athenians to take their food with them and set out for Euboea at once, "like the decree of Miltiades", a decree that seems to have been famous for being carried out without a moment's delay. When the Athenians made a truce with Epidaurus and the towns along its coast, Iphicrates complained bitterly that they had robbed themselves of their travelling money for the war, since those were the places they could have lived off by plunder. Pitholaus called the state galley the Paralus "the people's cudgel", and Sestos, the town that controlled the grain trade from the Black Sea, "the bread-bin of the Piraeus". Pericles told the Athenians to get rid of Aegina, the island that lies across the water from their harbour, calling it "the eyesore of the Piraeus". Moerocles named a man of good reputation and said that he himself was no worse a scoundrel than that man: the other played the scoundrel at thirty-three per cent interest, and Moerocles only at ten. And there is the line of iambic verse by the comic poet Anaxandrides about daughters who were slow to marry, where the word he uses, *hyperêmeroi*, is the one used for a debtor who has let the day of payment go by:

	My girls have let the due date on their weddings pass.

Then there is what Polyeuctus said about a man named Speusippus, who was paralysed: that he could not keep still, although fortune had locked him in the five-holed stocks of disease, the kind that hold a man by the neck, both hands and both feet. Cephisodotus called the warships "painted mills", because they ground down the cities that paid Athens tribute, and Diogenes the Cynic called the taverns of Athens "the common messes of Attica", after the plain public dinners at which the Spartans ate together. Aesion said that the Athenians had "poured the city out into Sicily", meaning the disastrous expedition there. That is a metaphor, and it sets the thing before the eyes. So is his phrase "so that Greece cried aloud": that too is in a way a metaphor, and a vivid one. So is Cephisodotus' warning to the Athenians to take care that they did not make their assemblies into so many stampedes, and so is Isocrates' phrase about the people who "come running together" at the great festivals.

The Funeral Speech that goes under the name of Lysias says that at the tomb of the men who died at Salamis it would have been right for Greece to cut off her hair in mourning, "since her freedom was being buried along with their courage". If the speaker had said it was right to weep because their courage was being buried with them, that would have been a metaphor and a vivid one. But "her freedom along with their courage" adds a kind of antithesis as well. Iphicrates said, "The road of my speech runs right through the middle of what Chares has done": that is a metaphor by proportion, and the words "through the middle" set it before the eyes. To say that you are calling on dangers to come and rescue you from dangers is a vivid metaphor too. Lycoleon, speaking in defence of the general Chabrias, spoke of men "not even ashamed before his suppliant, the bronze statue". The statue, which stood in the market place in sight of the court, showed Chabrias kneeling, in the position he had ordered his soldiers to take as they waited for the enemy. That is a metaphor for the moment only, not for all time, but it is vivid: now that Chabrias is in danger, the statue itself begs for mercy on his behalf, the lifeless thing becomes a living one, the monument to what he has done for the city.

Or take "working hard in every way to be small-minded". To work hard at something is to make it grow, so the phrase has a man cultivating his own smallness. Or "God lit reason as a lamp in the soul", since reason and light both make something plain. Or "we do not end our wars; we put them off", since both are about the future, putting a thing off and a peace of that sort. Or to say that a treaty is a trophy far finer than any won in battle, since a battle trophy is set up over some small gain and a single stroke of luck, while a treaty stands for the whole war, and both are signs of victory. Or to say that cities pay a heavy account to the censure of mankind, since paying an account, in the sense of the official audit, is a kind of just penalty.
