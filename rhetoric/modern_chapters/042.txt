Chapter 2: Clarity and Propriety

Let us take those matters as settled, then, and let us define the excellence of style as being clear. A speech is a kind of sign, and if it does not make its meaning plain it will not do its own job. Style should also be neither flat nor above the dignity of its subject, but fitting. The style of poetry, perhaps, is not flat, but it is not fitting for prose.

Among nouns and verbs, it is the standard words, the ones in everyday use for a thing, that make style clear. The other kinds of word, the ones discussed in my Poetics, keep it from being flat and give it ornament. For stepping away from the ordinary makes language seem grander. People feel about style the way they feel about strangers compared with their fellow citizens: what comes from elsewhere impresses them.

That is why you should give your language an unfamiliar air, a touch of the strange, since people admire what is remote, and what is admired is pleasant. In verse there are many ways of doing this, and there it suits, because the things and the people a poem talks about are further removed from ordinary life. In plain prose there are far fewer, because the subject is humbler. Even in poetry, it would be rather unfitting if fine language came from a slave or a very young man, or were spent on very small matters. Even there, what is fitting draws in or swells out to match the subject.

So you must do it without being noticed, and must not seem to be speaking in an artificial way but in a natural one. The natural is persuasive and the artificial the opposite, because people take offence at a speaker they suspect of laying a trap for them, just as they do at wine that has been watered. It should be like the voice of the actor Theodorus compared with the voices of the other actors: his seemed to be the voice of the man he was playing, theirs the voices of somebody else.

The art is well hidden if you build your language out of words chosen from ordinary speech. Euripides does this, and he was the first to show how.

A speech is made up of nouns and verbs, and nouns come in all the kinds that were examined in my book on poetry. Of these, rare words (words from other dialects, or old words out of use), compound words and newly coined words should be used seldom and in few places. Where they should be used I shall say later; why, I have said already: they step too far from what is fitting, in the direction of the grand. The standard word, the word that properly belongs to the thing, and metaphor are the only kinds useful for the style of plain prose. The proof is that these are the only kinds everyone uses, for everyone talks in metaphors, in the words that belong to things and in standard words. So it is clear that if you do this well, your language will have an unfamiliar air, and yet the art can escape notice, and you will be clear. And that, as we said, is the excellence of the style of rhetoric.

Among words, the sophist finds most use in words with more than one meaning, since he plays his tricks with them; the poet finds most use in synonyms. By synonyms I mean words that are both standard and mean the same, such as *poreuesthai* and *badizein*, both of which mean "to go" or "to walk": each is a standard word, and each means the same as the other.

What each of these kinds of word is, how many kinds of metaphor there are, and that metaphor has the greatest power both in poetry and in prose, has been said, as I mentioned, in my Poetics. But in prose you have to take all the more trouble over metaphors, because prose has fewer resources to help it than verse has. Metaphor, more than anything, gives clearness, pleasure and an unfamiliar air, and it is something you cannot learn from anybody else.

Both epithets and metaphors must suit the thing. This will come from proportion, from making the metaphor answer to the thing as one thing answers to another. Otherwise it will look out of place, because opposites show up most sharply when they stand side by side. You have to ask: as a scarlet cloak suits a young man, what suits an old one? For the same clothes do not suit both.

If you want to dress a thing up, take your metaphor from the best thing of the same family; if you want to run it down, from the worst. I mean, for example, that since opposites belong to the same family, you can say that a man who begs is praying, or that a man who prays is begging, because both are ways of asking, and this is the method I am describing. So Iphicrates called Callias, whose family held the honoured office of torch-bearer at the Mysteries of Eleusis, a begging priest, one of the wandering priests of the Mother goddess who go round collecting alms, instead of a torch-bearer. Callias answered that Iphicrates could never have been initiated into the Mysteries, or he would not have called him a begging priest but a torch-bearer. Both titles have to do with the service of a god, but one is honoured and the other despised. In the same way some people call actors "hangers-on of Dionysus", while they call themselves "artists". Both are metaphors, one of them smearing the thing and the other the opposite. Pirates, too, nowadays call themselves "suppliers", *poristai*. That is why you can say that a man who has done a wrong has made a mistake, or that a man who has made a mistake has done a wrong; and why you can say of a thief that he "took" something, or that he "procured" it, *porisasthai*, the pirates' own word. But the line from the Telephus of Euripides,

	ruling the oar, he stepped ashore in Mysia,

is unfitting, because "ruling", *anassôn*, the word for a king's rule, is grander than the thing deserves, and so the art is not hidden.

There can also be a fault in the very sounds of a word, if they are not signs of a pleasant sound. Dionysius, nicknamed the Brazen because he proposed a bronze coinage, calls poetry in his elegies "the scream of Calliope", the Muse of epic. Both poetry and a scream are sounds, so far the metaphor holds; but it is a bad metaphor, because a scream is a sound that means nothing.

Next, when you give a name by metaphor to something that has no name of its own, you must not fetch the metaphor from far off, but from things that are related to it and of the same kind, so that as soon as it is said it is clear that the two are related. So in the famous riddle,

	I saw a man who glued bronze onto a man with fire,

the operation had no name, but both it and gluing are a kind of putting one thing onto another; so the riddle called the application of the cupping-glass, the heated bronze cup a doctor sets on the skin, "gluing". And in general you can get good metaphors out of well-made riddles, since metaphors are riddles of a kind; so it is clear that the transfer has been well made.

Metaphors should also come from beautiful things. The beauty of a word lies, as Licymnius says, in its sound or in its meaning, and its ugliness likewise. There is a third point, which answers the sophists' argument. It is not true, as Bryson said, that nobody ever uses foul language, on the ground that it means the same thing whether you use this word or that. That is false. One word is more standard than another, more like the thing, and better suited to putting the thing before our eyes. Besides, two words do not signify the thing in the same light, so for that reason too one must count one word more beautiful or more ugly than another. Both words may signify what is beautiful or what is ugly, but not as beautiful, or not as ugly; or, if they do, then more or less so. So metaphors should be drawn from what is beautiful in sound, or in its effect, or to the eye, or to some other sense. It makes a difference whether you say, as Homer does, "rosy-fingered dawn", *rhododaktulos*, rather than "crimson-fingered", *phoinikodaktulos*, or, worse still, "red-fingered", *eruthrodaktulos*. All three name a colour of the fingers, but only the first calls up something lovely.

With epithets too, you can take them from the bad or ugly side of a thing, as when Orestes is called "the man who killed his mother", or from the better side, as when he is called "his father's avenger": the same deed, seen from its two sides, as the two men argue it in Euripides' Orestes. And Simonides, when the winner of a mule race offered him a small fee, refused to write a poem, as if he found it beneath him to write about half-asses. But when the man paid him enough, he wrote,

	Hail, daughters of storm-footed horses!

And yet they were the daughters of donkeys too.

Diminutives work the same way. A diminutive makes both the bad and the good smaller, as Aristophanes mocks in his Babylonians, where people say *chrusidarion*, "goldikins", instead of gold, *himatidarion*, "a little cloaklet", instead of cloak, *loidorêmation*, "a teeny insult", instead of an insult, and *nosêmation*, "a tiny illness", instead of an illness. But you must be careful, with epithets and diminutives alike, and keep to the mean.
