Chapter 7: The Greater Good

People often agree that two courses are both useful but dispute which is more useful. So the next thing to discuss is the greater good and the more useful.

First, some definitions. One thing exceeds another when it is as much as the other and something more; the thing exceeded is the one contained in it. "Greater" and "more" are always said in relation to something less. "Great" and "small", "much" and "little", are said in relation to the size of most things: the great is what exceeds that ordinary size, and the small is what falls short of it; and the same holds for much and little.

Now, we call a thing good if it is worth choosing for its own sake and not for the sake of something else; if it is what all things aim at; if it is what anything would choose if it had intelligence and practical wisdom; and if it produces or preserves such things, or if such things follow from it. That for the sake of which we act is the end, and the end is that for the sake of which everything else is done; and a thing is good for a particular person if it stands in these relations to him. It follows, then, that several goods are a greater good than one, or than a smaller number, provided that the one, or the smaller number, is counted in among the several. For then the several exceed it, and what is contained in something is exceeded by it.

Next: if the greatest member of one class exceeds the greatest member of another, then the first class exceeds the second; and whenever one class exceeds another, the greatest member of the first exceeds the greatest member of the second. For example, if the biggest man is bigger than the biggest woman, then men in general are bigger than women; and if men in general are bigger than women, then the biggest man is bigger than the biggest woman. The margins by which classes exceed one another match the margins by which their greatest members do.

Next: when one thing follows from a second, but the second does not follow from the first, the second is the greater good, because the benefit of what follows is already contained in the benefit of what it follows from. A thing may follow from another at the same time, or later, or potentially. Being alive follows from being healthy at the same time, but being healthy does not follow from being alive. Knowing follows from learning, later. And robbery follows from robbing a temple potentially, since a man who has robbed a temple would also rob a person.

Things that exceed the same thing by a larger amount are greater, since they must also exceed the larger of the two amounts.

Things that produce a greater good are greater, since that is what we meant by producing something greater. The same goes for a thing produced by something greater. If what makes us healthy is more worth choosing than what gives us pleasure, and a greater good, then health is a greater good than pleasure.

What is worth choosing for its own sake is more worth choosing than what is not. Strength, for example, is a greater good than what is good for your health, since the second is not chosen for its own sake while the first is, and being chosen for its own sake was what we meant by a good.

If one thing is an end and the other is not, the end is greater: the other is chosen for the sake of something else, the end for its own sake. Exercise, for instance, is chosen for the sake of a body in good condition.

What has less need of some other thing or things is greater, since it is more sufficient in itself; and a thing has less need when it needs fewer things, or easier ones.

When one thing cannot exist, or cannot come into existence, without a second, while the second can do without the first, the one that needs nothing is more sufficient in itself, and so it looks like the greater good.

If one thing is a starting point and the other is not, or one is a cause and the other is not, the first is greater for the same reason: without a cause and a starting point, nothing can exist or come to be. And of two starting points, what comes from the greater starting point is greater; of two causes, what comes from the greater cause is greater. Conversely, of two starting points, the starting point of the greater thing is greater; and of two causes, the cause of the greater thing is greater.

It is clear from all this that a thing can be made to look greater in both of two opposite ways. If it is a starting point and the other is not, it will seem greater; and if it is not a starting point and the other is, it will also seem greater, because the end is greater than the starting point. That is how Leodamas argued in two trials over the affair of Oropus, a border town that the Athenian statesman Callistratus had advised leaving in Theban hands for the time being, a plan the general Chabrias carried out. Accusing Callistratus, Leodamas said that the man who gave the advice was more guilty than the man who carried it out, because the thing would never have been done if he had not advised it. Accusing Chabrias, he said the opposite: that the man who carried it out was more guilty than the man who advised it, because it would never have happened without someone to do it, and the whole reason people plot is so that someone will carry the plot out.

What is rarer is greater than what is plentiful: gold, for example, than iron, though gold is less useful, because getting hold of it is harder and so a greater thing. From another angle, though, the plentiful is greater than the rare, because we get more use out of it: "often" beats "seldom". That is why the poet Pindar begins an ode by saying:

	Best of all things is water.

In general, what is harder is greater than what is easier, because it is rarer. From another angle, what is easier is greater than what is harder, because it is as we would like it to be.

A thing is greater if its opposite is greater, or if the loss of it is greater. And virtue is greater than the absence of vice, and vice is greater than the absence of virtue, since virtue and vice are ends and the mere absences are not.

Things whose works are nobler or more shameful are themselves greater. And where the virtues or the vices are greater, the works are greater too. For as causes and starting points are to each other, so are their results, and as results are to each other, so are their causes and starting points.

Things are greater if excelling in them is more worth choosing or nobler. Seeing sharply, for example, is more worth choosing than smelling sharply, since sight is more worth choosing than smell. And since caring more for your friends is nobler than caring more for money, the love of friends is nobler than the love of money. Conversely, excelling in better things is better, and excelling in nobler things is nobler.

Things are greater if the desire for them is nobler or better, since greater longings are longings for greater things. And for the same reason, the desires for nobler or better things are themselves better and nobler.

Where the sciences are nobler or more serious, their subject matter is nobler and more serious too, since as the knowledge stands, so stands the truth it knows, and each science lays down what belongs to its own field. And for the same reason, the sciences of more serious and nobler things are correspondingly more serious and nobler.

Whatever the wise would judge, or have judged, to be the greater good, whether it is all of them, or most of them, or the greater number, or the best of them, must be so, either without qualification or insofar as they judged it by their wisdom. This rule holds for everything else as well: what a thing is, how much of it there is and what kind it is are all as knowledge and wisdom would say. But we have stated it for goods, since we defined the good as what anything would choose if it had wisdom. So it is clear that the greater good is the one that wisdom rates higher.

What belongs to better people is greater, either without qualification or insofar as they are better: courage, for example, is greater than strength. So is what a better person would choose, either without qualification or insofar as he is better: to suffer wrong, for example, rather than to do it, since that is what the juster man would choose.

What is more pleasant is greater than what is less pleasant, since everything pursues pleasure and people long for the feeling of pleasure for its own sake, and these are the marks by which we defined the good and the end. And a pleasure is greater when it has less pain mixed in and when it lasts longer.

What is nobler is greater than what is less noble, since the noble is either what is pleasant or what is worth choosing for its own sake.

Things that people would more like to bring about for themselves or for their friends are greater goods; and things they would least like to bring about are greater evils.

Things that last longer are greater than things that last a shorter time, and things that are secure are greater than things that are not. With the first we get more use out of them in time; with the second, our use of them depends on our wishes, since a secure possession is there whenever we want it.

Whatever holds of one word in a family of related words holds of the others, and of the other grammatical forms of the same word. If acting bravely, for example, is nobler and more worth choosing than acting with self-control, then courage is more worth choosing than self-control, and being brave than being self-controlled.

What everyone chooses is greater than what not everyone chooses, and what more people choose is greater than what fewer people choose. For the good was what everyone aims at, so the greater good is what people aim at more. So too with what is preferred by our opponents, or our enemies, or by the judges, or by the people the judges themselves think well of. In the first case it is as if everyone were saying it; in the second, it is the people who have the authority and who know.

Sometimes what everyone has a share in is greater, since not having a share in it is a disgrace. Sometimes what no one, or only a few, have a share in is greater, since it is rarer.

Things more worthy of praise are greater, since they are nobler. Likewise things for which the honours are greater, since honour is a kind of measure of worth. And likewise things for which the penalties are greater.

Things greater than what everyone agrees, or it seems, is great are greater still. And the same thing, divided into its parts, looks greater, since it seems to exceed in more respects. That is why Homer says that Meleager, who was sitting out the fighting in a rage, was persuaded to rise up by his wife listing everything that happens to a captured city:

	All the evils that fall on people whose city is taken:
	the men are killed, and fire levels the city to dust,
	and strangers carry off the children.

Putting things together and building one on top of another, as the comic poet Epicharmus did, works the same way, and for the same reason as dividing: putting things together displays a great superiority, and it looks like the starting point and the cause of great things.

Since what is harder and rarer is greater, the right moment, a person's age, the place, the time and a person's power all make things great. If a man does something beyond his power, beyond his age, or beyond what people like him could do, or if he does it in a particular way, or in a particular place, or at a particular time, it will take on greatness, whether it is a noble, good or just act, or the opposite. That is the point of Simonides' epigram on the Olympic victor:

	Once I carried a rough yoke across my shoulders
	and brought fish from Argos down to Tegea.

And it is why Iphicrates, the general who had risen from a cobbler's son, used to praise himself by pointing to what he had started from.

What is natural is greater than what is acquired, since it is harder. That is why Homer has the singer Phemius, pleading for his life before Odysseus, say:

	I taught myself.

The greatest part of something great is greater. So Pericles, in his funeral speech for the war dead, said that the city's loss of its young men was as if the spring had been taken out of the year.

Things useful in greater need are greater, such as the things useful in old age and in illness. Of two things, the one nearer the end is greater. What is good for a particular person is greater than what is good without qualification. What is possible is greater than what is impossible, since the possible is good for us and the impossible is not. And things that come at the end of life are greater, since what comes near the end is more of an end.

Things aimed at the truth are greater than things aimed at appearances. The test of a thing aimed at appearances is that you would not choose it if no one were ever going to find out. That is why receiving a kindness might seem more worth choosing than doing one: you would choose to receive it even if no one ever knew, but it does not seem that anyone would choose to do a kindness in secret.

So too, things that people would rather have in reality than in appearance are greater, since they are more a matter of truth. That is why people say that justice is a small thing, because it is more worth choosing to seem just than to be just. That is not so with being healthy.

What is useful for more purposes is greater: for example, what helps us to live, and to live well, and to enjoy ourselves, and to do noble things. That is why wealth and health are thought to be the greatest goods, since they have all of these in them.

What brings less pain and comes with pleasure is greater, since that is more than one good: pleasure is a good, and so is freedom from pain. And of two goods, the greater is the one that, added to the same thing, makes the whole greater.

Things that do not go unnoticed when we have them are greater than things that do, since they tend towards the truth. That is why being rich would seem a greater good than seeming rich.

Last, a thing is greater if it is held dear, and it is held dearer by someone who has only that one than by someone who has others besides. That is why the damage is not equal if someone blinds a man who has only one eye and a man who has two: the one-eyed man has lost the one thing he held dear.

These, more or less, are the sources from which we must bring our proofs when we urge people to do something and when we urge them not to.
