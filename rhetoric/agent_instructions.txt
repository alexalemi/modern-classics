THE VOICE, AND THE ONE RULE THAT MATTERS MOST
The Rhetoric is lecture notes: terse, elliptical, full of lists, and packed
with allusions to speeches, poems and people a modern reader has never heard
of. Freese renders it word for word and it is nearly unreadable. The job is
the Dōgen readability ruling: EVERY SENTENCE ONE A BRIGHT FOURTEEN-YEAR-OLD
COULD READ ALOUD AND FOLLOW, WITH EVERY CLAUSE OF THE GREEK PRESENT.
  - Unpack the ellipses. Where Aristotle writes "for example, the saying of
    Iphicrates", say what Iphicrates said and why it is an example, in the
    same sentence. Where he quotes half a line and trusts the reader to know
    the rest, give the reader what they need to see the point.
  - FOLD THE STORY INTO THE SENTENCE, never a footnote: "Iphicrates, the
    general who had risen from a cobbler's son, ..." A short identifying
    phrase for a person, a place, a play or a law, drawn from Freese's
    notes where he has one; never more than a clause. Do NOT invent facts:
    if Freese does not explain an allusion and you are not sure, say only
    what the Greek says.
  - Keep his lists as lists in prose (or numbered where he numbers them),
    and keep the joints of the argument visible: "first", "next", "so",
    "but". He is teaching; the signposts are the teaching.
  - Plain words for plain things. Keep a TECHNICAL TERM only where the book
    turns on it, and then use the ledger's word every time.
  - He is dry and often funny (the young "love honour more than money,
    because they have not yet learned what it is to go without"). Let it be.
  - NO archaism: no thou, hath, whilst, unto, behold, whence, 'tis.
    Freese's English is 1926; do not drift into it.

QUOTED VERSE stays VERSE, tab-indented, ONE ENGLISH LINE PER GREEK LINE, the

MARKUP: the pipeline is markup-free. *italic* only for a Greek word you keep

other asterisks, no bold, no brackets for your own notes, no ALL-CAPS line

full stop (it renders as a subheading).

THE VERNE RULE: his claims stand as his, unhedged, with no modern note:
slaves and their evidence under torture (1.15), women and the household,
"barbarians", the character of the poor and the rich, the lot. Say plainly
what he says.
