Aladdin; Or, the Wonderful Lamp
(Part 1 of 13)

[Editor's note: Aladdin and Ali Baba, which close this selection, are the two most famous stories in the Nights and the two that are not in it. Neither appears in any Arabic manuscript older than the eighteenth century. Both reached Europe through Antoine Galland, who took them down in Paris in 1709 from a young Syrian traveller named Hanna Diyab, and only afterwards were they written back into Arabic and printed. Burton translated them from that later Arabic. They carry their own night numbering, which is why the count starts again here instead of going on from the tale before.]

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that there lived in a city of the cities of China a man who was a tailor, and a poor one; and he had one son, whose name was Aladdin.

Now this boy had been a good-for-nothing and a scapegrace from his babyhood; and when he reached his tenth year his father wanted to teach him his own trade — for he was too poor to spend money on having him taught any other work or craft or apprenticeship — and took the lad into his shop to learn tailoring.

But as Aladdin was a scapegrace and a good-for-nothing, and used to play at all hours with the gutter boys of the quarter, he would not sit in the shop for a single day. He would wait for his father to leave it on some errand or other, such as going to meet a creditor, and then he would run straight off and away to the gardens with the other scapegraces and low companions who were his friends.

That was how things stood; advice and beatings were no use, and he would not obey either parent in anything, nor learn any trade at all. And presently, out of sadness and sorrow at his son's shiftless idleness, the tailor fell ill and died.

Aladdin carried on in his old bad ways; and when his mother saw that her husband was dead and her son was a scapegrace and good for nothing whatever, she sold the shop and everything in it and took to spinning cotton yarn. By this hard work she fed herself and found food for her son Aladdin the scapegrace — who, finding himself free of his father's severities, grew more idle and low in his habits than ever, and would never stay at home except at mealtimes, while his poor wretched mother lived on nothing but what her hands could spin. And so it went on until the boy had reached his fifteenth year.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifteenth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when Aladdin had come to his fifteenth year, it happened one day, as he was hanging about the quarter playing with the vagabond boys, that a dervish from the Maghrib, the Land of the Setting Sun, came up and stood watching the lads for his amusement; and he looked hard at Aladdin and studied his face carefully, hardly noticing his companions at all.

Now this dervish was a Moor from the interior of Morocco, and he was a magician who could heap hill upon hill by his magic, and he was an adept in astrology too. So after examining Aladdin closely he said to himself, "This is truly the lad I need, and the one I left my native country to find."

Presently he drew one of the children aside and questioned him about the scapegrace, saying, "Whose son is he?" And he asked for every scrap of information about his circumstances and everything to do with him.

After that he walked up to Aladdin and, taking him aside, asked, "My son, are you not the child of such-and-such a tailor?"

And the lad answered, "Yes, sir; but he died a long time ago."

When the Moroccan magician heard these words he threw himself upon Aladdin and wound his arms round his neck and fell to kissing him, weeping all the while, with the tears running down his cheeks.

But when the lad saw the state the Moroccan was in he was greatly surprised, and questioned him, saying, "What is making you weep, sir? And how did you come to know my father?"

"How can you ask me such a question, my son," replied the Moroccan, in a soft voice full of feeling, "after telling me that your father — my brother — is dead? For he was my own brother; and now I have come from my adopted country, and after a long exile I was rejoicing at the hope of seeing him once more and talking over old times with him — and now you tell me he is gone. But blood does not hide from blood, and it revealed to me that you are my nephew, my brother's son; and I knew you out of all the lads, although your father, when I parted from him, was not yet married."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Sixteenth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the Moroccan magician said to the tailor's orphan, "My son Aladdin, I have missed the mourning ceremonies now, and lost the joy I looked for in meeting your father, my brother, whom after my long banishment I had hoped to see once more before I die. But the great distance did me this injury; and a creature has no refuge from the Creator, and no trick to play against the commandments of Almighty Allah."

Then he clasped Aladdin to his bosom again, crying, "My son, I have nobody left to grieve with now except yourself; and you stand in your father's place, being his offspring and his representative — and whoever leaves offspring does not die, my child!"

So saying, the magician put his hand to his purse and pulled out ten gold pieces and gave them to the lad, asking, "My son, where is your house, and where does she live — your mother, my brother's widow?"

Aladdin got up with him at once and showed him the way to their home; and as they went, the wizard said, "My son, take this money and give it to your mother, with my greetings, and let her know that your uncle, your father's brother, has come back from his exile; and that tomorrow, God willing, I will visit her and greet her with the salaam, and see the house my brother lived in, and look at the place where he lies buried."

At this Aladdin kissed the Moroccan's hand, and then ran in his joy at top speed to his mother's house and went in to her, quite contrary to his custom, since he never came near her except at mealtimes. And when he found her, the lad exclaimed in his delight, "Mother, I bring you good news of my uncle, who has come back from his exile and who now sends me to greet you!"

"My son," she replied, "it seems to me you are making fun of me. Who is this uncle? How can you have an uncle in the land of the living?"

He answered, "How can you say, mother, that I have no living uncles and no kinsmen, when this man is my father's own brother? Indeed he embraced me and kissed me, shedding tears all the while, and told me to let you know about it."

She retorted, "My son, I know very well that you had an uncle; but he is dead now, and I am not aware that you have another."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Seventeenth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the Moroccan magician went out the next morning and set about finding Aladdin, for his heart would no longer let him be parted from the lad; and as he was going to and fro about the city streets he came face to face with him, amusing himself as usual among the vagabonds and the scapegraces.

So he went up to him and, taking his hand, embraced him and kissed him; then he pulled two dinars out of his pocket and said, "Off you go to your mother and give her these two gold pieces, and tell her that your uncle would like to have supper with you. So take the two coins and get us a good supper ready. But before anything else, show me the way to your house once more."

"On my head and on my eyes be it, uncle," replied the lad, and went in front of him, pointing out the street that led to the house. Then the Moroccan left him and went his way, and Aladdin ran home and gave his mother the news and the two coins, saying, "My uncle is coming to supper with us."

So she got up at once and went to the market street and bought everything she needed; then, coming back to the house, she borrowed from the neighbours whatever was wanted in the way of pans and dishes and so on. And when the meal was cooked and suppertime came, she said to Aladdin, "My child, the food is ready, but perhaps your uncle does not know the way to our house; so go out and meet him on the road."

He answered, "To hear is to obey"; and before the two of them had finished talking there was a knock at the door.

Aladdin went out and opened it, and there was the Moroccan magician, with a servant carrying the wine and the dessert fruit; so the lad led them in, and the servant went off about his business.

The Moroccan, on entering, greeted his sister-in-law with the salaam, and then began to shed tears and to question her, saying, "Where is the place where my brother used to sit?"

She showed it to him, and he went up to it and prostrated himself in prayer and kissed the floor, crying, "Ah, how small my satisfaction is and how luckless my lot, that I have lost you, my brother, vein of my eye!" And he went on in this fashion, weeping and wailing, until he fainted away from the violence of his sobbing and lamenting; so that Aladdin's mother was quite convinced he was telling the truth.

So she came up to him and raised him from the floor and said, "What good is there in killing yourself?"

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Eighteenth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Aladdin's mother set about consoling the Moroccan magician and settled him on the divan; and as soon as he was comfortably seated, and before the food trays were brought in, he fell to talking with her and saying:

"Wife of my brother, it must seem a wonder to you that in all your days you never saw me, nor heard anything about me during the lifetime of my brother, who has found mercy. The reason is that forty years ago I left this town and exiled myself from my birthplace, and wandered over all the lands of India and Sind, and went into Egypt and settled a long time in its magnificent city, which is one of the wonders of the world; until at last I made my way to the regions of the Setting Sun and lived thirty years in the interior of Morocco.

"Now one day, sister-in-law, as I was sitting alone at home, I fell to thinking of my own country and my birthplace and my brother, who has found mercy; and my longing to see him grew so strong that I wept and bewailed being a stranger and so far away from him. And at last my longings drove me homewards, until I made up my mind to travel to the country where my head shall fall and where my homestead is, so that I might see my brother again.

"And I said to myself: Man, how long will you wander like a desert Arab, away from your birthplace and your native soil? Moreover, you have one brother and no more; so up with you and travel and look at him before you die, for who knows the woes of the world and the changes of the days? It would be the saddest regret if you lay down to die without ever seeing your brother — and Allah (praise be to the Lord!) has granted you ample wealth; and it may be that he is in straitened and poor circumstances, and then you will be able to help your brother as well as see him.

"So I got up at once and equipped myself for the journey and recited the Fatihah; and then, when Friday prayers were over, I mounted and travelled to this town, after suffering all sorts of toils and troubles which I bore patiently, while the Lord (to whom be honour and glory!) covered me with the veil of His protection.

"So I arrived; and while I was wandering about the streets, the day before yesterday, I saw my brother's son Aladdin playing with the boys. And by God the Great, sister-in-law, the moment I saw him this heart of mine went out to him — for blood yearns to blood — and my soul felt and told me that he was my very nephew. So I forgot all my toils and troubles at once at the sight of him, and I was ready to fly for joy. But when he told me that the dear one had departed to the mercy of Almighty Allah, I fainted away from the shock of my grief and disappointment. Perhaps my nephew has told you of the pains that overcame me. But I am consoled after a fashion by the sight of Aladdin, the legacy left to us by him who has found mercy; for whoever leaves offspring is not wholly dead."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Nineteenth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the Moroccan magician said to Aladdin's mother, "Whoever leaves offspring is not wholly dead." And when he looked at his sister-in-law she wept at these words of his; so he turned to the lad, to make her forget the mention of her husband — as a way of comforting her, and also of completing his deception — and asked him, "My son Aladdin, what have you learned in the way of work, and what is your business? Tell me, have you mastered any trade by which you can earn a living for yourself and for your mother?"

The lad was abashed and put to shame, and he hung his head and bowed his face towards the ground. But his mother spoke out: "How, indeed? By Allah, he knows nothing at all. Such an ungracious child I never yet saw — no, never! All day long he idles away his time with the boys of the quarter, vagabonds like himself; and his father — oh, my grief! — died of nothing but sorrow over him. And I am now in a pitiful state myself: I spin cotton and toil at my distaff night and day, so that I may earn a couple of loaves of bread which we eat together. That is how things are, brother-in-law; and, by your life, he never comes near me except at mealtimes, and at no other. Indeed, I am thinking of locking the house door and never opening to him again, but leaving him to go and find a living he can live on; for I am a woman getting on in years now and have no strength left to work and go about earning a keep in this way. O Allah, I am forced to provide him with his daily bread when it is I who need providing for!"

At this the Moroccan turned to Aladdin and said, "Why is this, son of my brother? Why do you go about so ungraciously? It is a disgrace to you and unbecoming in a man like yourself. You are a lad with sense, my son, and the child of honest folk; so it is a shame for you that your mother, a woman in years, should struggle to support you. And now that you have grown to a man's estate it is for you to contrive some way of living, my child.

"Look around you — and praise be to Allah, in this town of ours there are many teachers of every kind of craft, and nowhere are there more of them. So choose some calling that pleases you, and I will set you up in it; and when you are grown, my son, you shall have a business to live by. Perhaps your father's trade is not to your liking; and if so, choose some other handicraft that suits your fancy, and let me know, and I will help you with everything I can, my son."

But when the Moroccan saw that Aladdin kept silence and made him no answer, he knew that the lad wanted no occupation but the life of a scapegrace; so he said to him, "Son of my brother, do not let my words seem hard and harsh to you; for if, in spite of everything I say, you still dislike learning a trade, I will open you a merchant's shop stocked with the costliest stuffs, and you shall become famous among people and take and give and buy and sell and be well known in the city."

Now when Aladdin heard his uncle the Moroccan's words, and his plan of making him a merchant and a gentleman, he was overjoyed, knowing that such people dress handsomely and live delicately. So he looked at the Moroccan, smiling and drooping his head towards the ground, and saying, in the language of the situation, that he was content.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Twentieth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the Moroccan magician looked at Aladdin and saw him smiling, by which he understood that the lad was content to become a trader. So he said to him, "Since you are content that I should open you a merchant's shop and make a gentleman of you, then, son of my brother, prove yourself a man; and, God willing, tomorrow I will take you to the bazaar in the first place and have a fine suit of clothes cut out for you, the sort of thing merchants wear; and in the second place I will look out a shop for you and keep my word."

Now Aladdin's mother had had some doubts about the Moroccan being her brother-in-law; but as soon as she heard him promise to open a merchant's shop for her son and set him up with goods and capital and the rest of it, the woman decided and determined in her mind that this Moroccan was truly her husband's brother, seeing that no stranger would do such a good turn for her son.

So she began directing the lad into the right road, and teaching him to cast the ignorance out of his head and prove himself a man; moreover, she told him always to obey his excellent uncle as though he were his son, and to make up for the time he had wasted in wilfulness with his friends.

After this she got up and spread the table and served the supper; so they all sat down and fell to eating and drinking, while the Moroccan talked with Aladdin about matters of business and the like, delighting him to such a degree that he got no sleep that night.

But when the Moroccan saw that the dark hours were passing and the wine was drunk, he got up and hurried off to his own place; but before he went he agreed to come back next morning and take Aladdin and see to a suit of merchant's clothes being cut out for him.

And as soon as it was dawn, there was the Moroccan rapping at the door, which was opened by Aladdin's mother. He would not come in, though, but asked to take the lad with him to the market street. So Aladdin went out to his uncle and wished him good morning and kissed his hand; and the Moroccan took him by the hand and went with him to the bazaar.

There he went into a clothier's shop stocked with all kinds of clothes, and asked for the most sumptuous suit in it; and the merchant brought out what he wanted, all fashioned and ready sewn, and the Moroccan said to the lad, "Choose whatever pleases you, my child."

Aladdin was overjoyed to see that his uncle had given him his choice, so he picked out the suit that most took his fancy, and the Moroccan paid the merchant the price of it in ready money. Then he took the lad to the baths, where they bathed; and then they came out and drank sherbets, after which Aladdin got up and put on his new clothes in enormous joy and delight, and went up to his uncle and kissed his hand and thanked him for his kindness.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.
