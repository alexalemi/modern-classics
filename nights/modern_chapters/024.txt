The Eldest Lady's Tale

Truly my tale is a strange one, and it is this.

Those two black bitches are my eldest sisters, by the same mother and father; and these two others — the one who carries the marks of the whip on her, and the third, our caterer — are my sisters by another mother.

When my father died, each of us took her share of the inheritance; and after a while my mother died too, leaving me and my full sisters three thousand dinars, so that each daughter received a thousand, and I the same, although I was the youngest.

In due course my sisters married with the usual festivities and went to live with their husbands, who bought merchandise with their wives' money and set out travelling together. And so they threw me off.

My brothers-in-law were away with their wives five years, in which time they spent all the money they had; and going bankrupt, they abandoned my sisters in foreign parts among strangers.

After five years my eldest sister came back to me in beggar's clothes, her dress in rags and tatters and a dirty old cloak over it; and truly she was in the foulest and sorriest state. At first sight I did not know my own sister; but presently I recognised her and said, "What state is this?"

"Sister," she answered, "words cannot undo what is done; and the pen of Destiny has run through what Allah decreed."

Then I sent her to the baths and dressed her in a suit of my own, and boiled her a broth and brought her some good wine, and said to her:

"Sister, you are the eldest, and you still stand to us in the place of father and mother. And as for the inheritance that came to me as it did to you two, Allah has blessed it and prospered it and made it grow; and my circumstances are comfortable, for I have made a good deal of money spinning and cleaning silk. You and I will share what I have equally."

I treated her with every kindness, and she stayed with me a whole year, during which our thoughts and our imaginations were always on our other sister.

Not long afterwards she came home too, in an even fouler and sorrier state than the eldest; and I dealt with her still more generously than I had with the first, and each of them had her share of what I had.

After a time they said to me, "Sister, we want to marry again; truly we have not the patience to drag out our days without husbands and live the lives of widows under a spell."

And I replied, "My dears! You have seen precious little good in marriage so far; these days good men and true have become rarities and curiosities. And I do not think your plans are advisable, since you have already tried matrimony and it failed."

But they would not take my advice, and married without my consent — although I gave them their outfits and their dowries out of my own money; and off they went with their husbands.

In a very little while their husbands played them false and, taking whatever they could lay hands on, absconded and left them in the lurch. At which they came to me ashamed and in an abject state, and made their excuses, saying:

"Pardon our fault and do not be angry with us; for though you are younger in years, you are older in sense. From now on we will never mention marriage again; so take us back as your servants, so that we may have a mouthful to eat."

Said I, "Welcome to you, sisters; there is nothing dearer to me than you." And I took them in and redoubled my kindness to them.

We went on living in this loving way for a full year, when I decided to sell my goods abroad, and first of all to fit out a passage to Basra. So I equipped a large ship and loaded her with merchandise and valuable goods for trading, and with provisions and everything needed for a voyage, and said to my sisters, "Will you stay at home while I travel, or would you rather come with me on the voyage?"

"We will travel with you," they answered, "for we cannot bear to be parted from you."

So I divided my money into two parts, one to come with me and the other to be left with a trustworthy person — for, as I said to myself, "Some accident may happen to the ship and we still be alive; in which case we shall find something on our return to stand us in good stead."

I took my two sisters and we voyaged some days and nights; but the master was careless enough to lose his course, and the ship went astray with us and entered a sea other than the sea we were making for.

For a time we knew nothing about it; and the wind blew fair for us ten days, after which the lookout man went aloft to look about him and cried, "Good news!" Then he came down rejoicing and said, "I have seen what seems to be a city, no bigger than a pigeon."

At this we were delighted; and before an hour of the day had gone by the buildings showed plain in the offing, and we asked the captain, "What is the name of that city?"

And he answered, "By Allah I do not know, for I never saw it before and never sailed these seas in my life. But since our troubles have ended safely, all that remains is for you to land there with your merchandise; and if you find selling profitable, then sell and make your market of what is there; and if not, we will rest two days and provision ourselves and go on."

So we entered the port, and the captain went up into the town and was gone a while, after which he came back to us and said, "Get up; go up into the city and marvel at the works of Allah with His creatures, and pray to be preserved from His righteous anger."

So we landed, and going up into the city, saw at the gate men holding staves in their hands; but when we came close to them, they had been transformed by the anger of Allah and had become stone.

Then we went into the city and found everyone who lived there turned into black stone. Not one inhabited house was to be seen by anybody looking for one, and there was not a soul blowing up a fire.

We were awestruck at the sight, and threaded through the market streets, where we found the goods and the gold and the silver left lying where they were; and we were glad, and said, "There must be some mystery in all this."

Then we scattered about the thoroughfares, and every one of us busied himself with collecting the wealth and the money and the rich stuffs, taking very little notice of friend or companion.

As for me, I went up to the castle, which was strongly fortified; and going into the King's palace by its gate of red gold, found all the plate of gold and silver, and the King himself seated in the middle of his chamberlains and governors and emirs and viziers, all dressed in clothing that confounded the art of man.

I came nearer and saw him sitting on a throne encrusted and inlaid with pearls and gems; and his robes were of cloth of gold decorated with jewels of every kind, each one flashing like a star. Around him stood fifty Mamluks, white slaves, dressed in silks of various sorts and holding their drawn swords in their hands — but when I came close to them, every one of them was black stone.

My understanding was confounded at the sight; but I walked on and came into the great hall of the harem, whose walls I found hung with tapestries of gold-striped silk and spread with silk carpets embroidered with flowers of gold.

Here I saw the Queen lying at full length, dressed in robes edged with fresh young pearls; on her head was a diadem set with all sorts of gems, each one fit for a ring, and round her neck hung collars and necklaces. All her clothing and her ornaments were in their natural state, but she had been turned to black stone by Allah's anger.

Presently I noticed an open door and made straight for it, and found a flight of seven steps leading up to it. So I walked up and came upon a place floored with marble and spread and hung with gold-worked carpets and tapestries, in the middle of which stood a throne of juniper wood inlaid with pearls and precious stones and set with bosses of emerald.

In the far wall was an alcove whose curtains, strung with pearls, were let down; and I saw a light coming out of it. So I went nearer, and saw that the light came from a precious stone as big as an ostrich egg, set at the upper end of the alcove on a little couch of gold and ivory; and this jewel, blazing like the sun, cast its rays far and wide. The couch too was spread with every kind of silk, amazing anyone who looked at it with their richness and their beauty.

I marvelled a great deal at all this, and most of all at seeing candles there ready lit; and I said in my mind, "Somebody must have lit these candles."

Then I went out and came to the kitchen, and from there to the pantry and the King's treasure chambers; and I went on exploring the palace and walking from place to place. I forgot myself in my awe and wonder at all this, and I was drowned in thought until the night came on.

Then I would have gone out; but not knowing where the gate was, I lost my way. So I went back to the alcove, where the lit candles guided me, and sat down on the couch; and wrapping myself in a coverlet, after repeating something from the Koran, I would have slept — but I could not, for restlessness had hold of me.

When night was at its middle I heard a voice chanting the Koran in the sweetest tones; but the sound of it was weak. So I got up, glad to hear the silence broken, and followed the sound until I came to a small room whose door stood ajar.

Then, peeping through a chink, I looked at the place — and it was a place of prayer, with a prayer niche and two wax candles burning and lamps hanging from the ceiling. And in it a prayer carpet was spread, on which a young man was sitting, fair to look at; and in front of him on its stand was a copy of the Koran, which he was reading from.

I marvelled to see him alive, alone among all the people of the city; and going in, I greeted him, at which he raised his eyes and returned my greeting.

Said I, "Now by the truth of what you are reading in Allah's Holy Book, I beg you to answer my question."

He looked at me with a smile and said, "Handmaid of Allah, first tell me why you have come here, and I in my turn will tell you what has happened to me and to the people of this city, and why I escaped their doom."

So I told him my story, at which he wondered; and I questioned him about the people of the city, and he replied, "Have patience with me a moment, sister." And reverently closing the Holy Book, he laid it up in a satin bag.

Then he sat me down at his side; and I looked at him — and he was like the moon at the full, fair of face and rare of form, soft-sided and slight, of well-proportioned height, and with a cheek smoothly bright and giving off light; in short a sweet thing, a sugar stick — just as the poet says of somebody like him:

	That night the astrologer drew a scheme of the planets,
	and there appeared in view a graceful shape of a young man.
	Saturn had stained his hair with the most saturnine jet
	and blown spots of nut-brown musk on the rose of his cheek;
	Mars tinctured both cheeks with the tincture of martial red;
	Sagittarius shot arrows out of his eyelids;
	Mercury endowed him with a bright mercurial wit;
	and the Bear carried off whatever ill men's glances grew.
	The star-lover stood amazed to see this marvel born
	when the Moon at the full bowed low to kiss the Earth.

And truly Allah the Most High had robed him in the raiment of perfect grace, and had edged it and fringed it with a cheek all beauty and loveliness — just as the poet says of such a man:

	By his eyelids shedding perfume and his fine slim waist I swear,
	by the shooting of his arrows barbed with rare sorcery;
	by the softness of his sides, and the lingering light of his glances,
	and his brow of dazzling daylight with the night inside his hair;
	by his eyebrows, which deny all rest to whoever looks at them,
	now bidding, now forbidding, always dealing joy and care;
	by the rose that decks his cheek and the myrtle of its down,
	by the jacinths bedded in his lips and the pearls his smile lays bare;
	by his gracefully bending neck and the curving of his breast,
	whose polished surface carries that lovely pair of pomegranates;
	by his heavy hips that quiver as he passes in his pride,
	or as he rests on that waist that is slim beyond compare;
	by the satin of his skin, by that fine unsullied spirit;
	by the beauty in him that contains everything bright and debonair;
	by that ever-open hand, by the candour of his tongue;
	by noble blood and high degree, of which he is the hope and heir —
	musk borrows its muskiness from him and loves to breathe it out,
	and all the airs of ambergris perfume the air through him.
	The sun, I think — the broad bright sun — would go pale beside my love,
	and without his splendour would look like a paring of his nail.

I glanced at him with one glance of the eyes that cost me a thousand sighs, and my heart was taken captive that instant; so I asked him, "My lord and my love, tell me what I asked you."

And he answered, "To hear is to obey. Know, handmaid of Allah, that this city was my father's capital; and my father is the King you saw on the throne, transformed by Allah's anger into black stone; and the Queen you found in the alcove is my mother.

"They and all the people of the city were Magians who worshipped fire instead of the Omnipotent Lord, and used to swear by flame and heat, by shade and light, and by the spheres revolving day and night.

"My father had no son until he was blessed with me near the end of his days; and he brought me up, and I grew, and prosperity ran ahead of me in everything.

"Now it happened that there was with us an old woman well on in years, a Muslim who, believing inwardly in Allah and His Apostle, outwardly conformed to the religion of my people. And my father trusted her completely, because he knew her to be trustworthy and virtuous, and he treated her with more and more kindness, believing her to be of his own faith.

"So when I was nearly grown up my father put me in her charge, saying: take him and educate him and teach him the rules of our faith; give him the best instruction, and do not leave off your fostering care of him.

"So she took me and taught me the tenets of Islam, with the divine ordinances of the ritual washing and the five daily prayers; and she made me learn the Koran by heart, saying over and over: serve nobody but Allah Almighty.

"When I had mastered this much she said to me: my son, keep this matter hidden from your father, and reveal nothing to him, or he will kill you. So I hid it from him.

"I lived like this for a while, until the old woman died; and the people of the city redoubled in their impiety and their arrogance and their error.

"One day, while they were going on as usual, they heard a loud and terrible sound, and a crier crying out with a voice like rolling thunder, so that every ear could hear it, far and near: 'People of this city, leave off your fire-worshipping and worship Allah, the All-compassionate King!'

"At this, fear and terror fell on the citizens, and they crowded to my father, he being King of the city, and asked him, 'What is this awful voice we have heard? It has confounded us with the excess of its terror.'

"And he answered, 'Do not let a voice frighten you or shake your steadfast spirit or turn you back from the faith that is right.'

"Their hearts inclined to what he said, and they did not stop worshipping the fire; and they persisted in their rebellion for a full year from the time they heard the first voice. And on the anniversary came a second cry, and a third at the head of the third year, each year once.

"Still they persisted in their evil practices, until one day at daybreak judgement and the anger of Heaven came down on them all of a sudden; and by the visitation of Allah they were all turned into black stones — they and their beasts and their cattle — and nobody was saved except me, who at that hour was busy at my devotions.

"From that day to this I have been in the state you see, constant in prayer and fasting and reading and reciting the Koran; but I have truly grown weary of my loneliness, with nobody to keep me company."

Then said I to him — for in very truth he had won my heart and was the lord of my life and my soul:

"Young man, will you come with me to the city of Baghdad, and visit the learned men and the men skilled in the law and the doctors of divinity, and get yourself an increase of wisdom and understanding and theology? And know that the woman standing in front of you will be your servant, although she is the head of her family and mistress over men and eunuchs and servants and slaves. Truly my life was no life before it met with your youth. I have a ship here loaded with merchandise; and in very truth Destiny drove me to this city so that I should come to know all this, for it was fated that we should meet."

And I did not stop persuading him and speaking him fair and using every art until he consented:—

When it was the Eighteenth Night,

she went on: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the lady did not stop persuading the young man with soft speech to go away with her until he consented and said yes. She slept that night lying at his feet, hardly knowing where she was for the excess of her joy.

As soon as the next morning dawned (she went on, addressing the Caliph), I got up, and we went into the treasuries and took from them whatever was light in weight and great in worth; and then we went down side by side from the castle into the city, where we were met by the captain and my sisters and the slaves, who had been looking for me.

When they saw me they were delighted, and asked what had kept me; and I told them everything I had seen, and told them the story of the young prince and the transformation that had justly come on the citizens. At which they all marvelled — but when my two sisters (these two bitches, Commander of the Faithful!) saw me at the side of my young lover, they grew jealous of me on his account and were angry, and plotted mischief against me.

We waited for a fair wind and went on board rejoicing, and ready to fly for joy at the goods we had got; but my own greatest joy was in the young man. And we waited a while until the wind blew fair for us, and then we set sail and went on our way.

Now as we sat talking, my sisters asked me, "And what will you do with this handsome young man?"

And I answered, "I mean to make him my husband." Then I turned to him and said, "My lord, I have something to propose to you in which you must not cross me; and it is this: that when we reach Baghdad, my native city, I offer you my life as your servant in holy matrimony, and you shall be my husband and I will be your wife."

He answered, "I hear and I obey. You are my lady and my mistress, and whatever you do I will not gainsay."

Then I turned to my sisters and said, "This is my gain; I am content with this young man, and those who have got any of my property, let them keep it as their gain with my goodwill."

"You speak and do well," the two of them answered; but they were imagining mischief against me.

We sailed on before a fair wind until we had exchanged the sea of peril for the seas of safety; and in a few days we made the city of Basra, whose buildings loomed clear in front of us as the evening fell.

But after we had gone to bed and were sound asleep, my two sisters got up and picked me up, bed and all, and threw me into the sea. They did the same to the young prince, who, being unable to swim, sank and was drowned; and Allah enrolled him in the noble army of Martyrs.

As for me — would to Heaven I had drowned with him; but Allah judged that I should be one of the saved. So when I woke and found myself in the sea, and saw the ship making off like a flash of lightning, He threw a piece of timber in my way, which I got astride of; and the waves tossed me to and fro until they cast me up on the coast of an island, a high land and an uninhabited one.

I landed and walked about the island the rest of the night; and when the morning dawned I saw a rough track, barely fit for a child of Adam to walk on, leading to what turned out to be a shallow ford joining the island to the mainland.

As soon as the sun had risen I spread my clothes out to dry in it, and ate some of the island's fruit and drank its water; then I set out along the footpath, and did not stop walking until I reached the mainland.

Now when there was only a two hours' journey left between me and the city, a great serpent, as big round as a date palm, came fleeing towards me in a tremendous hurry, gliding along now to the right and now to the left until she was close on me, her tongue lolling a span long towards the ground and sweeping the dust as she went.

She was being pursued by a dragon no longer than two lances, of slender build, about as thick as a spear; and although her terror lent her speed and she kept wriggling from side to side, he overtook her and seized her by the tail — at which her tears streamed down and her tongue was thrust out in her agony.

I took pity on her; and picking up a stone and calling on Allah for help, I threw it at the dragon's head with such force that he died then and there. And the serpent opened a pair of wings and flew up into the sky and disappeared from in front of my eyes.

I sat down marvelling at this adventure; but I was tired, and drowsiness came over me, and I slept where I was for a while.

When I woke I found a jet-black girl sitting at my feet massaging them; and by her side stood two black bitches — my sisters, Commander of the Faithful!

I was embarrassed in front of her; and sitting up, I asked her, "Sister, who and what are you?"

And she answered, "How quickly you have forgotten me! I am the one you did a good turn for and sowed the seed of gratitude in, and killed her enemy; for I am the serpent whom by Allah's aid you delivered just now from the dragon.

"I am a jinniyah, and he was a jinni who hated me, and nobody saved my life from him but you. As soon as you freed me from him I flew on the wind to the ship your sisters threw you from, and moved everything in it to your house. Then I ordered my attendant marids to sink the ship, and I transformed your two sisters into these black bitches — for I know everything that has passed between them and you. But as for the young man, the truth is that he is drowned."

So saying, she flew up with me and the bitches, and presently set us down on the terrace roof of my house, where I found the whole of the property that had been in my ship already stored, with nothing missing.

"Now," the serpent that was went on, "I swear by everything engraved on the seal ring of Solomon, on whom be peace: unless you give each of these bitches three hundred stripes every day, I will come and imprison you for ever under the earth."

I answered, "Hearing and obedience!" And away she flew. But before going she charged me again, saying, "I swear again by Him who made the two seas flow — and let this be my second oath — that if you disobey me I will come and transform you as I have your sisters."

Since then I have never once failed, Commander of the Faithful, to beat them with that number of blows, until their blood flows along with my tears — and I pitying them all the while. And they know perfectly well that their being scourged is no fault of mine, and they accept my excuses.

And this is my tale and my history.

The Caliph marvelled at her adventures, and then signed to Ja'afar, who said to the second lady, the doorkeeper, "And you — how did you come by the welts and the weals on your body?"
