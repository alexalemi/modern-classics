Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves
(Part 3 of 3)

When it was the Six Hundred and Thirty-sixth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Morgiana went on to Ali Baba:

"Judge now and see whether these signs and all this villainy are not the work of the bandits of the forest, who marked our house so that they might know it again. Of these forty thieves there are still two others left, about whose case I know nothing; so beware of them, but chiefly of the third one still remaining, their captain, who fled from here alive. Take good heed and be cautious of him; for if you fall into his hands he will in no way spare you, but will certainly murder you. I will do everything in my power to save your life and your property from hurt and harm, nor shall your servant be found wanting in any service to my lord."

Hearing these words, Ali Baba rejoiced exceedingly and said to her, "I am well pleased with you for this conduct of yours; and tell me what you would have me do on your behalf. I shall not fail to remember your brave deed as long as I have breath in me."

She said, "Before everything else, we must bury these bodies in the ground at once, so that the secret may not become known to anybody."

At this Ali Baba took his slave boy Abdullah with him into the garden, and there under a tree they dug a deep pit for the corpses of the thieves, of a size to fit its contents; and they dragged the bodies to the trench — having first carried off their weapons — and threw them in; and then, covering up the remains of the thirty-seven robbers, they made the ground look level and clean as it usually was.

They also hid the leather jars and the gear and the arms; and presently Ali Baba sent the mules by ones and twos to the bazaar and sold them all, with the able help of his slave boy Abdullah.

So the matter was hushed up and did not reach anybody's ears; and yet Ali Baba never stopped being uneasy, in case the captain or the two surviving robbers should wreak their vengeance on his head. He kept himself private with all caution and took care that nobody should learn a word of what had happened, or of the wealth he had carried off from the bandits' cave.

Meanwhile the captain of the thieves, having escaped with his life, fled to the forest in hot anger and sore vexation of mind, and his senses were scattered and the colour of his face vanished like rising smoke.

Then he thought the matter over again and again, and at last firmly resolved that he must take Ali Baba's life, or else he would lose all the treasure, which his enemy, knowing the magic words, would carry away and turn to his own use. Furthermore, he determined that he would undertake the business single-handed; and that, after getting rid of Ali Baba, he would gather another band of robbers together and carry on his career of brigandage, as indeed his forefathers had done for many generations.

So he lay down to rest that night, and, getting up early in the morning, put on a suitable-looking outfit; then, going to the city, he put up at a caravanserai, thinking to himself, "No doubt the murder of so many men has reached the governor's ears, and Ali Baba has been seized and brought to justice, and his house is levelled and his goods confiscated. The townsfolk must surely have heard news of all this."

So he asked the keeper of the inn straight away, "What strange things have happened in the city these last few days?" — and the man told him everything he had seen and heard; but the captain could not learn a jot of what most concerned him.

By this he understood that Ali Baba was wary and wise, and that he had not only carried off such a store of treasure but had also destroyed so many lives and come off unscathed into the bargain; and furthermore, that he himself would need to have all his wits about him not to fall into his enemy's hands and perish.

With this resolve the captain hired a shop in the bazaar, to which he brought whole bales of the finest stuffs and fine merchandise from his forest treasure house; and presently he took his seat in the store and fell to doing a merchant's business.

By chance his place faced the shop of the late Kasim, where his son, Ali Baba's nephew, now traded; and the captain, who called himself Khwajah Hasan, soon struck up an acquaintance and friendship with the shopkeepers around him and treated them all with lavish civilities — but he was especially gracious and cordial to Kasim's son, a handsome and well-dressed youth, and would often sit and chat with him for a long while.

A few days later it happened that Ali Baba, as he sometimes did, came to see his nephew, whom he found sitting in his shop. The captain saw and recognised him at sight; and one morning he asked the young man, "Tell me, please: who is the man who comes to you now and then at your place of business?"

To which the youth answered, "He is my uncle, my father's brother."

Whereupon the captain showed him greater favour and affection still, the better to deceive him for his own purposes, and gave him presents and had him sit at meat with him and fed him with the daintiest of dishes.

Presently Ali Baba's nephew thought it was only right and proper that he should invite the merchant to supper in his turn; but since his own house was small, and he was cramped for room and could not put on a show of splendour as Khwajah Hasan did, he took counsel with his uncle about it.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Thirty-seventh Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Ali Baba replied to his nephew:

"You are quite right: you ought to entertain your friend as handsomely as he has entertained you. Tomorrow, which is Friday, shut your shop as all merchants of repute do; then, after the early meal, take Khwajah Hasan out to smell the air, and as you walk lead him here without his knowing. Meanwhile I will give orders that Morgiana shall make ready the best of dishes and everything needful for a feast against his coming. Do not trouble yourself in any way, but leave the matter in my hands."

So the next day, that is Friday, Ali Baba's nephew took Khwajah Hasan to walk about the garden; and as they were coming back he led him along the street where his uncle lived.

When they came to the house the youth stopped at the door and, knocking, said, "My lord, this is my second home: my uncle has heard a great deal about you and about your goodness to me, and wants exceedingly to meet you; so if you would consent to come in and visit him, I should be truly glad and grateful to you."

Although Khwajah Hasan rejoiced in his heart that he had found a means of getting into his enemy's house and household, and although he hoped soon to gain his end by treachery, still he hesitated to go in, and stood there making his excuses and preparing to walk away.

But when the door was opened by the slave porter, Ali Baba's nephew seized his companion's hand and, after a great deal of persuasion, led him in; at which he entered with a great show of cheerfulness, as though much pleased and honoured.

The master of the house received him with all favour and respect and asked after his welfare, and said to him, "My lord, I am obliged and grateful to you for having shown favour to my brother's son; and I perceive that you regard him with an affection even fonder than my own."

Khwajah Hasan replied with pleasant words and said, "Your nephew greatly takes my fancy, and I am well pleased with him; for although he is young in years, Allah has endowed him with a great deal of wisdom."

So the two of them talked together in friendly conversation; and presently the guest rose to leave and said, "My lord, your servant must now say goodbye to you; but on some future day, God willing, he will wait upon you again."

Ali Baba, however, would not let him go, and asked, "Where are you off to, my friend? I would invite you to my table, and I beg you to sit at meat with us and afterwards go home in peace. Perhaps the dishes are not as delicate as those you are used to eating; still, be pleased to grant me this request, I beg you, and refresh yourself with my food."

Khwajah Hasan said, "My lord, I am beholden to you for your gracious invitation, and I would gladly sit at meat with you; but for a particular reason I must excuse myself. Allow me, then, to go, for I may not stay any longer nor accept your gracious offer."

To this the host replied, "I beg you, my lord, tell me what this reason may be, that is so urgent and weighty."

And Khwajah Hasan answered, "The cause is this: by order of the physician who lately cured me of my complaint, I must not eat any food prepared with salt."

Ali Baba said, "If that is all, do not deprive me, I beg you, of the honour your company will do me: since the dishes are not yet cooked, I will forbid the cook to use any salt at all. Stay here a while and I will come back to you presently."

So saying, Ali Baba went in to Morgiana and told her not to put salt into any one of the dishes; and she, busy with her cooking, fell to marvelling greatly at such an order and asked her master, "Who is this man who eats food with no salt in it?"

He answered, "What does it matter to you who he is? Just do as I tell you."

She replied, "Very well; everything shall be as you wish" — but in her mind she wondered at the man who made such a strange request, and very much wanted to get a look at him.

So when all the dishes were ready to be served up, she helped the slave boy Abdullah to lay the table and set out the meal; and no sooner did she see Khwajah Hasan than she knew who he was, although he had disguised himself in the dress of a foreign merchant. And furthermore, when she looked at him attentively she spotted a dagger hidden under his robe.

"So ho!" she said to herself. "This is the reason the villain does not eat salt — because he is looking for an opportunity to kill my master, whose mortal enemy he is. But I will be beforehand with him and finish him off before he finds a chance to harm my lord."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Thirty-eighth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Morgiana, having spread a white cloth on the table and served up the meal, went back to the kitchen and thought out her plot against the robber captain.

Now when Ali Baba and Khwajah Hasan had eaten their fill, the slave boy Abdullah brought Morgiana word to serve the dessert; and she cleared the table and set out fruit, fresh and dried, in dishes; then she placed beside Ali Baba a small stand with three cups and a flagon of wine; and lastly she went off with the slave boy Abdullah into another room, as though she meant to have her own supper.

Then Khwajah Hasan — that is, the captain of the robbers — perceiving that the coast was clear, exulted mightily, saying to himself, "The time has come for me to take my full revenge. With one thrust of my dagger I will finish this fellow off, then escape across the garden and go my way. His nephew will not venture to stay my hand; for if he so much as moves a finger or a toe with that intent, another stab will settle his earthly account. Still, I must wait a while until the slave boy and the cook maid have eaten and lain down to rest in the kitchen."

Morgiana, however, was watching him closely, and, guessing his purpose, said in her mind, "I must not let this villain get the advantage of my lord; and by some means I must make his plan come to nothing and put an end to his life at once."

So the trusty slave girl changed her clothes with all haste and put on the sort of dress that dancers wear; she veiled her face with a costly kerchief; round her head she bound a fine turban, and about her middle she tied a waist cloth worked with gold and silver, in which she stuck a dagger whose hilt was rich with filigree and jewels.

Disguised in this way, she said to the slave boy Abdullah, "Take your tambourine now, so that we may play and sing and dance in honour of our master's guest."

So he did as she told him, and the two of them went into the room, the lad playing and the girl following. Then, making a low bow, they asked leave to perform and entertain and play; and Ali Baba gave them permission, saying, "Dance now, and do your best, so that this guest of ours may be mirthful and merry."

Khwajah Hasan said, "My lord, you do indeed provide very pleasant entertainment."

Then the slave boy Abdullah, standing by, began to strike the tambourine, while Morgiana rose up and showed her perfect art and pleased them greatly with her graceful steps and her sportive motion; and suddenly, drawing the dagger from her belt, she brandished it and paced from side to side — a spectacle that pleased them most of all. At times, too, she stood in front of them, now clapping the sharp-edged dagger under her armpit and now setting it against her breast.

Lastly she took the tambourine from the slave boy Abdullah and, still holding the dagger in her right hand, went round for largesse, as is the custom among entertainers. First she stood in front of Ali Baba, who threw a gold coin into the tambourine, and his nephew put in a gold coin too; then Khwajah Hasan, seeing her about to come up to him, fell to pulling out his purse — when she heartened her heart and, quick as blinding lightning, plunged the dagger into his vitals, and the villain fell back on the instant, stone dead.

Ali Baba was dismayed and cried out in his anger, "Wretched girl, what is this deed you have done, to bring about my ruin!"

But she replied, "No, my lord — it was to save you and not to do you harm that I killed this man. Loosen his clothes and see what you find underneath."

So Ali Baba searched the dead man's dress and found a dagger hidden in it.

Then Morgiana said, "This wretch was your deadly enemy. Look at him well: he is none other than the oil merchant, the captain of the band of robbers. When he came here meaning to take your life, he would not eat your salt; and when you told me that he wanted none in the food I suspected him, and at first sight I was sure that he would certainly put you to death. Almighty Allah be praised — it is just as I thought."

Then Ali Baba lavished thanks and expressions of gratitude on her, saying, "See now, twice you have saved me from his hand"; and, falling on her neck, he cried, "You are free; and as a reward for this loyalty of yours, I have married you to my nephew."

Then, turning to the youth, he said, "Do as I tell you and you shall prosper. I want you to marry Morgiana, who is a model of duty and loyalty. You see now that this Khwajah Hasan sought your friendship only so that he might find an opportunity to take my life; but this girl, with her good sense and her wisdom, has killed him and saved us."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Thirty-ninth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Ali Baba's nephew consented at once to marry Morgiana.

After which the three of them raised the dead body and carried it out with all care and vigilance and buried it privately in the garden; and for many years nobody knew anything about it.

In due time Ali Baba married his brother's son to Morgiana with great pomp, and spread a wedding feast in the most sumptuous fashion for his friends and neighbours, and made merry with them and enjoyed singing and every kind of dancing and amusement.

He prospered in every undertaking, and Time smiled on him, and a new source of wealth was opened to him.

For fear of the thieves he had not once visited the cave in the jungle where the treasure lay, since the day he had carried out his brother Kasim's body. But some time afterwards he mounted his horse one morning and rode there with all care and caution; and, finding no sign of man or horse, and reassured in his mind, he ventured to go up to the door.

Then, dismounting from his beast, he tied it to a tree and, going to the entrance, spoke the words he had not forgotten: "Open, Sesame!"

At this, as it always did, the door flew open; and, going in by it, he saw the goods and the hoard of gold and silver untouched and lying just as he had left them. So he was assured that not one of all the thieves was left alive, and that apart from himself there was not a soul who knew the secret of the place.

At once he tied up in his saddlecloth a load of gold coins, as much as his horse could carry, and brought it home; and in later days he showed the hoard to his sons and his sons' sons, and taught them how the door could be made to open and shut.

So Ali Baba and his household lived all their lives in wealth and gladness in that city where once he had been a pauper; and by the blessing of that secret treasure he rose to high rank and dignity.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Fortieth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King — and so Shahrazad went on telling the King her tales, night after night, until the thousand nights and a night were fulfilled.
