The Tale of the Prince and the Ogress
(Part 1 of 2)

There was a certain King whose son was far too fond of hunting and coursing, and he ordered one of his viziers to attend on the boy wherever he went.

One day the young man set out for the chase with his father's minister; and as they jogged along together a big wild animal came into view.

"Up and after that fine quarry!" cried the vizier to the King's son.

So the prince went after it until he was out of everyone's sight, and the animal got away from him in the wilderness; and he was left confused, not knowing which way to turn. And then a girl appeared ahead of him, in tears.

"Who are you?" the King's son asked.

"I am the daughter of a king among the kings of India," she answered, "and I was travelling with a caravan through the desert when drowsiness came over me and I fell off my mount without knowing it. So I am cut off from my people and thoroughly lost."

The prince, hearing this, pitied her, and mounted her behind him on his horse's crupper; and they rode until they passed an old ruin, where the girl said, "Master, I need to relieve myself."

So he set her down at the ruin, and she took so long there that the King's son thought she was simply wasting time. So he followed her without her knowing it — and she was a ghulah, an ogress, and she was saying to her brood:

"Children, today I am bringing you a fine fat young man for dinner."

To which they answered, "Bring him quickly, mother, so that we can browse on him till our bellies are full."

The prince, hearing this talk, gave himself up for dead, and the muscles in his sides quivered in fear for his life; so he turned away and was about to run.

The ogress came out, and seeing him in such a fright — for he was trembling in every limb — cried, "What are you afraid of?"

"I have run into an enemy," he replied, "and I am very much afraid of him."

"Did you not tell me you were a King's son?" asked the ogress.

"I did," he answered.

"Then why not give this enemy of yours some money and satisfy him that way?"

"He will not be satisfied with my purse," he said, "only with my life. And I am mortally afraid of him, and I am a man being wronged."

"If you are as distressed as you think you are," she replied, "then ask Allah for help against him. He will certainly protect you from the man's mischief and from the evil you are afraid of."

Then the prince raised his eyes to heaven and cried, "You who answer the needy man when he calls on You, and drive his distress away — my God, grant me victory over my enemy, and turn him away from me, for You are Almighty over all things."

The ogress, hearing his prayer, turned away from him; and the prince went back to his father and told him the whole story about the vizier — at which the King summoned the minister into his presence and killed him then and there.

And you likewise, O King: if you go on trusting this doctor, you will be made to die the worst of deaths. The very man you have made so much of, and treated as an intimate, will bring about your destruction. Do you not see how he cured the disease from outside your body, with something gripped in your hand? Do not be so sure he will not destroy you with something held the same way.

"You have spoken truly, vizier," King Yunan replied. "It may well be as you hint, my good advising minister. Perhaps this sage has come as a spy, looking to put me to death — for if he certainly cured me with a something held in my hand, he can certainly kill me with a something given me to smell."

Then King Yunan asked, "Minister, what is to be done with him?"

And the vizier answered, "Send after him this very instant and summon him into your presence; and when he comes, strike him across the neck. So you will be rid of him and his wickedness, and will deceive him before he can deceive you."

"You have spoken truly again, vizier," said the King. And he sent a man to call the sage, who came in a cheerful mood, not knowing what the Compassionate One had appointed for him. As a certain poet says, by way of illustration:

	You who go in fear of Fate — travel trusting;
	commit the whole of it to Him who built the world, and wait.
	What Fate says "Be" to must be, my lord;
	and from what Fate has not decreed you are safe.

As Duban the physician came in, he addressed the King in these lines:

	If I fail of my thanks to you, and do not thank you day after day,
	then for whom did I ever put together prose and verse, my speech and my song?
	You lavished your generous gifts before ever I asked for them;
	you lavished your favours unasked, with no pretext and no delay.
	How shall I stint my praise of you, how shall I stop praising
	your graciousness, in private and in the most open show?
	No; I will thank your kindnesses, for your favours always lie
	light on my thought and my tongue, though they weigh heavy on my back.

And he said further, on the same theme:

	Turn away from grief and do not care a jot!
	Commit what you need to Fate and to your Lot.
	Enjoy the present, and enjoy it thoroughly,
	and let the past be entirely forgotten.
	For what happens to look like the worst
	shall work out for your good, as Allah knows.
	Allah will do whatever He wills,
	and in His will do not oppose Him.

And further still:

	To the All-wise, the Subtle One, trust the things of this world;
	rest yourself from everything the worldly man clings to.
	Learn well and wisely that nothing comes about by your will,
	but only as Allah wills it, the King of Kings.

And lastly:

	Be cheerful and glad, and forget every grief you have.
	Grief has worn out the wisest hearts often enough.
	Thinking is only folly in a feeble slave:
	shun it, and so be saved for ever.

The King's only reply was, "Do you know why I have sent for you?"

And the sage answered, "Allah Most High alone knows hidden things."

But the King retorted, "I sent for you only to take your life and destroy you utterly."

Duban the Wise wondered at this strange address beyond all wondering, and asked, "O King — and why would you kill me? What harm have I done you?"

"Men tell me you are a spy," the King answered, "sent here with the intention of killing me. And so I will kill you before I am killed by you." And he called to his headsman and said, "Strike me off this traitor's head and deliver us from his evil practices."

"Spare me and Allah will spare you," said the sage. "Do not kill me, or Allah will kill you." And he said those very words to him, ifrit, exactly as I said them to you — and yet you would not let me go, being bent on my death.

King Yunan only answered, "I shall not be safe unless I kill you; for just as you healed me with something held in the hand, so I am not secure against your killing me with something given me to smell, or by some other means."

"So this, O King," said the physician, "is my recompense and my reward? You return nothing but evil for good."

"There is no help for it," the King replied. "Die you must, and without delay."

Now when the physician was certain the King would kill him without waiting, he wept, and regretted the good he had done to the man who did not deserve it. As somebody has said on this subject:

	Maymunah is bare of wit and wisdom,
	whose own father outstrips every wit in wisdom.
	A man cannot walk on mud or dust or clay
	except by good sense — otherwise he trips and slips.

At that the headsman stepped forward and bound the sage Duban's eyes and bared his blade, saying to the King, "By your leave." And the physician wept and cried, "Spare me and Allah will spare you; do not kill me, or Allah will kill you," and began reciting:

	I was kind, and did not escape; they were cruel, and escaped;
	and my kindness led me nowhere but to the Hall of Ruin.
	If I live I will never be kind again; and if I die, then damnation
	on all who come after me, and a curse on their kindliness.

"Is this," Duban went on, "the return I get from you? It seems to me you are paying me the crocodile's kindness."

"What is the tale of the crocodile?" said the King.

"It is impossible for me to tell it in the state I am in," said the physician. "In Allah's name, spare me, as you hope Allah will spare you." And he wept beyond all weeping.

Then one of the King's favourites stood up and said, "O King, grant me this physician's blood. We have never seen him sin against you, or do anything but heal you of a disease that baffled every doctor and man of science."

"You do not know why I am putting this physician to death," said the King, "and it is this. If I spare him, I doom myself to certain death; for a man who healed me of such a malady with something held in my hand can certainly kill me with something held to my nose. And I am afraid he may kill me for a price, since he may well be a spy whose only purpose in coming here was to bring about my destruction. So there is no help for it. Die he must — and only then shall I be sure of my own life."

Again Duban cried, "Spare me and Allah will spare you; do not kill me, or Allah will kill you." But it was no use.

Now when the physician knew for certain that the King would kill him, ifrit, he said, "O King, if there is no help for it and I must die, then grant me a little delay, so that I can go down to my house and settle my obligations and tell my household and my neighbours where to bury me and how to distribute my medical books. Among them I have one that is the rarest of rarities, which I should like to present to you as a gift: keep it as a treasure in your treasury."

"And what is in the book?" asked the King.

"Things past counting," the sage answered. "And the least of its secrets is this: that if, directly after you have cut off my head, you open three leaves and read three lines of the page on your left hand, my head will speak, and answer every question you care to ask it."

The King wondered beyond all wondering, and shaking with delight at the strangeness of it, said, "Physician, are you really telling me that when I cut off your head it will speak to me?"

"Yes, O King," he replied.

"This is a strange matter indeed," said the King. And he sent him home at once under close guard, and Duban settled all his obligations then and there.

Next day he went up to the King's audience hall, where emirs and viziers, chamberlains and governors, grandees and lords of the realm were all gathered together, making the presence chamber as gay as a garden of flower beds. And the physician came up and stood before the King, carrying a worn old volume and a little metal case full of powder, like the powder used for the eyes.

Then he sat down and said, "Give me a tray." So they brought him one, and he poured the powder onto it and levelled it off, and at last he spoke:

"O King, take this book, but do not open it until my head falls. Then set the head on this tray and have it pressed down onto the powder, and at once the blood will stop flowing. That is the moment to open the book."

So the King took the book and made a sign to the headsman, who rose and struck off the physician's head and set it in the middle of the tray and pressed it down onto the powder. The blood stopped flowing; and the sage Duban opened his eyes and said, "Now open the book, O King."

The King opened the book and found the leaves stuck together; so he put his finger to his mouth, and by moistening it he managed to turn over the first leaf, and the second the same way, and the third, each leaf opening with a good deal of trouble. And when he had unstuck six leaves he looked them over and, finding nothing written on them, said, "Physician, there is no writing here."

"Turn over more," Duban replied. And he turned over three more in the same way.

Now the book was poisoned; and before long the venom worked into his system, and he fell into violent convulsions and cried out, "The poison has done its work!"

At which the sage Duban's head began to improvise:

	There have been rulers who ruled with a foul and tyrannical hand,
	and soon they were as though they had never, never been.
	Had they been just, they would have had justice done to them; they
	oppressed, and were oppressed in turn by Fortune, who paid them back
	with ruin and bane and misery. So they faded like the morning,
	and the tongue of things repeats: take this for that,
	and do not vent your spleen on the ways of Fortune.

No sooner had the head stopped speaking than the King rolled over dead.

Now I would have you know, ifrit, that if King Yunan had spared the sage Duban, Allah would have spared him; but he refused to do it and decreed his death, and so Allah killed him. And you too, ifrit — if you had spared me, Allah would have spared you:—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

Then Dunyazad said, "Sister, how pleasant your tale is, and how good to listen to — how sweet, and how welcome!"

She answered, "And what is this compared to what I could tell you tomorrow night, if I live and the King spares me?"

The King said to himself, "By Allah, I will not kill her until I have heard the rest of her story, for it is truly a wonder."

They rested that night in each other's arms until dawn. Then the King went out to his court; the viziers and the troops came in and the audience hall filled up; and the King gave his orders and gave judgement, appointed men and dismissed men, commanding and forbidding through the rest of that day, until the court broke up and King Shahryar went into his palace.

When it was the Sixth Night,

her sister Dunyazad said to her, "Please finish your story for us." And she answered, "I will, if the King gives me leave."

"Say on," said the King. And she went on:

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the fisherman said to the ifrit, "If you had spared me I would have spared you; but nothing would satisfy you except my death. So now I will put you to death by shutting you up in this jar, and I will hurl you into this sea."

Then the marid roared aloud and cried, "In Allah's name, fisherman — don't! Spare me, and pardon what I have done; and as I have been tyrannical, be you generous. For it is said, among the sayings that go about: You who do good to the man who has done you evil — let the evildoer's own evil deeds be enough for him. And do not deal with me as Umamah dealt with Atikah."

"And what happened between them?" asked the fisherman.

"This is no time for storytelling," the ifrit answered, "and me in this prison. Set me free and I will tell you the tale."

"Leave off this talk," said the fisherman. "There is no help for it: you must be thrown back into the sea, and there is no way of your ever getting out of it, for ever and ever. I put myself under your protection for nothing; I humbled myself to you in tears, and all you wanted was to kill me — me, who had done you no injury to deserve it. Far from injuring you by any evil act, I did you nothing but good in releasing you from that prison of yours.

"Now I knew you for an evildoer when you did to me what you did. And know this: when I have thrown you back into the sea, I shall warn whoever fishes you up about what happened to me with you, and I shall advise him to toss you straight back in again. So you shall stay here under these waters until the End of Time makes an end of you."

But the ifrit cried out, "Set me free! This is a fine occasion for generosity, and I make a covenant with you and vow never to do you hurt or harm. On the contrary, I will help you to something that will put you beyond want."

The fisherman accepted his promises on both conditions — that he should not trouble him as before, but on the contrary do him a service; and after making the pledge firm and swearing him a solemn oath by Allah Most High, he opened the jar.

At that the pillar of smoke rose up until the whole of it was out; and then it thickened and once more became an ifrit, hideous to look at, who promptly gave the bottle a kick and sent it flying into the sea.

The fisherman, seeing how the jar was treated and making sure his own death was next, wet himself, and said to himself, "This looks bad." But he steadied his heart and cried:

"Ifrit, Allah has said: keep your covenant, for the keeping of your covenant shall be inquired into hereafter. You have made a vow to me and sworn an oath not to play me false, in case Allah plays you false — for truly He is a jealous God, who gives the sinner time but does not let him escape. I say to you what the sage Duban said to King Yunan: spare me, so that Allah may spare you."

The ifrit burst out laughing and stalked away, saying to the fisherman, "Follow me." And the man walked after him at a safe distance, not being at all sure of getting away, until they had gone right round the outskirts of the city.

From there they struck out into uncultivated ground, and crossed it, and came down into a broad wilderness; and in the middle of it stood a mountain tarn.

The ifrit waded into the middle of it and again cried, "Follow me." And when that was done he took his stand in the centre and told the man to cast his net and catch his fish.

The fisherman looked into the water and was astonished to see fish of different colours in it, white and red, blue and yellow. But he cast his net, and hauling it in, saw he had netted four fish, one of each colour.

He was overjoyed at it, and more so when the ifrit said, "Carry these to the Sultan and set them in front of him; and he will give you what will make you a wealthy man. And now accept my apology, for by Allah I know no other way of doing you good at present, seeing that I have lain in this sea for eighteen hundred years and have not seen the face of the world until this hour. But I would not have you fish here more than once a day."

Then the ifrit wished him well, saying, "Allah grant we meet again," and struck the ground with one foot; and the earth split open and swallowed him up.

The fisherman, marvelling a great deal at what had happened to him with the ifrit, took the fish and made for the city; and as soon as he got home he filled an earthenware bowl with water and dropped the fish into it, and they began to struggle and wriggle about.

Then he carried the bowl off on his head and went to the King's palace, as the ifrit had told him to, and laid the fish before the presence. And the King marvelled beyond all marvelling at the sight, for never in his life had he seen fish like these, in colour or in shape.

So he said, "Give those fish to the foreign slave girl who is cooking for us now" — meaning the bondmaid whom the King of Rum had sent him only three days before, so that he had not yet tested her talents in the dressing of meat.

At which the vizier carried the fish to the cook and told her to fry them, saying, "Girl, the King sends you this message: I have not stored you up, O treasure of mine, except against a hard time of mine. So show us today your delicate handiwork and your savoury cooking; for this dish of fish is a present sent to the Sultan, and plainly a rarity."

The vizier, having instructed her carefully, went back to the King, who ordered him to give the fisherman four hundred dinars. He gave them accordingly, and the man clutched them to his chest and ran off home, stumbling and falling and getting up again and thinking the whole thing was a dream. Anyway, he bought his family everything they needed, and at last he went in to his wife in enormous joy and gladness.

So much for him. As for the cook, she took the fish and cleaned them and set them in the frying pan, basting them with oil until one side was done. Then she turned them over — and the kitchen wall split apart, and out of it came a young lady, beautiful in shape, oval in the face, perfect in grace, with eyelids that lines of kohl enchase.

Her dress was a silk head-kerchief fringed and tasselled with blue; a large ring hung from each ear; a pair of bracelets went round her wrists; there were rings with priceless gems in their settings on her fingers; and she held in her hand a long rod of rattan cane, which she thrust into the frying pan, saying, "Fish! Fish! Are you keeping your covenant?"
