Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves
(Part 1 of 3)

In days of old, and in times and tides long gone before, there lived in a certain town of Persia two brothers, one named Kasim and the other Ali Baba, who at their father's death had divided the little wealth he left them fairly between them, and had lost no time in wasting and spending all of it.

The elder, however, presently took a wife, the daughter of a wealthy merchant; so that when his father-in-law went to the mercy of Almighty Allah he became the owner of a large shop full of rare goods and costly wares, and of a storehouse stocked with precious stuffs, and likewise of a great deal of gold buried in the ground. And so he was known throughout the city as a man of substance.

But the woman Ali Baba had married was poor and needy; so they lived in a mean hovel, and Ali Baba scraped a scanty living by selling firewood, which he gathered every day in the jungle and carried about the town to the bazaar on his three donkeys.

Now it happened one day that Ali Baba had cut as many dead branches and as much dry fuel as he needed, and had put the load on his beasts, when suddenly he saw a cloud of dust spiralling high in the air to his right and moving rapidly towards him; and when he looked at it closely he made out a troop of horsemen riding hard and about to reach him.

At this sight he was badly alarmed; and, fearing that they might perhaps be a band of robbers who would kill him and drive off his donkeys, in his fright he began to run. But since they were close at hand and he could not get out of the forest, he drove his animals with their loads of fuel into a side path among the bushes and swarmed up the thick trunk of a huge tree to hide himself in it; and he sat on a branch from which he could see everything below him, while nobody below could catch a glimpse of him above; and that tree grew close beside a rock that towered high overhead.

The horsemen — young, active, hardy riders — came right up to the rock face and all dismounted; at which Ali Baba took good note of them, and he was soon quite persuaded, by their look and their manner, that they were a troop of highwaymen who had fallen on a caravan and robbed it and carried off the spoil, and had brought their booty to this place meaning to hide it safely in some cache.

Moreover he observed that there were forty of them.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Twenty-seventh Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Ali Baba saw the robbers, as soon as they came under the tree, each unbridle his horse and hobble it; and then all of them took off their saddlebags, which proved to be full of gold and silver.

The man who seemed to be the captain presently pushed forward, load on shoulder, through thorns and thickets, until he came to a certain spot, where he spoke these strange words: "Open, Sesame!"

And immediately a wide doorway appeared in the face of the rock. The robbers went in, and last of all their chief, and then the door shut of itself.

They stayed inside the cave a long while, and Ali Baba was obliged to stay perched up in the tree, reflecting that if he came down the band might perhaps come out that very moment and seize him and kill him. At last he had determined to mount one of the horses and, driving on his donkeys, to head back to town — when suddenly the door flew open.

The robber chief was the first to come out; then, standing at the entrance, he watched and counted his men as they came out; and lastly he spoke the magic words, "Shut, Sesame!" — whereupon the door closed of itself.

When they had all passed muster and review, each slung on his saddlebags and bridled his own horse; and as soon as they were ready they rode off, led by their leader, in the direction they had come from.

Ali Baba stayed perched in the tree and watched them go; nor would he come down until they were completely out of sight, in case one of them should come back and look around and spot him.

Then he thought to himself, "I too will try the virtue of those magic words, and see whether the door will open and close at my bidding." So he called out loudly, "Open, Sesame!"

And no sooner had he spoken than the door flew open at once, and he went inside.

He saw a large vaulted cavern, as high as the height of a full-grown man, hewn out of the living stone and lit by light that came through air holes and skylights in the upper surface of the rock that formed the roof. He had expected to find nothing but outer darkness in this robbers' den, and he was surprised to see the whole room filled with bales of every kind of stuff, and heaped from floor to ceiling with camel-loads of silks and brocades and embroidered cloths, and mound upon mound of many-coloured carpets; besides which he saw coins of gold and silver beyond measure or reckoning, some piled on the ground and others tied up in leather bags and sacks.

Seeing these goods and moneys in such abundance, Ali Baba decided in his mind that not for a few years only, but for many generations, thieves must have been storing their gains and their spoils in this place.

When he was standing inside the cave the door had closed behind him; and yet he was not dismayed, since he had kept the magic words in his memory. And he took no notice of the precious stuffs around him, but applied himself only and entirely to the sacks of gold coins. Of these he carried out as many as he judged a sufficient load for his beasts; then he loaded them on his animals and covered the plunder with sticks and firewood, so that nobody might see the bags, but might think he was carrying home his usual goods.

Last of all he called out, "Shut, Sesame!" — and immediately the door closed; for the spell worked in such a way that whenever anybody went into the cave the door shut of itself behind him, and when he came out of it, it would neither open nor close again until he had pronounced the words.

Presently, having loaded his donkeys, Ali Baba drove them ahead of him with all speed to the city; and reaching home he drove them into the yard and, shutting the outer door fast, took down first the sticks and then the bags of gold, which he carried in to his wife.

She felt them, and finding them full of coin suspected that Ali Baba had been robbing, and fell to scolding and blaming him for doing such an ill thing.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Twenty-eighth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Ali Baba said to his wife, "Indeed I am no robber; you had far better rejoice with me at our good fortune."

At this he told her of his adventure and began to pour the gold out of the bags in heaps before her; and her sight was dazzled by the sheen of it and her heart delighted by his story and his adventures. Then she began counting the gold; at which Ali Baba said, "Silly woman, how long will you go on turning over the coin? Let me dig a hole now to hide this treasure in, so that nobody may learn the secret of it."

She said, "Your advice is right! Still, I should like to weigh the money and have some idea of the amount of it."

And he replied, "As you please — but see that you tell nobody."

So she hurried off to Kasim's house to borrow weights and scales with which she might balance the gold coins and make some reckoning of their value; and when she could not find Kasim she said to his wife, "Lend me your scales, I beg you, for a moment."

Her sister-in-law replied, "Do you need the bigger balance, or the smaller?"

And the other answered, "I do not need the large scales; give me the little ones."

And her sister-in-law cried, "Wait here a moment while I look about and find what you want."

With this excuse Kasim's wife went aside and secretly smeared wax and suet over the pan of the balance, so that she might learn what it was that Ali Baba's wife wanted to weigh; for she felt sure that whatever it might be, some bit of it would stick to the wax and the fat. So the woman took this chance to satisfy her curiosity; and Ali Baba's wife, suspecting nothing of it, carried the scales home and began to weigh the gold, while Ali Baba went on digging; and when the money had been weighed, the two of them stowed it into the hole, which they carefully filled up with earth.

Then the good wife took the scales back to her kinswoman, not knowing at all that a gold coin had stuck to the cup of the scales; but when Kasim's wife spotted the gold coin she seethed with envy and anger, saying to herself, "So ho! They borrowed my balance to weigh out gold coins?" And she marvelled greatly where a man as poor as Ali Baba could have got such a store of wealth that he had to weigh it with a pair of scales.

Now after pondering the matter a long while, when her husband came home at evening she said to him, "Husband, you think yourself a man of wealth and substance; but look — your brother Ali Baba is an emir beside you, and far richer than you are. He has such heaps of gold that he has to weigh his money out with scales, while you, forsooth, are satisfied to count your coin."

"How do you know this?" asked Kasim.

And in answer his wife told him all about the pair of scales, and how she had found a gold coin stuck to them, and showed him the coin, which bore the mark and inscription of some ancient king.

Kasim got no sleep at all that night, out of envy and jealousy and covetousness; and next morning he got up early and, going to Ali Baba, said, "Brother, to all appearances you are poor and needy; but in fact you have a store of wealth so abundant that you are obliged to weigh your gold with scales."

Ali Baba said, "What is this you are saying? I do not understand you; make your meaning plain."

And Kasim said, with ready anger, "Do not pretend to be ignorant of what I am saying, and do not think to deceive me." Then, showing him the gold coin, he cried, "You have laid by thousands of gold coins like this one; and meanwhile my wife found this one stuck to the cup of the scales."

Then Ali Baba understood that both Kasim and his wife knew he had a store of gold, and said in his mind that it would do him no good to keep the matter hidden, but would rather cause ill-will and mischief; and so he was induced to tell his brother every bit of it, about the bandits and about the treasure found in the cave.

When he had heard the story, Kasim exclaimed, "I want you to tell me exactly where you found the money; and the magic words too, by which the door opened and closed. And I warn you: if you do not tell me the whole truth, I will give notice of those gold coins to the governor, and then you will forfeit all your wealth and be disgraced and thrown into gaol."

Thereupon Ali Baba told him his tale, not forgetting the magic words; and Kasim, who kept careful note of all these matters, set out the next day driving ten mules he had hired, and readily found the place Ali Baba had described to him.

And when he came to the rock and the tree Ali Baba had hidden himself in, and had made sure of the door, he cried in great joy, "Open, Sesame!"

The door yawned wide at once, and Kasim went in and saw the piles of jewels and treasures ranged all around; and as soon as he was standing among them the door shut behind him, as it always did.

He walked about in ecstasy, marvelling at the treasures; and when he was weary of admiring he gathered together bags of gold coins, a sufficient load for his ten mules, and put them by the entrance ready to be carried outside and loaded on the beasts.

But by the will of Almighty Allah he had clean forgotten the magic words, and cried out, "Open, Barley!" — whereupon the door refused to move.

Astonished and confused beyond measure, he named the names of every kind of grain except sesame, which had slipped out of his memory as though he had never heard the word; and in his dire distress he paid no attention to the gold coins heaped at the entrance, but paced to and fro, backwards and forwards, inside the cave, sorely puzzled and perplexed. The wealth whose sight had a little while before filled his heart with joy and gladness was now the cause of bitter grief and sadness.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Twenty-ninth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Kasim gave up all hope of the life he had put in such peril by his greed and his envy.

It happened that at noon the robbers, coming back that way, saw from a distance some mules standing beside the entrance, and they marvelled much at what had brought the beasts to that place; for, since Kasim had by mischance failed to tether or hobble them, they had strayed about the jungle and were browsing here and there.

However, the thieves paid little attention to the strays and did not care to secure them, but only wondered by what means they had wandered so far out of the town. Then, reaching the cave, the captain and his troop dismounted and, going up to the door, repeated the formula, and at once it flew open.

Now Kasim had heard from inside the cave the hooves of the horses drawing nearer and nearer, and he had fallen to the ground in a fit of fear, never doubting that this was the clatter of the bandits, who would kill him without fail. Even so, he presently took heart of grace, and at the moment when the door flew open he rushed out, hoping to make good his escape.

But the unhappy man ran full tilt into the captain, who was standing in front of the band, and knocked him to the ground; whereupon a robber standing near his chief at once bared his sword and with one cut clove Kasim clean in two.

Thereupon the robbers rushed into the cavern and put back where they had been the bags of gold that Kasim had heaped up at the doorway ready for taking away; nor did they take any notice of the ones Ali Baba had removed, so dazed and amazed were they to discover by what means the strange man had got in.

They all knew it was not possible for anybody to drop through the skylights, so tall and steep was the rock's face and so slippery to climb; and also that nobody could come in by the door unless he knew the magic words that opened it.

However, they presently quartered Kasim's dead body and hung it up on the door inside the cavern, two parts to the right jamb and two to the left, so that the sight might be a warning of coming doom to anybody who dared to enter the cave. Then, coming out, they shut the door of the hoard and rode away about their usual work.

Now when night fell and Kasim did not come home, his wife grew uneasy and, running round to Ali Baba, said, "Brother, Kasim has not come back: you know where he went, and I am sorely afraid some misfortune has come to him."

Ali Baba also guessed that some mishap had happened to prevent his return; but nonetheless he tried to comfort his sister-in-law with cheering words, and said, "Wife of my brother, very likely Kasim is being cautious and, avoiding the city, is coming by a roundabout road and will be here soon. That, I do believe, is the reason he is so long."

At this, comforted in her spirits, Kasim's wife went home and sat waiting for her husband's return; but when half the night was gone and still he had not come, she was like one out of her mind. She was afraid to cry aloud in her grief, in case the neighbours should hear her and come and learn the secret; so she wept in silence, and, reproaching herself, fell to thinking, "Why did I tell him this secret, and breed envy and jealousy of Ali Baba? This is the fruit of it, and this is the disaster that has come down on me."

She spent the rest of the night in bitter tears, and early next morning hurried in the greatest haste to Ali Baba and begged him to go out in search of his brother; so he tried to console her and set out at once with his donkeys for the forest.

Presently, reaching the rock, he wondered to see stains of freshly shed blood; and, not finding his brother or the ten mules, he foresaw a calamity from so evil a sign. Then he went to the door and, saying, "Open, Sesame!", pushed in and saw Kasim's dead body, two parts hanging to the right and the rest to the left of the entrance.

Although he was frightened beyond all measure of fright, he wrapped the quarters in two cloths and laid them on one of his donkeys, hiding them carefully with sticks and firewood so that nobody should see them. Then he put the bags of gold on the two other animals and covered them just as carefully; and when everything was ready he closed the cave door with the magic words and set off homewards with all care and watchfulness.

The donkeys with the load of gold he handed over to his wife, telling her to bury the bags diligently; but he did not tell her the condition he had found his brother Kasim in.

Then he went with the other donkey — the one with the body on its back — to the widow's house and knocked gently at the door.

Now Kasim had a slave girl, shrewd and sharp-witted, named Morgiana. She undid the bolt just as softly and let Ali Baba and the donkey into the courtyard of the house, where he let the body down from the beast's back and said, "Morgiana, hurry and make ready to perform the rites for your master's burial. I am going now to tell the news to your mistress, and I will come back quickly to help you in the business."

At that moment Kasim's widow, seeing her brother-in-law, exclaimed, "Ali Baba, what news do you bring me of my husband? Alas, I see the tokens of grief written on your face. Tell me quickly what has happened."

Then he recounted to her how it had gone with her husband, and how he had been killed by the robbers, and in what way he had brought the dead body home.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Six Hundred and Thirtieth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Ali Baba went on: "My lady, what was to happen has happened; but we must keep this matter secret, for our lives depend upon its being kept private."

She wept bitterly and answered, "It has gone with my husband according to the decree of Fate; and now, for your own safety's sake, I give you my word to keep the affair concealed."

He replied, "Nothing can avail when Allah has decreed. Rest patiently until the days of your widowhood are accomplished; after which time I will take you to wife, and you shall live in comfort and happiness; and have no fear that my first wife will vex you or show any jealousy, for she is kindly and tender-hearted."

The widow, lamenting her loss noisily, cried, "Let it be just as you please."

Then Ali Baba took his leave of her, weeping and wailing for her husband; and, joining Morgiana, took counsel with her about how to manage his brother's burial. So, after much consultation and many warnings, he left the slave girl and went off home, driving his donkey before him.

As soon as Ali Baba had gone, Morgiana went quickly to a druggist's shop; and, so as to deceive him the better and not make the matter known, she asked him for a drug often given to men who are ill with a dangerous fever. He gave it, saying, "Who is there in your house so ill as to need this medicine?"

And she said, "My master Kasim is sick almost to death: for many days he has neither spoken nor tasted any food, so that we almost despair of his life."

Next day Morgiana went again and asked the druggist for more medicine and for the essences that are given to the sick when they are at death's door, so that a dying man may perhaps rally before his last breath. The man gave her the potion, and she, taking it, sighed aloud and wept, saying, "I am afraid he may not have the strength to drink this draught: I think everything will be over with him before I get back to the house."

Meanwhile Ali Baba was anxiously waiting to hear the sounds of wailing and lamentation in Kasim's house, so that at that signal he might hurry there and take part in the funeral ceremonies.

Early on the second day Morgiana went with her face veiled to one Baba Mustafa, a tailor well on in years whose trade was making shrouds and grave clothes; and as soon as she saw him open his shop she gave him a gold piece and said, "Bind a bandage over your eyes and come along with me."

Mustafa acted as though he would not go; whereupon Morgiana put a second gold coin in his palm and begged him to come with her. The tailor presently consented, for greed of gain; so, tying a kerchief tightly over his eyes, she led him by the hand to the house where her master's dead body lay.

Then, taking off the bandage in the darkened room, she told him to sew the quarters of the corpse together, limb to limb; and, throwing a cloth over the body, said to the tailor, "Make haste and sew a shroud to the size of this dead man, and I will give you yet another gold piece for it."

Baba Mustafa quickly made the grave cloth of the right length and breadth, and Morgiana paid him the promised coin; then, bandaging his eyes once more, she led him back to the place she had brought him from.

After this she hurried home and, with Ali Baba's help, washed the body in warm water and, putting on the shroud, laid the corpse in a clean place ready for burial.

This done, Morgiana went to the mosque and gave notice to an imam that a funeral was waiting for the mourners at a certain house, and begged him to come and read the prayers for the dead; and the imam went back with her.

Then four neighbours took up the bier and carried it on their shoulders and set out with the imam and the others who were used to helping at such funerals. After the funeral prayers were ended, four other men carried off the coffin; and Morgiana walked in front of it bare-headed, striking her breast and weeping and wailing with exceedingly loud lamentation, while Ali Baba and the neighbours came behind.

In this order they came into the cemetery and buried him; and then, leaving him to Munkar and Nakir, the Questioners of the Dead, they all went their ways.

Presently the women of the quarter, according to the custom of the city, gathered together in the house of mourning and sat an hour with Kasim's widow, comforting her and condoling with her, and presently left her somewhat resigned and cheered.

Ali Baba stayed forty days at home in ceremonial mourning for the loss of his brother; so that nobody in the town except himself and his wife and Kasim's widow and Morgiana knew anything about the secret.

And when the forty days of mourning were over, Ali Baba moved all the dead man's property to his own quarters and openly married the widow; and then he appointed his nephew, his brother's eldest son — who had lived a long time with a wealthy merchant and was thoroughly knowledgeable in all matters of trade, in selling and buying — to take charge of the dead man's shop and carry on the business.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.
