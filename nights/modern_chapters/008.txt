The Tale of the Wazir and the Sage Duban

Know, ifrit, that in the old days, and in the old time before them, a king called Yunan ruled over the city of Fars in the land of Rum. He was a powerful ruler and a rich one, with armies and guards and allies among every nation of men; but his body was afflicted with a leprosy that the doctors and the men of science could not heal. He drank potions and swallowed powders and rubbed on ointments, and none of it did him any good, and not one of the whole host of physicians could manage a cure.

At last there came to his city a great healer of men, well on in years, whose name was the sage Duban. This man was a reader of books — Greek, Persian, Roman, Arabic and Syriac. He was skilled in astronomy and in medicine, the theory as well as the practice; he knew everything that heals the body and everything that harms it; he was familiar with the properties of every plant and grass and herb, what good it did and what damage; and he understood philosophy, and had gone round the whole range of medical science and of the other branches on the tree of knowledge.

This physician had spent only a few days in the city before he heard of the King's illness and all the suffering the leprosy caused him, and how every doctor and wise man had failed to heal him. So he sat up through the night thinking hard; and when the dawn broke and the morning appeared and the light was born again, and the sun greeted the good, whose beauty adorns the world, he put on his handsomest clothes and went in to King Yunan and kissed the ground before him. Then he prayed in the finest language for the long continuance of his honour and his prosperity, and made himself known.

"O King," he said, "news has reached me of what has befallen you in your own body, and of how the whole host of physicians have proved unable to reduce it. And I can cure you, O King — and I will not make you drink a draught or rub you with any ointment."

Now when King Yunan heard this he said, in enormous surprise, "How will you do it? By Allah, if you make me whole I will make you rich, and your son's sons after you, and I will give you magnificent presents; and whatever you wish for shall be yours, and you shall be my drinking companion and my friend."

The King then robed him in a dress of honour and treated him graciously, and asked, "Can you really cure me of this complaint without drug or ointment?"

"I can," he answered. "I will heal you without the pains and penalties of medicine."

The King marvelled beyond all marvelling, and said, "Physician, when will this be that you speak of, and in how many days will it happen? Hurry, my son!"

"I hear and I obey," he replied. "The cure will begin tomorrow."

And so saying he went out of the presence, and rented a house in the city so as to have somewhere better to keep his books and scrolls, his medicines and his aromatic roots. Then he set to work choosing the fittest drugs and simples; and he made a polo mallet, hollow inside and fitted with a handle outside, and he made a ball to go with it, and both were prepared with consummate art.

Next day, when the two of them were ready for use and wanted nothing more, he went up to the King and kissed the ground between his hands and told him to ride out onto the parade ground and play polo. The King went, and his suite went with him — emirs and chamberlains, viziers and lords of the realm. And before he had even taken his seat, the sage Duban came up to him and handed him the mallet.

"Take this mallet," he said, "and grip it the way I do — so. And now push out onto the field, and lean well over your horse, and drive the ball with all your might until your palm is damp and your body sweats. Then the medicine will pass through your palm and go all through you. When you have finished playing, and can feel the medicine working, go back to your palace, and wash yourself completely at the baths, and lie down to sleep; and so you will be made whole. And now, peace be with you."

At that King Yunan took the mallet from the sage and gripped it firmly. Then he mounted, and drove the ball before him, and galloped after it until he caught it up, and struck it with all his strength, his palm gripping the mallet handle all the while; and he did not stop knocking the ball about until his hand was damp and his skin, sweating, took the medicine in out of the wood.

Then the sage Duban knew the drugs had gone through him, and told him to go back to the palace and get into the baths at once, without any delay. So King Yunan went straight back and gave orders to clear the baths for him. They did it — the carpet-spreaders hurrying and the slaves hurrying — and got out a change of clothes for the King. He went in and washed himself long and thoroughly; then he dressed inside the bathhouse, and rode from there to his palace, and lay down, and slept.

So much for King Yunan. As for the sage Duban, he went home and slept as usual, and when morning broke he went to the palace and asked for an audience. The King ordered him admitted; and then, having kissed the ground between his hands, he recited these lines about the King, speaking them solemnly:

	Eloquence is happy when you are named as her father,
	but she goes into mourning when any other man claims the title.
	Lord of the fairest face, whose shining rays
	clear off the fogs of doubt that hang over famous deeds —
	may your face never stop shining like the dawn and the sunrise,
	and may you never show Time a face hot with anger.
	Your favour has given us gifts that did such work in us
	as rain clouds do raining on hills that frame the open country.
	Freely you spent your wealth so as to rise high,
	until you won from Time the very heights your greatness aimed at.

Now when the sage had finished reciting, the King got quickly to his feet and fell on his neck; then, seating him at his side, he ordered him dressed in a magnificent robe. For it had happened that when the King came out of the baths he looked at his body and saw no trace of the leprosy: the skin was all as clean as new silver. He was overjoyed at it, and his chest opened with delight, and he felt thoroughly happy.

Presently, when it was full day, he came into his audience hall and sat on the throne of his kingship, and his chamberlains and great men flocked into the presence, and the sage Duban with them. Seeing the doctor, the King rose to honour him and seated him at his side; and then the food trays were brought in loaded with the choicest dishes, and the physician ate with the King, and stayed in his company all that day.

What is more, at nightfall he gave the physician Duban two thousand gold pieces, besides the usual robe of honour and a great many other gifts, and sent him home on his own horse.

After the sage had gone out, King Yunan said again how astonished he was at the doctor's art. "This man treated my body from the outside," he said, "and never rubbed me with any ointment at all. By Allah, this is nothing short of perfect skill. I am bound to honour a man like that with rewards and distinctions, and take him as my companion and my friend for the rest of my days."

So King Yunan passed the night in joy and gladness, because his body had been made whole and had thrown off so destructive a disease.

Next morning the King came out from his private apartments and sat on his throne, and the lords of the realm stood about him, and the emirs and viziers sat as usual on his right hand and his left. Then he asked for the sage Duban, who came in and kissed the ground before him; and the King rose to greet him and seated him at his side, and ate with him and wished him a long life. What is more he robed him again and gave him presents, and did not stop talking with him until the evening drew on. Then the King ordered him, by way of salary, five robes of honour and a thousand dinars. And the physician went home to his own house full of gratitude to the King.

Now when the next morning came the King went to his audience hall, and his lords and nobles gathered round him, and his chamberlains and his ministers, the way the white of the eye encloses the black.

Now the King had a vizier among his viziers who was unpleasant to look at, an ill-omened sight, mean and ungenerous, full of envy and ill will. When this minister saw the King put the physician next to him and give him all these presents, he grew jealous of him and planned to do him harm — as the saying on the subject goes, "Envy lurks in everybody"; and as the other saying goes, "Oppression is hidden in every heart: power reveals it and weakness conceals it."

So the minister came before the King and kissed the ground between his hands and said, "O King of the age and of all time, in whose kindness I grew to manhood — I have serious advice to offer you, and if I hold it back I am a bastard and no true-born man. So if you order me to disclose it, I will do it at once."

The King, who was troubled at the way the minister put it, said, "And what is this advice of yours?"

"O glorious monarch," he said, "the wise men of old said: whoever does not consider how a thing ends will not have Fortune for a friend. And indeed I have lately seen the King going down a road that is anything but the right one; for he is lavishing gifts on his enemy, on a man whose whole object is the downfall and ruin of his kingship. To this man he has shown favour, honouring him beyond all honouring and making an intimate of him. And so I fear for the King's life."

The King, much troubled, and changing colour, asked, "Who is it you suspect, and who is it you are hinting at?"

And the minister answered, "O King, if you are asleep, wake up! I am pointing at the physician Duban."

"Shame on you," the King retorted. "This is a true friend, whom I favour above all men, because he cured me with something I held in my own hand, and healed the leprosy that had baffled every physician there is. He is a man whose like is not to be found these days — not in the whole world, from the furthest east to the furthest west. And it is of such a man that you say such hard things.

"Now from this day forward I allot him a fixed salary and allowance, a thousand gold pieces every month; and if I were to share my kingdom with him it would be a small enough matter. I cannot help suspecting that you speak this way out of nothing but envy and jealousy — as they tell of King Sindibad":—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

Then Dunyazad said, "Sister, how pleasant your tale is, and how good to listen to — how sweet, and how welcome!"

She answered, "And what is this compared to what I could tell you tomorrow night, if the King is pleased to spare my life?"

Then the King said to himself, "By Allah, I will not kill her until I have heard the rest of her tale, for it is truly a wonder."

So they rested that night in each other's arms until dawn. Then the King went out to his Hall of Rule, and the vizier and the troops came in, and the audience chamber filled up; and the King gave his orders and gave judgement, appointed men and dismissed men, commanding and forbidding through the rest of that day, until the court broke up and King Shahryar went back to his palace.
