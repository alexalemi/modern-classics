The Fisherman and the Jinni

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that there was once a fisherman well on in years, who had a wife and three children and was in poor circumstances. It was his habit to cast his net four times a day, and no more.

One day he went out about noon to the seashore and set down his basket; and tucking up his shirt and wading into the water, he made a cast with his net and waited for it to settle on the bottom. Then he gathered the cords together and hauled — and found it heavy. However hard he pulled towards the shore, he could not get it up. So he carried the ends of it up the beach and drove a stake into the ground and made the net fast to it. Then he stripped and dived into the water all round the net, and did not stop working at it until he had brought it up.

He was delighted, and put his clothes back on and went to the net — and found in it a dead donkey which had torn the meshes.

When he saw it he cried out in his grief, "There is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah the Glorious, the Great!" And then he said, "This is a strange way of earning a day's bread." And he began making up verses on the spot:

	You who work through the dark of the night in danger and in pain —
	the work you do for a day's bread does not come by strength or effort.
	Do you not see the fisherman going out on the sea after his bread
	while the stars of night glimmer overhead like a tangled skein?
	Down he goes into the water, and the waves knock him about,
	and all the while his eyes strain to see the net belly out;
	till, glad of a good night's work, he carries home a fish
	whose throat the hook of Fate had caught and cut in two.
	And a man buys that fish from him — a man who spent the night hours
	warm and comfortable, out of the cold and the wet and the dark.
	Praise to the Lord who gives to this one and denies that one his wish,
	and dooms one man to do the work and catch it, and another to eat it.

Then he said, "Up and get on with it. I am certain of His generosity, if Allah wills." And he went on:

	When Evil Fate has hold of you, put on
	the long patience of a noble soul: it is your best.
	Do not complain to your fellow creatures; that is complaining
	from the most merciful of them to the least merciful of all.

The fisherman, having looked at the dead donkey, got it free of the tangle and wrung out his net and spread it; then he waded into the sea saying, "In Allah's name!" and made a cast and pulled — and it grew heavy, and settled down even more firmly than the first time.

Now he thought there must be fish in it, so he made it fast, and took off his clothes and went into the water and dived and hauled until he had dragged it up onto dry land. And this time he found in it a large earthenware pitcher full of sand and mud.

Seeing this he was greatly upset, and began reciting:

	Leave off, troubles of the world —
	and if you will not leave off, then forgive.
	I went out to look for my day's bread
	and I find I must go breadless.
	Neither does my craft bring me anything
	nor does Fate allot me a share.
	How many fools reach up to the Pleiades
	while the dark swallows the wise and the careful.

So he begged Allah's pardon and threw the jar away, and wrung out his net and cleaned it, and went back to the sea a third time to cast, and waited until it had sunk. Then he pulled it in and found in it broken crockery and broken glass; at which he began to speak these lines:

	Your daily bread is His to give: you can neither tie it up nor loose it,
	and neither pen nor writing will help you find it.
	Joy and daily bread are what Fate condescends to allow;
	this is sad and barren ground, while that makes the farmer glad.
	The arrows of Time and Life bring down a great many worthy men
	while they lift up to high position men of no worth at all.
	So come, Death — for truly life is not worth a straw
	when the falcon falls low and the mallard rides the wind.
	It is no wonder to see the great in soul and mind
	go poor, while many a worthless clown is marked out for luck.
	This bird will fly over the whole world from east to furthest west,
	and that one gets every wish it has without leaving the nest.

Then he raised his eyes to heaven and said, "My God, You know very well that I cast my net only four times a day. The third is done, and so far You have granted me nothing. So this time, my God, be pleased to give me my daily bread."

Then, having called on Allah's name, he threw his net again and waited for it to sink and settle; and then hauled at it, and could not draw it in, because it had caught fast on the bottom. He cried out in his vexation, "There is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah!" and began reciting:

	Curse this wretched world, if this is how it is —
	that I must be swamped by grief and misery.
	Though a man's lot look cheerful when the morning breaks,
	he drains the cup of sorrow before he sees the evening.
	And yet I was the very man of whom the world, when asked
	whose lot was happiest, would say: his.

At that he stripped and dived down to the net and worked away at it until he got it to land. Then he opened the meshes — and found in it a jar shaped like a cucumber, made of yellow copper, plainly full of something, its mouth closed with a lead cap stamped with the seal ring of our lord Solomon, son of David, may Allah accept them both.

At the sight of it the fisherman was delighted, and said, "If I sell this in the brass market it is worth ten gold dinars." He shook it, and finding it heavy went on, "I wish I knew what was inside. But I must and will open it and look at what is in it, and then put it in my bag and sell it in the brass market."

So he took out a knife and worked at the lead until he had loosened it off the jar; and he laid the cap on the ground and tipped the jar up to pour out whatever might be inside.

Nothing came out at all, which astonished him a great deal. But then a smoke came out of the jar and went spiralling up into the sky — which astonished him a great deal more — and trailed along the surface of the ground; and when it had reached its full height the thick vapour drew itself together and became an ifrit, enormous in bulk, whose head touched the clouds while his feet were still on the earth. His head was like a dome, his hands like pitchforks, his legs as long as masts and his mouth as big as a cave; his teeth were like boulders, his nostrils like water jugs, his eyes like two lamps, and his whole look was fierce and black.

Now when the fisherman saw the ifrit the muscles in his sides quivered, his teeth chattered, his mouth went dry and he could not think what to do.

At this the ifrit looked at him and cried, "There is no god but God, and Solomon is the prophet of God!" and then added, "Apostle of Allah, do not kill me. Never again will I contradict you in a word or sin against you in a deed."

"Marid," said the fisherman — for that is the name of the mightiest and most rebellious of the jinn — "did you say Solomon, the Apostle of Allah? Solomon has been dead for a thousand and eight hundred years, and we are living in the last days of the world. What is your story, and what account do you give of yourself, and how did you come to be inside this jar?"

Now when the evil spirit heard the fisherman's words he said, "There is no god but God. Be of good cheer, fisherman!"

"Why do you tell me to be of good cheer?" said the fisherman.

"Because," he replied, "you are going to die a bad death within the hour."

"For such good news as that," said the fisherman, "you deserve to have Heaven's protection taken off you, you distant one. Why should you kill me? What have I done to deserve death — I, who freed you out of the jar and saved you from the bottom of the sea and brought you up onto dry land?"

"Ask me one thing only," the ifrit answered, "which death you would like to die, and by what manner of killing I am to kill you."

"What is my crime," said the fisherman, "and why should I be paid back like this?"

"Hear my story, fisherman," said the ifrit.

"Say on," he answered, "and keep it short, for truly my life is in my nostrils this minute."

So the jinni said: Know that I am one of the jinn who fell into heresy, and I sinned against Solomon son of David, on the two of them be peace, along with the famous Sakhr the Jinni. So the Prophet sent his minister, Asaf son of Barkhiya, to seize me; and this vizier brought me against my will and led me to him in chains — and I went with him though every part of me refused to go — and stood me in front of him like a beggar.

When Solomon saw me he took refuge with Allah, and ordered me to embrace the True Faith and obey his commands. I refused. So he sent for this jar and shut me up in it and stopped it with lead, and stamped on it the Most High Name, and gave his orders to the jinn, who carried me off and threw me into the middle of the ocean.

There I stayed a hundred years, and all that time I said in my heart, "Whoever sets me free, I will make him rich for ever and ever." But the full century went by, and nobody set me free.

So I began the second hundred, saying, "Whoever sets me free, for him I will open the treasure hoards of the earth." Still nobody set me free, and so four hundred years went past.

Then I said, "Whoever sets me free, for him I will grant three wishes." And still nobody set me free.

At that I flew into a rage past all raging, and said to myself, "Whoever sets me free from this time on, I will kill him, and I will give him his choice of what death he dies." And now, since you are the one who has set me free, I give you your full choice of deaths.

The fisherman, hearing all this, said, "O Allah — the wonder of it, that I should not have come along to free you until now!" And he added, "Spare my life, and may Allah spare yours; and do not kill me, or Allah will set somebody on to kill you."

"There is no help for it," the stubborn creature replied. "Die you must. So ask me, as a favour, what manner of death you will die."

Even so certain of it, the fisherman tried once more. "Forgive me my death," he said, "as a generous reward for having freed you."

"I would certainly not be killing you," said the ifrit, "except on account of that same freeing."

"Chief of the ifrits," said the fisherman, "I do you good and you pay me back with evil. Truly the old saying does not lie:

	We did them good, and they met our good with ill;
	such, on my life, is every bad man's work.
	Whoever does a kindness to people who do not deserve it
	gets what the man got who took in the hyena."

Now when the ifrit heard this he answered, "No more of this talk. I have to kill you."

At which the fisherman said to himself, "This is a jinni, and I am a man, and Allah has given me a reasonably cunning wit. So I will now find a way to bring about his destruction by my own contrivance and my own intelligence — just as he has taken counsel of nothing but his malice and his perversity."

He began by asking the ifrit, "Have you really made up your mind to kill me?"

And getting no answer but "I have," he cried, "Now, by the Most Great Name engraved on the seal ring of Solomon son of David, peace be on the holy two — if I ask you about a certain matter, will you give me a truthful answer?"

The ifrit replied, "I will." But at the mention of the Most Great Name his wits were shaken, and he said, trembling, "Ask, and be quick about it."

"How," said the fisherman, "did you fit into this bottle, which would not hold your hand — no, nor even your foot? And how did it come to be big enough to hold the whole of you?"

"What!" said the ifrit. "Do you not believe I was all in there?"

And the fisherman answered, "No. I will never believe it until I see you inside it with my own eyes":—

When it was the Fourth Night,

her sister said to her, "Please finish this tale for us, if you are not sleepy!" So she took it up again:

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when the fisherman said to the ifrit, "I will never in any way believe you until I see you inside it with my own eyes," the evil spirit instantly shook himself and became a vapour, which drew together and went into the jar little by little, until all of him was well inside. And at that the fisherman snatched up the lead cap with the seal on it and stoppered the mouth of the jar, and called out to the ifrit:

"Ask me, as a favour, what death you would like to die! By Allah, I am going to throw you into the sea here in front of us, and I will build myself a hut on this spot; and whoever comes here I shall warn him off fishing and tell him: In these waters there lives an ifrit who offers the man who rescues him a choice of deaths and a choice of ways of being killed."

Now when the ifrit heard this from the fisherman, and saw himself shut in, he wanted to escape; but Solomon's seal prevented him. So he understood that the fisherman had cheated him and outwitted him, and he became meek and submissive, and began humbly saying, "I was only joking with you."

But the other answered, "You are lying, vilest of the ifrits, and meanest, and filthiest." And he set off with the bottle for the shore; the ifrit calling out "No! No!" and the fisherman calling out "Yes! Yes!"

At that the evil spirit softened his voice and smoothed his speech and abased himself, and said, "What will you do with me, fisherman?"

"I will throw you back into the sea," he answered, "where you have been housed and homed for a thousand and eight hundred years; and now I shall leave you there till Judgement Day. Did I not say to you: spare me and Allah will spare you, and do not kill me or Allah will kill you? And you rejected my plea and had no intention but to deal ungraciously with me. And now Allah has thrown you into my hands, and I am the cunninger of the two of us."

"Open the jar for me," said the ifrit, "and I will do you good."

"You are lying, you accursed thing," said the fisherman. "My case with you is the case of the vizier of King Yunan with the sage Duban."

"And who was the vizier of King Yunan, and who was the sage Duban, and what was the story about them?" said the ifrit.
