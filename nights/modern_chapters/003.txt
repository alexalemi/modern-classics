Tale of the Trader and the Jinni

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that there was once a merchant among the merchants, a man of great wealth with business in a number of cities.

One day he mounted his horse and rode out to collect money owed him in certain towns; and the heat came down on him hard, so he sat down under a tree, and put his hand into his saddlebag and took out some broken bread and a few dry dates, and began to eat.

When he had finished the dates he flung the stones away, hard — and there in front of him stood an ifrit, an enormous one, brandishing a drawn sword. The ifrit came up to him and said, "Stand up, so that I can kill you, exactly as you killed my son."

"How have I killed your son?" asked the merchant.

"When you ate those dates and threw away the stones," he answered, "they struck my son full in the chest as he was walking past, and he died on the spot."

"Truly we come from Allah, and to Allah we are returning," said the merchant. "There is no Majesty and there is no Might save in Allah, the Glorious, the Great! If I killed your son, I killed him by pure accident. I beg you to pardon me."

"There is no help for it," said the jinni. "I have to kill you."

Then he seized him and dragged him along, and threw him down on the ground, and raised the sword to strike. And the merchant wept and said, "I commit my case to Allah," and began to recite:

	Time holds two sorts of day, this one a blessing and that one a curse,
	and life holds two halves, this one all pleasure and that one all pain.
	Do you not see, when the hurricane blows, stripping and striking hard,
	that nothing but the great tree of the forest feels the strain of it?
	How many trees does the earth feed, the dry ones and the green —
	and none but the ones bearing fruit get stones thrown at them.
	Do you not see how corpses rise and float on the top of the tide,
	while the pearls worth having lie hidden in the deepest part of the sea?
	The stars in heaven are past counting,
	but no star is ever eclipsed except the sun and the moon.
	You judged well of the days that saw you going about safe and sound,
	and never counted the pangs and the pains that Fate is always ready with.
	The nights kept you safe, and being safe made you proud —
	but the good things of the night are the very things that breed the bad.

When the merchant had finished his verses the jinni said to him, "Cut it short, by Allah. I have to kill you."

But the merchant spoke to him like this. "Understand, ifrit, that I have debts owed to me and a great deal of property, and children and a wife, and a number of things left in my keeping. Let me go home and settle with every man who has a claim on me; and I will come back to you at the head of the new year. Let Allah be my witness and my guarantor that I will return to you, and then you may do with me whatever you like. And Allah is witness to what I say."

The jinni took a firm promise from him and let him go.

So he went back to his own city and put his affairs in order and paid every man what he was owed; and after telling his wife and children what had happened to him, he appointed a guardian for them and lived with them for a full year.

Then he got up, and washed himself clean as a man does before death, and took his shroud under his arm, and said goodbye to his household and his neighbours and every one of his relations, and went out — though every part of him refused to go. They began weeping and wailing and beating their breasts over him; but he travelled on until he came to the same garden, and the day he arrived was the head of the new year.

As he sat there weeping over what had happened to him, an old man came up, a very ancient man, leading a gazelle on a chain. He greeted the merchant and wished him a long life, and said, "What has brought you to sit in this place, all by yourself, when it is a haunt of evil spirits?"

The merchant told him everything that had passed between himself and the ifrit; and the old man, the owner of the gazelle, marvelled at it and said, "By Allah, brother, your faith is nothing short of extraordinary, and your story is a strange one. If it were engraved with needles on the inner corners of the eye, it would be a warning to anyone willing to take warning."

Then he sat down beside the merchant and said, "By Allah, brother, I am not leaving you until I see what happens between you and this ifrit."

And as he sat there and the two of them talked, the merchant began to feel fear and terror, and a grief past all easing, and a care that kept growing, and the last extremity of despair.

The owner of the gazelle was still at his side when a second old man came up to them, and with him two dogs, both greyhounds and both black. This second old man greeted them and asked their news as well, and said, "What has brought you to sit in this place, which is a dwelling of the jinn?"

So they told him the tale from beginning to end. And they had not been sitting there long before a third old man came up, with him a she-mule of a bright bay colour; and he greeted them and asked why they were sitting there. So they told him the story from first to last — and there is no use, my lord, in telling a tale twice.

He sat down with them. And then a cloud of dust came on, and a great dust-devil rose up in the middle of the waste ground; and the cloud opened, and inside it was that same jinni, with a drawn sword in his hand and his eyes throwing off sparks of rage.

He came up to them and hauled the merchant out from among them and shouted at him, "Get up, so that I can kill you, exactly as you killed my son, the life-blood of my liver."

The merchant wailed and wept, and the three old men began sighing and crying and weeping and wailing along with their companion.

Then the first old man, the owner of the gazelle, stepped out from among them and kissed the ifrit's hand and said, "Jinni — Crown of the Kings of the Jinn — if I were to tell you the story of myself and this gazelle, and you found it a wonder, would you give me a third part of this merchant's blood?"

"I would," said the jinni. "If you tell me this tale and I hold it to be a marvel, then I will give you a third of his blood."
