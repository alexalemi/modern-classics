Sindbad the Seaman and Sindbad the Landsman

There lived in the city of Baghdad, in the reign of the Commander of the Faithful, Harun al-Rashid, a man named Sindbad the Porter, a poor man who carried burdens on his head for hire.

It happened to him one day of great heat that while he was carrying a heavy load he grew extremely tired and sweated profusely, the heat and the weight oppressing him alike. Presently, as he was passing the gate of a merchant's house, in front of which the ground had been swept and watered and the air was temperate, he caught sight of a broad bench beside the door; so he set his load down on it to rest and take the air:—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Thirty-seventh Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when the porter had set his load on the bench to rest and take the air, there came out to him from the courtyard door a pleasant breeze and a delicious fragrance.

He sat down on the edge of the bench, and at once heard from inside the melodious sound of lutes and other stringed instruments, and voices singing and reciting to stir up mirth, together with the song of birds warbling and glorifying Almighty Allah in all sorts of tunes and tongues — turtle doves, mockingbirds, blackbirds, nightingales, wood pigeons and stone-curlews; at which he marvelled to himself and was moved to a mighty joy and comfort.

Then he went up to the gate and saw inside a great flower garden, in which were pages and Black slaves and such a train of servants and attendants and the rest as is only found with kings and sultans; and his nostrils were greeted with the savoury smells of every kind of meat, rich and delicate, and of delicious and generous wines.

So he raised his eyes to heaven and said:

"Glory to You, O Lord, O Creator and Provider, who provide for whom You will without counting or stinting! O my Holy One, I beg Your pardon for all my sins and turn to You in repentance for all my offences!

"O Lord, there is no gainsaying You in Your ordinance and Your dominion, nor will You be questioned about what You do, for You are indeed Almighty over all things. Extolled be Your perfection: whom You will You make poor, and whom You will You make rich; whom You will You exalt and whom You will You abase; and there is no god but You.

"How mighty is Your majesty and how enduring Your dominion and how excellent Your government! Truly You favour whom You will among Your servants, so that the owner of this place lives in all the joyance of life and delights himself with pleasant scents and delicious food and exquisite wines of every kind.

"For indeed You appoint for Your creatures whatever You will and whatever You have foreordained for them; and so some are weary and others are at rest, and some enjoy good fortune and plenty while others suffer the last extremity of toil and misery — as I do."

And he fell to reciting:

	How many, by my labours that go on and on for ever,
	enjoy every good thing in life and lie back in the cool shade?
	Every morning that dawns I wake to toil and to grief,
	and my condition is strange and my burden makes me pine.
	Plenty of others are in luck and free of every misery,
	and Fortune never loads them with loads like mine.
	They live out their happy days in comfort and delight;
	they eat, drink and live in honour among the noble and the worthy.
	Every living thing was made out of a little drop of seed;
	your origin is mine and my beginning is yours;
	and yet the difference and the distance between the two of us
	are as far apart as the taste of vinegar and the taste of wine.
	But at You, O all-wise God, I do not venture to rail,
	whose ordinance is just and whose justice cannot fail.

When Sindbad the Porter had finished reciting his verses, he took up his burden and was about to go on his way, when there came out to him from the gate a little page boy, fair of face and shapely of figure and dainty in his dress, who caught him by the hand and said, "Come in and speak with my lord, for he is calling for you."

The porter would have excused himself to the page, but the boy would take no refusal; so he left his load with the doorkeeper in the entrance hall and followed the boy into the house, which he found to be a fine mansion, radiant and full of majesty — until he brought him into a grand sitting room, where he saw a company of nobles and great lords seated at tables decked with all sorts of flowers and sweet-scented herbs, besides great plenty of dainty dishes and fruit, dried and fresh, and sweets, and wines of the choicest vintages.

There too were instruments of music and mirth, and lovely slave girls playing and singing. The whole company was ranged according to rank; and in the highest place sat a man of a worshipful and noble aspect, whose beard was touched at the sides with grey; and he was stately in stature and handsome in his face, agreeable to look at and full of gravity and dignity and majesty.

So Sindbad the Porter was confounded at what he saw, and said to himself, "By Allah, this must be either a piece of Paradise or some king's palace!"

Then he greeted the company with great respect, praying for their prosperity; and kissing the ground before them, stood with his head bowed down in a humble attitude:—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Thirty-eighth Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Sindbad the Porter, after kissing the ground between their hands, stood with his head bowed in a humble attitude.

The master of the house told him to come nearer and be seated, and spoke to him kindly and made him welcome. Then he set various kinds of food in front of him, rich and delicate and delicious; and the porter, after saying "In Allah's name," fell to and ate his fill; after which he exclaimed, "Praised be Allah, whatever our condition!" and washing his hands, thanked the company for entertaining him.

Said the host, "You are welcome, and your day is a blessed one. But what is your name and your calling?"

Said the other, "My lord, my name is Sindbad the Porter, and I carry people's goods on my head for hire."

The master of the house smiled and answered, "Know, porter, that your name is the same as mine, for I am Sindbad the Seaman. And now, porter, I should like you to let me hear the verses you were reciting at the gate just now."

The porter was embarrassed and replied, "In Allah's name, excuse me! Toil and travail and lack of luck, when the hand is empty, teach a man bad manners and boorish ways."

Said the host, "Do not be ashamed; you have become my brother. But repeat the verses to me, for they pleased me when I heard you recite them at the gate."

At which the porter repeated the lines, and they delighted the merchant, who said to him:

Know, porter, that my story is a wonderful one; and you shall hear everything that happened to me and everything I went through before I rose to this state of prosperity and became the lord of this place where you see me. For I did not come to this high estate except after grievous toil and perils in plenty; and how much labour and trouble have I not suffered in days gone by!

I have made seven voyages, and by each one of them hangs a marvellous tale, such as confounds the reason; and all of it came about by the doom of fortune and fate, for from what destiny writes there is neither refuge nor flight.
