Aladdin; Or, the Wonderful Lamp
(Part 7 of 13)

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifty-third Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when the Queen heard these words from Princess Badr al-Budur she said to her, "My child, compose your thoughts. If you tell such a tale in front of anybody, he will very likely say, 'Truly, the Sultan's daughter has lost her wits.' And you did quite right not to choose to tell your adventure to your father; and beware, and again I say beware, my daughter, of letting him know about it."

The Princess replied, "Mother, I have spoken to you like somebody sound in her senses, and I have not lost my wits: this is what happened to me; and if you do not believe it coming from me, ask my bridegroom."

To which the Queen replied, "Get up at once, my daughter, and banish such fancies from your thoughts; and dress yourself and come out to look at the wedding feasts and festivities they are holding in the city for your sake and your marriage; and listen to the drumming and the singing and look at the decorations, all of them meant to honour your marriage, my daughter."

So saying, the Queen at once summoned the tirewomen, who dressed and prepared the Lady Badr al-Budur; and presently she went in to the Sultan and assured him that their daughter had suffered all through her wedding night from bad dreams and nightmares, and said to him, "Do not be severe with her for not answering you."

Then the Queen sent privately for the Vizier's son and asked him about the matter, saying, "Tell me, are these words of the Lady Badr al-Budur true or not?"

But he, in his fear of losing his bride there and then, answered, "My lady, I know nothing of what you are speaking about."

And so the mother made sure that her daughter had been seeing visions and dreams.

The marriage feasts lasted all that day, with dancing girls and singers and the striking of every kind of instrument of mirth and merriment, while the Queen and the Vizier and his son strove their hardest to enhance the festivities so that the Princess might enjoy herself; and that day they left nothing that excites pleasure unperformed in her presence, so that she might forget what was on her mind and take more joy in it all.

Yet none of this had any effect on her at all; on the contrary, she sat in silence, sad in her thoughts and badly perplexed at what had happened to her the night before.

It is true that the Vizier's son had suffered even more, because he had spent his sleeping hours lying in the water closet; he, however, had lied about the story and had cast the memory of the night out of his mind — in the first place for fear of losing his bride, and with her the honour of a connection that brought him so much consideration and for which men envied him so much; and, secondly, on account of the wonderful loveliness of the Lady Badr al-Budur and her marvellous beauty.

Aladdin also went out that day and looked at the merrymaking that ran through the whole city as well as the palace; and he fell to laughing, especially when he heard people chattering about the high honour that had come to the son of the Vizier and the prosperity of his fortunes in becoming son-in-law to the Sultan, and the great consideration shown by the wedding celebrations. And he said in his mind, "Indeed you have no idea, you miserable creatures, what happened to him last night, that you should envy him!"

But after darkness fell and it was time for sleep, Aladdin got up and, going to his room, rubbed the Lamp; whereupon the slave appeared immediately.

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifty-fourth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when the slave appeared before Aladdin he was ordered to bring him the Sultan's daughter along with her bridegroom, as on the night before, before the Vizier's son could take her maidenhead.

So the marid vanished without pause or delay for a little while, until the appointed time, when he came back carrying the bed on which the Lady Badr al-Budur and the Vizier's son were lying; and he did with the bridegroom as he had done before — that is, he took him up and laid him at full length in the privy, and left him there dried up with the excess of his fear and trembling.

Then Aladdin got up and, laying the sword between himself and the Princess, lay down beside her; and when day broke the slave restored the pair to their own place, leaving Aladdin filled with delight at the state of the minister's son.

Now when the Sultan woke that morning he resolved to visit his daughter and see whether she would treat him as she had the day before; so, shaking off his sleep, he sprang up and dressed himself and, going to the Princess's apartment, ordered the door opened.

At this the Vizier's son got up at once and came down out of bed and began putting his clothes on, while his ribs were wrung with cold; for the slave had only just brought him back when the King came in.

The Sultan, raising the curtain, went up to his daughter as she lay in bed and bade her good morning; then, kissing her between the eyes, he asked her how she was. But he saw her looking sour and sad, and she did not answer him at all, only glowering at him like somebody angry; and her condition was pitiable.

At this the Sultan grew furious with her for not replying, and he suspected that something evil had happened to her; whereupon he bared his sword and cried to her, blade in hand, "What is this that has come over you? Either tell me what has happened, or this very moment I will take your life! Is this the sign of honour and respect I expect of you — that I speak to you and you do not answer me a word?"

When the Lady Badr al-Budur saw her father in high indignation, with the naked blade in his grip, she was freed from her earlier fear; so she raised her head and said to him, "My beloved father, do not be angry with me, and do not be hasty in your hot passion, for I am excusable in what you shall see of my case. So lend an ear to what has happened to me; and I know well that after hearing my account of what befell me these last two nights, you will pardon me, and your Highness will be softened into pitying me, as I claim of you affection for your child."

Then the Princess told her father everything that had happened to her, and added, "Father, if you do not believe me, ask my bridegroom, and he will tell your Highness the whole adventure; for I did not know what they would do with him when they carried him away from my side, nor where they would put him."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifty-fifth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that when the Sultan heard his daughter's words he was saddened, and his eyes brimmed with tears; then he sheathed his sword and kissed her, saying, "My daughter, why did you not tell me what happened the night before, so that I might have guarded you against this torture and terror that visited you a second time? But it is no matter now. Get up and throw off all such care; and tonight I will set a watch to guard you, and no mishap shall ever make you miserable again."

Then the Sultan went back to his palace and immediately ordered the Grand Vizier summoned, and asked him, as he stood before him in attendance, "Vizier, how do you look at this business? Perhaps your son has told you what happened to him and to my daughter."

The minister replied, "O King of the Age, I have not seen my son either yesterday or today."

At this the Sultan told him everything that had afflicted the Princess, and added, "It is my wish that you go at once and get news from your son about the facts of the case: it may be that out of her fear my daughter is not fully aware of what really happened to her — although I hold every word she said to be truthful."

So the Grand Vizier got up and, going out, ordered his son summoned and asked him whether everything his lord had told him was true or untrue.

The youth replied, "My father the Vizier, Heaven forbid that the Lady Badr al-Budur should speak falsely: indeed everything she said was true, and these two nights proved to us the worst of our nights instead of being nights of pleasure and marriage joys. But what happened to me was the greater evil, because instead of sleeping in bed with my bride I lay in the wardrobe — a black hole, frightful, foul-smelling, truly damnable; and my ribs were bursting with cold."

In short, the young man told his father the whole tale, and added as he finished it, "Dear father, I beg you to speak to the Sultan and have him set me free from this marriage. Yes indeed, it is a high honour for me to be the Sultan's son-in-law, and especially since love of the Princess has got hold of my vitals; but I have no strength left to endure a single night like these last two."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifty-sixth Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the Vizier, hearing his son's words, was exceedingly saddened and sorrowful, for it had been his plan to advance and promote his child by making him son-in-law to the Sultan. So he became thoughtful and perplexed about the affair and about how to manage it; and it was very grievous to him to break off the marriage, since it had been a rare pleasure to him to have fallen upon such high good fortune.

So he said, "Be patient, my son, until we see what may happen tonight, when we will set watchmen to guard you; and do not give up the exalted distinction that has fallen to nobody but yourself."

Then the Vizier left him and, going back to his sovereign, reported that everything the Lady Badr al-Budur had told him was a true tale; whereupon the Sultan said, "Since the affair stands like this, we need no delay"; and he at once ordered all the rejoicings to stop and the marriage to be broken off.

This made the people and the citizens marvel at the business, especially when they saw the Grand Vizier and his son leaving the palace in a pitiable state for grief and stress of feeling; and everybody fell to asking, "What has happened, and what is the reason for the wedding being made null and void?" Nor did anybody know a thing about the truth of it except Aladdin, the lover who laid claim to the Princess's hand; and he laughed in his sleeve.

But even after the marriage was dissolved, the Sultan forgot, and never even called to mind, the promise he had made to Aladdin's mother; and it was the same with the Grand Vizier, while neither of them had the least inkling of where the thing that had happened to them had come from.

So Aladdin waited patiently for the three months to pass, after which the Sultan had pledged himself to give him his daughter in marriage; but the moment the term came round, he sent his mother to the Sultan to require him to keep his covenant.

So she went to the palace; and when the King appeared in the Divan and saw the old woman standing before him, he remembered his promise to her about the marriage after a term of three months, and he turned to the minister and said, "Vizier, this is the old lady who presented me with the jewels, and to whom we pledged our word that when the three months had gone by we would summon her to our presence before all others."

So the minister went out and fetched her; and when she came into the Sultan's presence she saluted him and prayed for his glory and the permanence of his prosperity. At this the King asked her whether she needed anything, and she answered, "O King of the Age, the three months' term you assigned me is finished, and this is the time for you to marry my son Aladdin to your daughter, the Lady Badr al-Budur."

The Sultan was thrown into confusion by this demand, especially when he saw the old woman's poverty-stricken condition, one of the meanest of her kind; and yet the offering she had brought him had been of the most magnificent, far beyond his power to pay the price of. So he turned to the Grand Vizier and said, "What plan have you? Truly I did pass my word; and yet it seems to me they are poor people and not persons of high condition."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifty-seventh Night,

Dunyazad said, "Sister, if you are not sleepy, do tell us one of your pleasant tales." And Shahrazad replied, "With love and good will."

It has reached me, O fortunate King, that the Grand Vizier, who was dying of envy and who was particularly saddened by what had happened to his son, said to himself, "How shall a man like this marry the King's daughter, and my son lose this highest of honours?"

So he answered his sovereign, speaking privately, "My lord, it is an easy matter to keep off a poor devil like this, for he is not worthy that your Highness should give your daughter to a fellow whom nobody knows anything about."

"By what means," asked the Sultan, "shall we put the man off, when I have pledged my promise? And the word of kings is their bond."

The Vizier replied, "My lord, my advice is that you demand of him forty platters made of pure gold and full of gems, such as the woman brought you before, with forty white slave girls to carry the platters and forty black eunuch slaves."

The King answered, "By Allah, Vizier, you have spoken to the purpose, seeing that such a thing is not possible, and by this means we shall be free of him."

Then he said to Aladdin's mother, "Go and tell your son that I am a man of my word, just as I pledged it to him — but on condition that he has the power to pay the dowry of my daughter. And what I require of him is a settlement consisting of forty platters of virgin gold, all brimming with gems like the ones you brought me, and as many white handmaids to carry them, and forty black eunuch slaves to attend and escort the bearers. If your son can manage this, I will marry him to my daughter."

At this she went home wagging her head and saying in her mind, "Where can my poor boy get these platters and jewels? And granting that he went back to the enchanted treasury and picked them off the trees — which, however, I hold to be impossible — yet even if he brought them, where is he to come by the girls and the black slaves?"

Nor did she stop communing with herself until she reached her house, where she found Aladdin waiting for her; and she lost no time in saying, "My son, did I not tell you never to imagine that your power would stretch as far as the Lady Badr al-Budur, and that such a thing is not possible for people like ourselves?"

"Tell me the news," he said.

So she said, "My child, truly the Sultan received me with all honour, as he usually does, and it seems to me his intentions towards us are friendly. But your enemy is that accursed Vizier. For after I addressed the King in your name, as you told me to say — 'In very truth the promised term is past,' adding, 'It would be well if your Highness would be pleased to give the order for the betrothal of your daughter the Lady Badr al-Budur to my son Aladdin' — he turned and spoke to the minister, who answered him privately; after which the Sultan gave me his reply."

Then she went through the King's demands and said, "My son, he certainly expects an immediate answer from you; but I fancy we have no answer for him."

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.
