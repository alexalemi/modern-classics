The Fourth Voyage of Sindbad the Seaman
(Part 1 of 2)

Know, my brothers, that after I came back from my third voyage and met up with my friends, and forgot all my perils and hardships in the enjoyment of ease and comfort and rest, I was visited one day by a company of merchants, who sat down with me and talked of foreign travel and trade — until the old bad man inside me yearned to go with them and enjoy the sight of strange countries, and I longed for the society of the various races of mankind and for trade and profit.

So I resolved to travel with them; and buying the necessaries for a long voyage, and a great store of costly goods, more than ever before, transported them from Baghdad to Basra, where I took ship with the merchants in question, who were among the chief men of the town.

We set out trusting in the blessing of Almighty Allah; and with a favouring breeze and the best of conditions we sailed from island to island and sea to sea — until one day a contrary wind rose against us, and the captain cast out his anchors and brought the ship to a standstill, afraid she might founder in mid-ocean.

Then we all fell to praying and humbling ourselves before the Most High; but while we were doing it a furious squall struck us that tore the sails to rags and tatters. The anchor cable parted, and the ship foundering, we were thrown into the sea, goods and all.

I kept myself afloat by swimming for half the day, until, when I had given myself up for lost, the Almighty threw in my way one of the ship's planks, which I and some of the other merchants scrambled onto:—

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifty-first Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Sindbad the Seaman went on as follows:

And when the ship foundered I scrambled onto a plank with some of the other merchants; and mounting it as we would a horse, we paddled with our feet in the sea.

We stayed like that a day and a night, the wind and the waves helping us along; and on the second day, shortly before the middle of the morning, the breeze freshened and the sea worked and the rising waves cast us up on an island, very nearly dead bodies for weariness and want of sleep, cold and hunger and fear and thirst.

We walked about the shore and found plenty of herbs, of which we ate enough to keep breath in our bodies and steady our failing spirits; then we lay down and slept until morning close by the sea.

And when the morning came with its brightness and shone, we got up and walked about the island to right and left, until we came in sight of an inhabited house a long way off. So we made towards it, and did not stop walking until we reached its door — when a number of naked men came out of it and, without greeting us or a word said, laid hold of us and mastered us and carried us off to their king, who signed to us to sit down.

So we sat down, and they set food in front of us of a kind we did not know and whose like we had never seen in all our lives. My companions ate some of it out of the stress of their hunger; but my stomach revolted at it and I would not eat — and my refusing it was, by Allah's favour, the reason I am alive today.

For no sooner had my comrades tasted it than their reason left them and their condition changed, and they began to devour it like madmen possessed by an evil spirit.

Then the savages gave them coconut oil to drink and anointed them with it; and straight after drinking it their eyes turned up in their heads and they fell to eating greedily, quite against their habit.

When I saw this I was confounded and concerned for them, and no less anxious about myself for fear of the naked people. So I watched them closely, and it was not long before I discovered that they were a tribe of Magian cannibals, whose King was a ghoul.

Everybody who came to their country, or whoever they caught in their valleys or on their roads, they brought to this King and fed on that food and anointed with that oil, at which their stomachs would swell so that they could eat a great deal, while their reason left them and they lost the power of thought and became idiots.

Then they stuffed them with coconut oil and the food I have described until they became fat and gross; when they slaughtered them by cutting their throats and roasted them for the King to eat. But as for the savages themselves, they ate human flesh raw.

When I saw this I was badly dismayed for myself and my comrades, who by now had become so stupefied that they did not know what was being done with them; and the naked people committed them to a man who used to lead them out every day and pasture them on the island like cattle. And they wandered among the trees and rested as they pleased, and so grew very fat.

As for me, I wasted away and grew sickly with fear and hunger, and my flesh shrivelled on my bones. Seeing which, the savages left me alone and took no notice of me, and so far forgot about me that one day I gave them the slip; and walking out of their place, made for the beach, which was a long way off.

There I caught sight of a very old man sitting on a high place, ringed about by the water. I looked at him and knew him for the herdsman who had charge of pasturing my companions; and with him were many others in the same state.

As soon as he saw me he knew I was still in possession of my reason and not afflicted like the rest he was pasturing; so he signed to me from a distance, as much as to say, "Turn back and take the right-hand road, for that will bring you to the King's highway."

So I turned back as he directed, and followed the right-hand road, now running for fear and now walking at leisure to rest myself, until I was out of the old man's sight.

By this time the sun had gone down and the dark had set in; so I sat down to rest, and would have slept, but sleep would not come to me that night for the stress of fear and famine and fatigue.

When the night was half gone I got up and walked on, until the day broke in all its beauty and the sun rose over the heads of the lofty hills and across the low gravelly plains.

Now I was weary and hungry and thirsty; so I ate my fill of the herbs and grasses that grew on the island and kept life in my body and stayed my stomach; after which I set out again and travelled all that day and the next night, staying my hunger with roots and herbs.

Nor did I stop walking for seven days and their nights, until the morning of the eighth, when I caught sight of a faint object in the distance. So I made towards it, though my heart quaked for everything I had suffered first and last — and it was a company of men gathering peppercorns.

As soon as they saw me they hurried up to me and surrounded me on all sides and said, "Who are you and where do you come from?"

I replied, "Know, good people, that I am a poor stranger" — and told them my case and all the hardships and perils I had suffered:—

And the men gathering pepper on the island questioned me about my case, when I told them all the hardships and perils I had suffered and how I had fled from the savages; at which they marvelled and wished me joy of my safety, saying:

"By Allah, this is wonderful! But how did you escape from these savages, who swarm all over the island and devour everybody who falls in with them? Nobody is safe from them, and nobody gets out of their clutches."

And after I had told them the fate of my companions they made me sit with them until they had finished their work; and fetched me some good food, which I ate, for I was hungry, and rested a while.

After which they took ship with me and, carrying me to their island home, brought me before their King, who returned my greeting and received me honourably and questioned me about my case.

I told him everything that had happened to me from the day I left the city of Baghdad; at which he wondered greatly at my adventures, he and his courtiers, and told me to sit beside him. Then he called for food, and I ate with him as much as I needed and washed my hands and gave thanks to Almighty Allah for all His favours, praising Him and glorifying Him.

Then I left the King and walked about the city to amuse myself, and found it wealthy and populous, abounding in market streets well stocked with food and merchandise and full of buyers and sellers.

So I rejoiced at having reached such a pleasant place, and took my ease there after my exhaustion; and I made friends among the townspeople, and before long I stood higher in honour and favour with them and with their King than any of the chief men of the realm.

Now I saw that all the citizens, great and small, rode fine horses, high-priced and thoroughbred, without saddles or housings; at which I wondered, and said to the King, "Why, my lord, do you not ride with a saddle? There is comfort in it for the rider and an increase of control."

"What is a saddle?" he asked. "I never saw one or used one in all my life."

And I answered, "With your permission I will make you a saddle, so that you may ride on it and see how comfortable it is."

And he said, "Do so."

So I said to him, "Furnish me with some wood"; which being brought, I looked out a clever carpenter and sat with him and showed him how to make the saddle tree, drawing the shape of it for him in ink on the wood.

Then I took wool and teased it and made felt of it; and covering the saddle tree with leather, stuffed it and polished it and attached the girth and the stirrup leathers.

After which I fetched a blacksmith and described to him the shape of the stirrups and the bit. So he forged a fine pair of stirrups and a bit, and filed them smooth and tinned them. What is more, I fastened silk fringes to them and fitted bridle leathers to the bit.

Then I fetched one of the best of the royal horses and, saddling and bridling him, hung the stirrups on the saddle and led him to the King.

The thing took his fancy and he thanked me; then he mounted and was delighted with the saddle, and rewarded me handsomely for my work.

When the King's vizier saw the saddle he asked me for one like it, and I made it for him. And after that all the grandees and officers of state came to me for saddles; so I fell to making saddles — having taught the craft to the carpenter and the blacksmith — and selling them to everybody who wanted one, until I amassed great wealth and stood in high honour and great favour with the King and his household and his grandees.

I lived like this until one day, as I was sitting with the King in all respect and contentment, he said to me:

"Know, my friend, that you have become one of us, dear as a brother; and we hold you in such regard and affection that we cannot part with you or allow you to leave our city. And so I want your obedience in a certain matter, and I will not have you cross me in it."

I answered, "O King, what is it you want of me? Far be it from me to cross you in anything, for I am indebted to you for many favours and bounties and much kindness; and, praise be to Allah, I have become one of your servants."

Said he, "I have a mind to marry you to a fair, clever, agreeable wife who is as wealthy as she is beautiful, so that you may be naturalised and settled among us. I will lodge you with me in my palace; so do not oppose me or cross me in this."

When I heard this I was abashed and held my peace, and could not answer him at all for my great bashfulness in front of him.

He asked, "Why do you not answer me, my son?"

And I answered, "My master, it is yours to command, O King of the age."

So he summoned the judge and the witnesses and married me straight away to a lady of a noble family and a high pedigree; wealthy in money and in means; the flower of an ancient race; of surpassing beauty and grace, and the owner of farms and estates and many a dwelling place:—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.

When it was the Five Hundred and Fifty-third Night,

she said: It has reached me, O fortunate King, that Sindbad the Seaman went on in these words:

Now after the King my master had married me to this choice wife, he also gave me a great and handsome house standing on its own, together with slaves and officers, and assigned me pay and allowances.

So I lived in all ease and contentment and delight, and forgot everything that had happened to me of weariness and trouble and hardship; for I loved my wife with the fondest love and she loved me no less, and we were as one, and lived in the utmost comfort of life and in its happiness.

And I said to myself, "When I go back to my own country I will take her with me." But whatever is predestined for a man, that must happen, and nobody knows what will befall him.

We lived like this a great while, until Almighty Allah bereaved one of my neighbours of his wife. Now he was a friend of mine; so hearing the cry of the mourners, I went in to condole with him on his loss, and found him in a very bad way, full of trouble and weary in soul and mind.

I condoled with him and comforted him, saying, "Do not mourn for your wife, who has now found the mercy of Allah; the Lord will surely give you a better one in her place, and your name shall be great and your life shall be long in the land, Allah willing."

But he wept bitter tears and replied, "My friend, how can I marry another wife, and how shall Allah replace her with a better than she was, when I have only one day left to live?"

"Brother," said I, "come back to your senses, and do not announce the glad tidings of your own death, for you are well and sound and in good case."

"By your life, my friend," he answered, "tomorrow you will lose me and will never see me again till the Day of Resurrection."

I asked, "How so?"

And he answered, "This very day they bury my wife, and they bury me with her in one tomb; for it is the custom with us, if the wife dies first, to bury the husband alive with her, and likewise the wife if the husband dies first, so that neither may enjoy life after losing the other."

"By Allah," I cried, "this is a most vile and wicked custom, and not to be endured by anybody!"

Meanwhile most of the townspeople came in and fell to condoling with my friend for his wife and for himself. Presently they laid the dead woman out, as was their custom; and setting her on a bier, carried her and her husband out of the city until they came to a place in the side of the mountain at the end of the island by the sea.

And here they raised a great rock and uncovered the mouth of a stone-lined pit or well, leading down into a vast underground cavern that ran beneath the mountain.

Into this pit they threw the corpse; then, tying a rope of palm fibre under the husband's armpits, they let him down into the cavern, and with him a great pitcher of fresh water and seven scones by way of provisions.

When he reached the bottom he loosed himself from the rope and they drew it up; and stopping the mouth of the pit with the great stone, went back to the city, leaving my friend in the cavern with his dead wife.

When I saw this I said to myself, "By Allah, this fashion of death is more grievous than the other!"

And I went in to the King and said, "My lord, why do you bury the living with the dead?"

Said he, "You must understand that it has been the custom of our forefathers and of our old kings from time out of mind: if the husband dies first, to bury his wife with him, and the same with the wife — so that we may not sever them, alive or dead."

I asked, "O King of the age, if the wife of a foreigner like myself dies among you, do you deal with him as you did with that man?"

And he answered, "Certainly; we do with him exactly as you have seen."

When I heard this my gall bladder was ready to burst for the violence of my dismay and my concern for myself. My wits were dazed; I felt as if I were in a foul dungeon; and I hated their society, for I went about in fear that my wife might die before me and they would bury me alive with her.

However, after a while I comforted myself, saying, "Perhaps I shall die before her, or shall have gone back to my own country before she dies; for nobody knows which will go first and which last."

Then I applied myself to turning my mind from this thought with various occupations; but it was not long before my wife fell ill and complained and took to her bed, and after a few days went to the mercy of Allah.

And the King and the rest of the people came, as was their custom, to condole with me and with her family and console us for her loss — and no less to condole with me for my own death.

Then the women washed her and, dressing her in her richest clothes and her golden ornaments, necklaces and jewellery, laid her on the bier and carried her to the mountain I have described, where they lifted the cover of the pit and threw her in.

After which all my intimates and acquaintances and my wife's family gathered round me, to say goodbye to me in my own lifetime and console me for my own death — while I cried out among them, saying:

"Almighty Allah never made it lawful to bury the living with the dead! I am a stranger and not one of your kind; and I cannot bear your custom; and if I had known of it I would never have married among you!"

They did not hear me and paid no attention to my words, but laying hold of me, bound me by force and let me down into the cavern with a large jar of sweet water and seven cakes of bread, according to their custom.

When I reached the bottom they called out to me to cast myself loose from the cords, but I refused to do it; so they threw them down on me, and closing the mouth of the pit with the stones I have described, went their ways:—

And Shahrazad saw the dawn breaking, and fell silent.
