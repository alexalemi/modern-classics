The Barber's Tale of Himself

I was living in Baghdad in the time of Al-Mustansir Billah, son of Al-Mustazi Billah, who was Caliph then — a prince who loved the poor and the needy and kept company with the learned and the pious.

One day it happened that he was angry with ten men, highwaymen who robbed on the Caliph's own road; and he ordered the Prefect of Baghdad to bring them into his presence on the anniversary of the Great Festival.

So the Prefect went out and made them his prisoners and embarked with them in a boat. I caught sight of them as they were getting aboard, and said to myself, "These men are surely gathered for a wedding feast; I think they mean to spend their day in that boat eating and drinking, and nobody shall share their cups but me."

So I got up, good company; and out of the excess of my courtesy and the gravity of my understanding, I got aboard with them and fell into conversation with them.

They rowed across to the opposite bank, where they landed; and up came the watch and the guardians of the peace with chains, which they put round the robbers' necks. And they chained me among the rest of them.

And, good people, is it not a proof of my courtesy and my sparingness of speech that I held my peace and did not care to say a word?

Then they took us away in irons; and next morning they carried the whole lot of us before Al-Mustansir Billah, Commander of the Faithful, who ordered the necks of the ten robbers to be struck off.

So the executioner came forward after they had been seated on the leather mat of blood; and drawing his blade, struck off one head after another until he had struck the neck of the tenth — and I alone was left.

The Caliph looked at me and asked the headsman, "What is the matter with you, that you have struck off only nine heads?"

And he answered, "Allah forbid that I should behead only nine when you ordered me to behead ten."

Said the Caliph, "It seems to me you have struck the necks of only nine, and this man in front of you is the tenth."

"By your beneficence," replied the headsman, "I have beheaded ten."

"Count them," cried the Caliph. And when they counted the heads — there were ten.

The Caliph looked at me and said, "What made you keep silent at a time like this? And how did you come to be in the company of these men of blood? Tell me the cause of it all; for although you are a very old man, your wits are certainly weak."

Now when I heard these words from the Caliph I sprang to my feet and replied:

"Know, O Commander of the Faithful, that I am the Silent Old Man, and I am called so to distinguish me from my six brothers. I am a man of immense learning; while as for the gravity of my understanding, the wiliness of my wits and the sparingness of my speech, there is no end to them; and my calling is that of a barber.

"I went out early yesterday morning and saw these men making for a skiff; and imagining they were bound for a wedding feast, I joined them and mixed in with them. After a while up came the watch and the guardians of the peace, who put chains round their necks and round mine along with the rest; but in the excess of my courtesy I held my peace and did not say a word — and that was nothing but generosity on my part.

"They brought us into your presence, and you gave an order to strike off the necks of the ten; and yet I did not make myself known to you, and stayed silent in front of the executioner, purely out of the great generosity and courtesy that led me to share their death with them.

"But all my life long I have dealt so nobly with mankind, and they pay me back with the foulest and most evil payment there is."

When the Caliph heard my words and knew that I was a man of surpassing generosity and very few words, one with no forwardness in him — as this young man would have it, whom I rescued from mortal danger and who has repaid me so scurvily — he laughed with such excessive laughter that he fell over on his back.

Then he said to me, "Silent Man, do your six brothers take after you in wisdom and knowledge and sparingness of speech?"

I replied: "They were never like me! You put a reproach on me, Commander of the Faithful, and it does not become you to set my brothers on a level with me; for out of the abundance of their speech and the deficiency of their courtesy and their gravity, every one of them has got himself some injury or other.

"One of them is one-eyed; one is palsied; one is stone blind; one is cropped of both his ears; one is shorn of both his lips; and one is a hunchback and a cripple.

"And do not imagine, Commander of the Faithful, that I am prodigal of speech; but I am obliged to explain to you that I am a man of greater worth and fewer words than any of them. From every one of my brothers there hangs a tale of how he came by his bodily defect, and these I will relate to you."
