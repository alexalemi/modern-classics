Jules Verne published Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea in 1870, after running it in instalments in the family magazine his publisher Hetzel produced. It belongs to the long series of adventure novels Hetzel marketed as the Voyages extraordinaires, and it is the one that did the most to invent science fiction as a form. The story opens in 1866 with ships all over the world reporting collisions with something enormous and fast in the open sea. A French marine biologist, Professor Pierre Aronnax, joins the American expedition sent to hunt it down, and is pulled overboard along with his servant Conseil and a Canadian harpooner named Ned Land. What picks them out of the water is the thing they were hunting: a submarine called the Nautilus, commanded by a man who calls himself Captain Nemo, which is Latin for nobody.

Submarines were not new in 1870 — hand-cranked boats had been built and had killed a warship in the American Civil War six years earlier — and that is exactly what Verne is writing against. The Nautilus runs on electricity, which its captain extracts from the sea itself, and the book's real subject is the claim that a man could cut himself off from every government on land and live entirely off the ocean. So the engineering matters. The long stretches where Aronnax counts the ship's fittings, reckons her displacement, or lists the fish going past the saloon window are not padding to be skipped; they are the argument that the thing would work, and they are kept here in full.

For a century English readers met this book only through Lewis Mercier's 1873 version, which cut close to a quarter of the text, mangled the science because the translator did not follow it, and toned down Nemo's rage at empire. Verne's standing in English as a writer of boys' books is largely the result of translations like that one. This edition is made from Verne's French, complete, as are the two other Verne novels in this collection.

Three things are worth knowing going in. Conseil never addresses Aronnax as "you" — he speaks to his employer in the third person, and the flat courtesy of it is his whole comedy. Verne's measurements are metric and his leagues are distance travelled, not depth: the title counts the length of the voyage. And Nemo tells you almost nothing about himself for a very long time, which is deliberate. The scenes people remember are the underwater funeral in the coral, the pearl diver at Ceylon, and the hunting party that walks out across the sea floor in diving suits.
