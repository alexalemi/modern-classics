James Stephens was born in Dublin, in 1880 according to the hospital register, though he himself gave the date as 2 February 1882, the day James Joyce was born. His father died when he was two, and at six he was committed to an industrial school for begging in the street. He was working as a solicitor's clerk when the poet George Russell, who wrote as Æ, came across his poems in 1907. His novel The Crock of Gold (1912) won the Polignac Prize, and from 1915 he was Registrar of the National Gallery of Ireland. At Easter 1916 he watched the fighting around St Stephen's Green and wrote an account of it, The Insurrection in Dublin. Irish Fairy Tales was published by Macmillan in 1920, while the Irish War of Independence was being fought.

The title is misleading in one respect. These are not nursery tales about small magical people. They are ten stories from the literature of early medieval Ireland, written down by monks from a much older tradition of kings, druids, gods and warriors. Many of them belong to the Fenian cycle, the stories of Fionn mac Uail and the Fianna, the band of hunter-warriors he led. The first, The Story of Tuan Mac Cairill, is found in an eleventh-century manuscript, the Book of the Dun Cow. In it a Christian saint, Finnian of Moville, comes to convert a stubborn old pagan and finds that the man has lived through the whole history of Ireland, in one animal shape after another, and remembers all of it.

Stephens was a poet, and he is not a translator of these stories but a teller of them in his own voice. He is funny, he digresses, and he stops the story to remark on women, or Time, or saints: "the gods that we do not approve of are treated scurvily, even by saintly men." The names are Irish and he sometimes says in brackets how to pronounce them — Fionn is "Fewn to rhyme with tune" — but a reader who simply goes on will not get lost. A few words recur. The Ard-Rí is the High King of Ireland, and a Shí is one of the hills where the people of the old gods live.

This is a restored edition, not a retelling: Stephens' words are unchanged. Arthur Rackham illustrated the 1920 book with sixteen colour plates, each captioned with a sentence from the story, and many drawings. The Project Gutenberg transcription this edition is built from dropped those captions and describes each plate only with a file code. This edition restores the sixteen printed captions from a scan of the 1920 printing, checking each against Stephens' own sentence near the plate, and adds alt text for every plate and drawing. It also adds modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
