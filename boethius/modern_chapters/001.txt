Chapter 1: Philosophy Appears and Drives Out the Muses

While I was brooding like this in silence, setting down my miserable complaints with my pen, it seemed to me that a woman appeared standing over my head, and her face was more than usually worth reverence. Her eyes burned like fire and saw further than human eyes see; her colour was fresh and her strength showed no sign of wearing out — and yet she was full of years, and plainly did not belong to our age at all. Her height was hard to judge. One moment she was no taller than anyone else; the next her forehead seemed to strike the sky, and when she lifted her head higher still she began to pierce into the heavens themselves and to defeat the eyes of anyone watching her. Her clothes were made of some imperishable stuff, woven of the finest thread with the most delicate workmanship; and she had made them herself with her own hands, as she told me later. Their beauty had been somewhat dulled by age and neglect, and they had the grimy look marble takes on when it is left out in the weather. Along the bottom edge was woven the Greek letter Π, which stands for the practical life; at the top the letter θ, for the life of thought; and between the two, steps like a staircase leading from the lower letter to the upper one. The robe had also been torn by violent hands, each of which had snatched away whatever it could grab. In her right hand she held a notebook, and in her left a staff.

And when she saw the Muses of Poetry standing by my bed, dictating the words of my lament, she was angry for a moment and her eyes flashed. "Who," she said, "let these stagey little tarts in to see a sick man? They have no medicine for his illness; they feed it with sweet poison. These are the ones who kill off the rich crop of reason with the barren thorns of feeling, who get people's minds used to being ill instead of setting them free. If it were some ordinary man your charms were seducing, as is usually the way with you, I would mind less. I should not have wasted my own work on him. But this man was brought up on the philosophy of Elea and the Academy. Go on, get out, you sirens — your sweetness does not last. Leave him to my Muses to look after and heal."

At this scolding the whole troop looked more downcast than ever, hung their heads, went red with shame, and left the room in misery.

But my sight was blurred with so much weeping, and I could not make out who this woman of such commanding authority was. I was struck dumb, and with my eyes fixed on the ground I went on silently waiting to see what she would do next. Then she came closer and sat down on the edge of my bed; and looking into my face, which was heavy with grief and turned down towards the ground in sadness, she lamented the disorder of my mind in these words:

Song II: His Despondency

	Look how deep his mind is sunk,
		how wildly it is thrown about!
	Always, always towards the dark
		it settles, its own light lost,
	whenever the winds that rise from earth
	beat on it, and the waves of worry climb.
	Once he walked the open sky,
		traced out the bright road of the sun,
	watched the cold moon fill and empty;
		and he would not rest until
	no star that steers among the spheres
	was missing from his reckoning.
	Why the blustering winds go out
		and trouble the calm face of the sea;
	whose hand it is that turns the world;
		and why the sun should run
	his even course from the red east
	down to the waters in the west;
	what it is that tempers so
		the mild hours of the spring,
	so that the earth breaks into roses
		and garlands itself with them;
	who loads the ripened year with grapes
	in clusters when the autumn comes:
	all this he knew — he always strove
		to guess at nature's deepest law.
	Now he lies with reason's light
		put out, his neck in chains,
	and forced down by the heavy weight,
	his eyes are fastened to dull earth.
