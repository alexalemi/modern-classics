Chapter 12: Goodness Governs the World, and the Paradox of Evil

Then I said: "I agree with Plato with all my heart. Indeed, this is now the second time these things have been brought back to my mind — first I lost them through the clogging contact of the body, and then through the weight of heavy grief."

Then she went on: "If you will think back over what you have already admitted, it will not be long before you also recollect the thing you confessed yourself ignorant of a while ago."

"What is that?" I said.

"The principles by which the world is governed," she said.

"Yes; I remember confessing it, and although I can already guess where you are going, I would like to hear the argument set out plainly."

"A little while ago you thought it beyond all doubt that God governs the world."

"I do not think it doubtful now, and never shall; and I will briefly set out the reasons that bring me to that assurance. This world could never have taken shape as a single system out of parts so diverse and opposed, if there were not One who joins such different things together. And once it had come together, the very diversity of its natures would have pulled it apart and torn it to pieces in universal discord, if there were not One who holds together what He has joined. Nor would the order of nature proceed so regularly, nor could its course display motions so exactly fixed in position, time, range, effect and character, unless there were One who, remaining unmoved Himself, arranged these various turns of change. This power, whatever it is, by which things remain as they were created and are kept in motion, I call by the name everyone recognises: God."

Then she said: "Since that is what you believe, it will cost me very little trouble, I think, to bring you to happiness and get you safely home to your own country. But let us give our attention to the task we set ourselves. Have we not counted independence as belonging to happiness, and agreed that God is happiness itself?"

"We certainly have."

"Then He will need no help from outside to rule the world. Otherwise, if He needs anything, He will not possess complete independence."

"That necessarily follows," I said.

"Then He arranges all things by His own power alone."

"That cannot be denied."

"Now, God was proved to be absolute good."

"Yes; I remember."

"Then He arranges all things by the agency of good, if it is true that the one who rules all things by His own power is the one we have agreed to be good — and *He* is, so to speak, the rudder and the tiller by which the mechanism of the world is kept steady and in order."

"I agree heartily; and in fact I saw what you were going to say, though perhaps only as a feeble guess."

"I well believe it," she said. "You now bring to the search, I think, eyes that are quicker at making out the truth. But what I shall say next is no less plain and easy to see."

"What is it?" I said.

"Why," she said, "since God is rightly believed to steer all things with the rudder of goodness, and since all things likewise, as I have taught you, hurry towards the good by the very aim of their nature, can it be doubted that His governing is willingly accepted, and that everything submits to the sway of the one who arranges it, being shaped and tuned to His rule?"

"Necessarily," I said. "No rule would look happy if it were a yoke imposed on unwilling wills rather than the safekeeping of obedient subjects."

"Then there is nothing which, while it follows its nature, tries to resist the good."

"No; nothing."

"But suppose something did: would it have the slightest success against the one we rightly agreed to be the supreme Lord of happiness?"

"It would be completely powerless."

"Then there is nothing that has either the will or the power to oppose this supreme good."

"No; I think not."

"So it is the supreme good," she said, "that rules in strength and graciously arranges all things."

Then I said: "How delighted I am by your reasonings, and by the conclusion you have brought them to — but most of all by these very words you are using! I am now at last ashamed of the foolishness that was tormenting me so badly."

"You have heard the story of the giants assaulting heaven; and a kindly strength dealt with them too, as they deserved. But shall we let our arguments collide with one another? Some fine spark of truth may be struck out of the impact."

"If that is your pleasure," I said.

"Nobody can doubt that God is all-powerful."

"Nobody at all who thinks consistently can question it."

"Now, there is nothing that One who is all-powerful cannot do."

"Nothing."

"But can God do evil, then?"

"No; by no means."

"Then evil is nothing," she said, "since the one to whom nothing is impossible is unable to do it."

"Are you making fun of me," I said, "weaving a maze of tangled arguments, seeming now to begin where you ended and now to end where you began? Or are you building up some marvellous circle of divine simplicity? For a little while ago you began with happiness, said it was the supreme good, and declared it to be seated in the supreme Godhead. You also affirmed that God Himself is the supreme good and complete happiness, and from that you went on to add, as if in passing, the proof that nobody would be happy unless he were a god as well. Then you said that the very form of the good is the essence both of God and of happiness, and you taught that the absolute One is the absolute good that all nature seeks. You maintained too that God rules the universe by the governance of goodness, that all things obey Him willingly, and that evil has no existence in nature. And you unfolded all of it without help from any assumption brought in from outside, using proofs that belong to the subject itself, each drawing its credit from the last."

Then she answered: "Making fun of you is the last thing I would do. By the blessing of God, whom we prayed to a moment ago, we have achieved the most important thing of all. For the form of the divine essence is such that it can neither pass out into things outside itself nor take anything external into itself; but, as Parmenides says of it,

"'in body like a sphere, on every side perfectly rounded,'

it rolls the restless globe of the universe while remaining motionless itself. And if I have used arguments that were not fetched in from outside but lay within the compass of our subject, you have no reason to be surprised, since you have learned on Plato's authority that words ought to be akin to the matter they treat of."

Song XII: Orpheus and Eurydice

		Happy the man whose feet have stood
	beside the fountain of good;
	happy the man whose will could break
	the chains of earth for wisdom's sake.
		The poet of Thrace, they say,
	mourned his dear wife, dead;
	the woods came trooping after him
	to hear that sorrowful strain,
	and the river stopped flowing,
	held still by so soft a grief;
	the deer lay down unafraid
	beside the lion,
	and the hound, quieted by the song,
	no longer hunted the hare.
	But the pain went unsoothed;
	it raged on in his own breast.
	The music that could calm
	all else brought him no comfort.
	Blaming the immortal powers,
	he came to the gate of Hell.
	There he breathed every tender thing
	into his sounding strings,
	every high-wrought melody
	his goddess mother had taught him —
	everything grief could lend him,
	and love that doubled the sorrow —
	until, as the echoes woke,
	all Taenarus was shaken;
	and he talked the king of the shades
	round into pity
	with his sweet pleading. Spellbound,
	the three-headed hound,
	at sounds so strangely sweet,
	falls crouching at his feet.
	The dread Avengers too,
	who hunt down guilty minds
	with fears that never let go,
	are all dissolved in tears.
	Ixion on his wheel
	feels a brief reprieve,
	for look — the wheel stands still.
	And while those sad notes thrill,
	Tantalus, maddened with thirst,
	listens, and forgets
	the stream's mockery
	and his long agony.
	The vulture, too, holds off
	a little while from tearing
	at Tityus' ripped-open side,
	full-fed and pacified.
		At last the shadowy king,
	pitying his sorrows,
	cried out: "He has won!
	We give him back his bride!
	She shall belong to him
	as the wages of his song.
	Only one condition
	is set upon the gift:
	he must not turn his eyes
	to look at his hard-won prize
	until the two have safely passed
	the gates of Hell." Alas —
	what law can move lovers?
	Love is a higher law.
	For Orpheus — the pity of it! —
	with the threshold of day all but won,
	looked at his Eurydice,
	lost her, and was undone.
		You who are chasing the light,
	this story is for you,
	you who are looking for the way
	up into the clearer day.
	If you cast one backward look
	at the darkness you have left,
	your weak and wandering eyes
	have lost the matchless prize.
