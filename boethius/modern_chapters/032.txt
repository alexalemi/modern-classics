Chapter 6: Fate and Providence

"True," I said; "but since it is your business to unfold the hidden causes of things and explain principles that are veiled in darkness, please tell me your own conclusions on this matter, since the wonder of it disturbs my mind more than anything else."

A smile played for a moment on her lips as she answered: "You are calling me to the greatest of all subjects of inquiry, a task the most exhaustive treatment barely covers. Its nature is such that as fast as one doubt is cut away, countless others spring up like the Hydra's heads; and there would be no limit to their sprouting again unless we put them down with the living fire of the mind. For the subject takes in the questions of the essential simplicity of providence, the order of fate, unforeseen chance, divine knowledge and predestination, and the freedom of the will. How heavy all that weighs you can judge for yourself. But since knowing these things is also part of the treatment of your illness, we will try to give them some consideration, in spite of the narrow limits of our time. What is more, you must do without the pleasures of music and song for a while, if you find any delight in them, while I weave the connected chain of reasons together in its proper order."

"As you wish," I said.

Then, as though making a fresh start, she began: "The coming into being of all things, the whole course of development in things that change, every sort of thing that moves in any way at all, receives its due cause, order and form from the steadfastness of the divine mind. That mind, calm in the citadel of its own essential simplicity, has decreed that its method of ruling shall be manifold. Seen in the very purity of the divine intelligence, that method is called *providence*; but seen with regard to the things it moves and arranges, it is what the ancients called *fate*. That these two are different will easily become clear to anyone who reviews what each of them does. Providence is the divine reason itself, seated in the Supreme Being, which arranges all things; fate is the arrangement inherent in all moving things, by which providence joins everything in its proper order. Providence embraces all things, however different, however infinite; fate sets individual things in motion separately, and assigns each of them its position, form and time.

"So the unfolding of this temporal order, gathered into one in the foresight of the divine mind, is providence; while the same unity, broken up and unfolded in time, is fate. And although they are different, there is a dependence between them, for the order of destiny issues from the essential simplicity of providence. Just as a craftsman first forms the idea of the thing to be made in his mind, then carries out his design and develops from moment to moment what he had seen before as a single whole in one instant, so God in His providence ordains all things as parts of a single unchanging whole, but carries out those very ordinances by fate, in a time of manifold unity. So whether fate is worked out by divine spirits as the servants of providence, or by a soul, or by the service of all nature — whether by the heavenly motion of the stars, by the power of angels, or by the many-sided cunning of demons — whether the destined series is woven by all of these or by some of them, this at least is clear: providence is the fixed and simple form of destined events, and fate is their shifting series in the order of time, as they are to take place by the arrangement of divine simplicity. And so all things that are under fate are subject to providence as well, since fate itself depends on it; whereas certain things that are set under providence are above the chain of fate — namely those things which by their nearness to the primal Divinity are steadfastly fixed and lie outside the order of fate's movements. For consider several circles revolving round the same centre. The innermost approaches the simplicity of the middle point, and is a kind of pivot for the outer circles to turn round, while the outermost, whirled in a wider orbit, sweeps through more and more space the further it gets from the indivisible unity of the centre; and whatever joins and allies itself to the centre is narrowed to a like simplicity and no longer spreads vaguely out into space. In just that way, whatever departs a long way from the primal mind gets more deeply entangled in the meshes of fate, and things are free of fate in proportion as they try to come nearer to that central pivot; while anything that clings close to the supreme mind in its absolute fixity is free of movement too, and rises above the necessity of fate. So what reasoning is to pure intelligence, what the generated is to what simply is, what time is to eternity, what a circle is to its centre — that is what the shifting series of fate is to the steadfastness and simplicity of providence.

"It is this causal series that moves the heavens and the stars, tunes the elements to mutual agreement and then in turn transforms them into new combinations; *this* is what renews the series of everything that is born and dies through the like succession of seed and birth; it is *its* working that binds the destinies of men in an unbreakable knot of causality — and since it issues at its source from unalterable providence, those destinies too must necessarily be unalterable. And so the world is ruled for the best if this unity, remaining in the divine mind, puts forth an inflexible order of causes. That order, by its intrinsic unchangeableness, holds in check changeable things that would otherwise ebb and flow at random. And so it happens that although to you, who are not entirely capable of understanding this order, everything looks confused and disordered, there is nevertheless an appointed limit everywhere, guiding all things towards good. Indeed nothing can be done for the sake of evil even by the wicked themselves — for, as we proved abundantly, they seek good and are dragged out of the way by a twisted error; still less can this order, which sets out from the supreme centre of good, turn aside anywhere from the road it began on.

"'And yet,' you will say, 'what confusion could be more unjust than that prosperity and adversity should fall on the good without distinction, and that what the bad like and what they loathe should come to them by turns?' Yes; but are men in real life so sound of mind that their judgments of righteousness and wickedness must necessarily correspond to the facts? Why, on this very point their verdicts contradict one another, and the men some think deserve a reward others think deserve punishment. But suppose there were someone who could rightly tell the good from the bad: would he be able to see into the innermost constitution of a soul, as we say of a body? The wonder here is not unlike what astonishes a man who does not know why, in health, sweet things suit some constitutions and bitter things others, or why some sick men are best relieved by mild remedies and others by severe ones. But the doctor, who can tell the precise conditions and characteristics of health and sickness, is not astonished. Now the health of a soul is nothing but righteousness, and vice is its sickness. It is God, the guide and physician of the mind, who preserves the good and drives out the bad. He looks out from the high watchtower of His providence, sees what suits each man, and assigns what He knows to be suitable.

"So this is what that extraordinary mystery of the order of destiny comes down to: something is done by one who knows, and the ignorant are astonished by it. But let us consider a few instances that show how competent human reason is to fathom the divine unsearchableness. Here is a man you take to be the perfection of justice and scrupulous integrity; to all-knowing Providence he looks quite otherwise. We all know our Lucan's remark that the winning cause found favour with the gods, and the beaten cause with Cato. So if you see something in this world turning out differently from what you expected, do not doubt that events are rightly ordered; the twisted confusion is in your judgment of them.

"But grant that somewhere there is found a man of so fortunate a character that God and man agree in their judgments about him: still he may be somewhat weak in strength of mind. It may be that if he falls into adversity he will give up practising the innocence that failed to secure his fortune. So God's wise arrangement spares the man whom adversity might make worse, and will not let a man suffer who is ill fitted to endure. There is another, perfect in every virtue, so holy and so near to God that providence judges it unlawful for anything harmful to happen to him — will not even allow him to be afflicted with bodily illness. As a better man than I has said:

"'The very body of the holy saint is built of purest ether.'

It often happens that government is given to the good so that a restraint may be put on the overflow of wickedness. To others providence assigns some mixed lot suited to their spiritual nature: some it will trouble in case they run rank through long prosperity; others it will let be harassed by painful afflictions so as to strengthen their virtues by the exercise and practice of patience. Some are too afraid of what they have the strength to bear; others are too dismissive of what their strength is unequal to. All of these it brings to the test of their true selves through misfortune. Some, too, have bought a name that later ages revere at the price of a glorious death; some by unconquerable steadiness under suffering have shown others by their example that virtue cannot be beaten by disaster. All of which, beyond doubt, comes about rightly and in due order, and to the benefit of the people it is seen to happen to.

"As for the other side of the wonder — that the bad now meet with affliction and now get their hearts' desire — that springs from the same causes. Nobody, of course, is surprised at the afflictions, since everybody holds that the wicked deserve to suffer. The truth is that their punishments both frighten others away from crime and improve the men they are inflicted on; while their prosperity is a powerful sermon to the good about what judgments they ought to pass on good fortune of that kind, which so often waits on the wicked so attentively.

"There is another object that may, I think, be achieved in such cases. There is perhaps a man whose nature is so reckless and violent that poverty would drive him more desperately into crime. *His* disorder providence relieves by letting him pile up money. Such a man, uneasy in a conscience stained with guilt, comparing his character with his fortune, may perhaps grow afraid of coming to mourn the loss of what it is so pleasant to have. So he will reform his ways, and out of fear of losing his fortune he abandons his wickedness. Some, carrying prosperity unworthily, have been flung headlong into ruin; to others the power of the sword has been given, so that the good may be tested by discipline and the bad punished. For just as there can be no peace between the righteous and the wicked, neither can the wicked agree among themselves. How should they, when each of them is at odds with himself, because his vices tear his conscience, and he often does things which, once done, he judges ought not to have been done? Hence this supreme providence brings about that notable wonder: the bad make the bad good. For some, when they see the injustice they suffer themselves at the hands of evildoers, are inflamed with loathing for the offenders, and in trying to be unlike the people they hate they come back to the ways of virtue. It is divine power alone that can make evil things good too, by putting them to a suitable use and bringing them in the end to some good outcome. For order embraces all things in one way or another, so that even what has departed from the appointed laws of the order still falls within *an* order — a different one, but *another* order — so that nothing in the realm of providence may be left to happen at random. But

"'Hard would the task be, even for a god, to recount it all, leaving nothing out.'

Nor is it lawful for a man to grasp the whole mechanism of the divine work in thought, or to set it out in speech. Let us be content with having understood just this much: that God, the creator of all nature, likewise arranges all things and guides them towards good; and while He works to preserve everything He has created in likeness to Himself, He banishes all evil from the borders of His commonwealth through the links of fated necessity. And so it comes about that if you look at the providence that does the arranging, you will nowhere find the evils that are believed to swarm on earth.

"But I see you have long been weighed down by the weight of the subject and tired out by the length of the argument, and are now looking for some refreshment of sweet poetry. Listen, then, and may the draught so restore you that you will bend your mind more resolutely to what is left."

Song VI: The Universal Aim

		If you want to see with an unclouded mind
	the laws God has designed,
	lift your steady gaze up high
	to the canopy of stars;
	see all the constellations move
	in a rightful league of love.
	The fiery Sun in full career
	never blocks the cold Moon's sphere;
	when the Bear at heaven's height
	wheels his horses' rapid flight,
	though he sees the starry train
	sinking in the western sea,
	he does not grudge it, and does not want
	to quench his own fires in the flood.
		In the true sequence laid down,
	morning and evening follow each day;
	the Evening Star brings on the shades of night,
	the Morning Star brings the light.
	Love, taking its turn as it should,
	keeps renewing the cycle,
	and discordant strife is driven
	out of the starry realm of heaven.
	So, in a marvellous friendship,
	the warring elements agree;
	hot and cold, and wet and dry
	lay their ancient quarrel by;
	the flickering flame climbs high,
	and earth tends downward for ever.
		So the year in spring's mild hours
	loads the air with the scent of flowers;
	summer paints the grain gold;
	then, when autumn comes round again,
	the orchards glow bright with fruit;
	winter brings the rain and the snow.
	So the fixed progression of the seasons,
	tempered in a due succession,
	nourishes and brings to birth
	everything that lives and breathes on earth.
	Then, when life's little day has run,
	it takes away all it brought.
		But One sits and holds the reins,
	who made everything and sustains it all;
	King and Lord and Fountainhead,
	most holy Judge, most dreadful Law;
	now He drives on and now holds back,
	and keeps each wanderer in the track.
	If that power were ever withheld
	that compels the circling spheres
	to turn in their orbits,
	this world's order would dissolve,
	and the harmonious whole would fall
	in one hideous ruin.
		But through this connected frame
	runs one universal aim;
	all things tend towards the Good,
	many paths, but one end.
	For nothing lasts unless it turns
	back in its course, and longs
	to flow again to that Source
	from which its being was first taken.
