Chapter 5: The Sickness Is in His Mind, Not His Fortune

When I had poured out my grievances in this long unbroken wail, she, with a calm face and not in the least put out by my complaints, said:

"When I saw you miserable and in tears, I knew at once that you were wretched and an exile. But how distant an exile I would not have known if your own speech had not told me. And yet how far from your country have you really been banished? Say rather that you have wandered off — or, if you insist on calling it banishment, that you have banished yourself, since nobody else could ever lawfully have had that power over you.

"Now if you will call to mind what country you come from, it is not governed, as Athens once was, by the sovereignty of the crowd. 'One is its ruler, one its king,' and he takes pleasure in the number of his citizens, not in their exile; and to submit to his government and obey his laws is perfect freedom. Do you not know that most ancient law of this country of yours, which lays down that no one at all who has chosen to settle there may be sent into exile? For there is truly no danger that a man inside its walls and defences should deserve to be exiled. But the man who has stopped wanting to live there has at the same moment stopped deserving to.

"So it is not the look of this place that moves me so much as the look of you. What I miss is not walls of a library set off with glass and ivory but the room in your mind, where I once put — not books, but the thing that gives books their value: the doctrines that are in mine.

"Now, what you have said about your services to the state is true, though far too little compared with the size of what you deserve. The things charged against you that you have described, whether they are to your credit or simply false accusations, are public knowledge. As for the crimes and deceptions of the informers, you were right to think it best to pass over them lightly, because popular opinion has passed a better and fuller judgement on them already. You have complained bitterly about the injustice of the senate. You have grieved that I have been slandered, and lamented the damage to my reputation. And at last your anger blazed out against Fortune, and you complained about how unfairly your merits have been repaid. Finally your frantic muse framed a prayer that the peace which rules in heaven might rule the earth as well.

"But since a crowd of unruly passions has attacked your mind, and you are pulled apart by anger and pain and grief, strong remedies are not right for you in your present state. So for a while I shall use gentler methods, so that the swellings which have hardened under this inflow of disturbing passion may be softened by mild treatment, until they can stand the force of sharper cures."

Song VI: All Things Have Their Needful Order

	The man who trusts the generous seed
		to furrows that will not take it
	when the Crab is at its fiercest
		and scorching all the plain
	will find his barn empty
	and live on acorns instead.
	Do not go out to gather violets
		from the purple hillside
	while the furious winds of winter
		are sweeping down the valleys;
	do not rush the grape to the press
	in the middle of spring.
	God has given each thing
		its own appointed season,
	and allows no confusing change
		in that great plan of his.
	So whoever leaves the proper order
	will regret how it turns out.
