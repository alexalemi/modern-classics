Chapter 7: All Fortune Is Good Fortune

"Do you see, then, what follows from everything we have said?"

"No; what follows?"

"That absolutely every fortune is good fortune."

"And how can that be?" I said.

"Listen," she said. "Since every fortune, welcome and unwelcome alike, has for its object the rewarding or testing of the good and the punishing or improving of the bad, every fortune must be good, since it is either just or useful."

"The reasoning is perfectly true," I said, "and the conclusion, as long as I keep in mind the providence and the fate you have taught me about, rests on a strong foundation. Still, with your permission, we will count it among the propositions you called paradoxical a while ago."

"Why is that?" she said.

"Because ordinary speech is apt to say, and to say often, that some men's fortune is bad."

"Shall we come a little closer for a while, then, to the language ordinary people use, so as not to seem to have wandered too far from human custom?"

"As you please," I said.

"You call what does you good, good, do you not?"

"Certainly."

"And whatever tests or improves a man does him good?"

"Granted."

"And so is good?"

"Of course."

"Well, this is *their* case who have attained virtue and are at war with adversity, or who are turning away from vice and taking hold of the path of virtue."

"I cannot deny it."

"What about the good fortune that is given as a reward to the good — do ordinary people judge that bad?"

"Anything but; they think it is the best there is, as indeed it is."

"What, then, about the one case left — the fortune which, harsh though it is, puts the restraint of just punishment on the bad? Does popular opinion count that good?"

"No; of everything imaginable it is reckoned the most miserable."

"Then see whether, in following popular opinion, we have not ended up with a thoroughly paradoxical conclusion."

"How so?" I said.

"Why, it follows from what we have admitted that for everyone who has attained virtue, or is advancing in it, or is aiming at it, fortune is good in every case; while for those who stay in their wickedness fortune is always utterly bad."

"That is true," I said, "though nobody would dare admit it."

"And so," she said, "the wise man ought not to take it badly if he is ever caught up in one of fortune's battles, any more than it suits a brave soldier to be annoyed whenever the trumpet sounds for the fight. For each of them the time of trial is exactly the opportunity — for the soldier to win glory, for the wise man to perfect his wisdom. That is where virtue gets its name, in fact: relying on its own strength, it does not give way to adversity. And you who have taken your stand on virtue's steep ascent — it is not for you to be dissolved in delights or made feeble by pleasure. You close with all the turns of fortune in a struggle, and a very sharp one, so as not to let foul fortune overwhelm you or fair fortune corrupt you. Hold the middle with all your strength. Whatever falls short of it or goes beyond it is loaded with contempt for happiness, and misses the reward of the effort. It rests with you to make your fortune what you want it to be. For every harsh-looking fortune, unless it disciplines or improves, is a punishment."

Song VII: The Hero's Path

	Ten years of tedious war dragged on
		before Troy's smoking ruins paid
		for a marriage defiled and a promise betrayed,
	and the great son of Atreus had his anger eased.
	But when heaven's anger demanded a life
		and baffling winds held back his fleet,
		the king put his fatherhood aside
	and killed his own child with a priest's knife.
	When by the cave's flickering light
		Odysseus saw his dear companions
		swallowed in the huge Cyclops' hideous jaws,
	he wept at the pitiful sight.
	But soon, blinded and wild with pain —
		in bitter tears and misery —
		grim Polyphemus paid in his turn
	for the unholy pleasure of that foul feast.
	The labours of Hercules win him
		a name of glory far and wide;
		he broke the Centaurs' arrogant pride
	and stripped the lion of its skin.
	He shot the foul birds down with unerring arrows;
		he stole the golden fruit — the dragon
		kept its watch for nothing; and with a triple chain
	he hauled Cerberus up out of hell.
	He fed the wild horses
		on their own savage master's flesh; he beat the Hydra
		with fire. Maimed Achelous hid his head
	in shame beneath his own waves.
	Huge Cacus was killed for his crimes;
		Antaeus was flung down on Libya's sand;
		the great boar's slaver stained
	the shoulders that were to hold up the world.
	The last labour of all: his strength held up
		the globe of heaven, and he did not bend
		beneath it; and that toil, the end of his labours,
	won him the prize of heaven's high glory.
	Brave hearts, press on! Look — these bright examples
		lead the way to heaven! Do not turn
		your backs and run from the fight;
	win the struggle on earth, and the stars are your reward.
