In 523 Boethius was the most powerful civilian official in Italy. He was a Roman aristocrat serving Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king who then ruled the peninsula; he had been consul, his two sons had been consuls together the year before, and he had set out to translate the whole of Plato and Aristotle into Latin, getting as far as most of Aristotle's logic before he was stopped. He had set out to translate all of Plato and Aristotle into Latin before the language was lost. Then he defended a senator accused of treasonable correspondence with Constantinople, was charged with the same offence himself, condemned without a hearing, and held in custody at Pavia. He wrote this book there, and was executed shortly afterwards.

It opens with him feeling sorry for himself, writing verses about his own misfortune with the Muses of poetry standing at his elbow. A tall woman appears in the cell, throws the Muses out for making the sickness worse, and identifies herself as Philosophy. The rest is a conversation between the two of them across five books: on Fortune and what she ever promised, on what happiness actually is, on why the wicked appear to prosper, and finally on whether God's knowing tomorrow leaves anything for a person to decide. He never mentions his sentence again.

The book alternates prose argument with poems — thirty-nine of them, one after most chapters. They are not ornament. Philosophy uses them deliberately, as a change of medicine, and they carry the argument restated as feeling. All thirty-nine are here, as verse, and they are unrhymed, because Boethius wrote them unrhymed in some twenty-eight different classical metres. The rhyme in the Victorian English this edition works from was the translator's own addition. Abridged editions cut the poems, which removes half the book.

There is not one Christian doctrine in it. No scripture, no Christ, no resurrection, nothing that would place its author in any church — a Christian under sentence of death consoling himself entirely by argument. That matters because of who read it afterwards. For a thousand years this was the book educated Europe actually had by heart: Alfred the Great put it into Old English, Chaucer into Middle English, Elizabeth I into her own hand. Fortune's wheel is in every medieval manuscript because of Book Two, where Fortune stops being a figure of speech and speaks in her own defence.

The definition of eternity in Book Five — the complete and perfect possession of endless life all at once — is the one Aquinas took over whole. It is also, given where the man was sitting, the point of the whole exercise.
