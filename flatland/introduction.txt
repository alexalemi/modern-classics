Flatland was published in London in 1884 by Edwin Abbott Abbott, a clergyman and schoolmaster who was then headmaster of the City of London School. His other books are about Greek grammar, Shakespeare's English, and the New Testament. He put this one out under the name A Square, which is also the narrator: a two-dimensional being who lives in a plane, and who is writing to us from prison.

The first half is a description of his world, and it reads as a straight-faced anthropology. Everything in Flatland is a shape, and the number of sides you have is your rank. Isosceles triangles are labourers and soldiers, equilateral triangles tradesmen, squares and pentagons professional men, and the priests who run the country are polygons of so many sides that they count as circles. A son is born with one more side than his father, so a family climbs by degrees over generations, and a child born with unequal sides is treated as a criminal from birth. Women are straight lines. They have no angles, are held therefore to have no minds, are forbidden education, and are required by law to sway constantly and to keep up a cry so that nobody walks into their sharp end and dies.

The second half is the argument the book is famous for. A sphere from three dimensions visits the Square, who can only see it as a circle that appears out of nowhere, grows, shrinks and vanishes. The sphere finally lifts him out of his plane, and he sees his own house from above with everything inside it laid open. Then he does the thing that gets him locked up. He points out that if a point makes a line and a line makes a square and a square makes a cube, there is no reason for the series to stop at three, and he asks his teacher to take him higher. It is still the clearest short account there is of how to think about a fourth dimension, and it needs no mathematics beyond counting corners.

How much of the social half is satire and how much is the author's own view is a question readers have argued about since the book came out, and it is not settled here. Abbott was asked it in his own lifetime. The preface he added to the second edition, printed at the end of this volume, answers the charge of hating women by having the Square report that seven years in a cell have changed his mind about women and about the lower classes — which some readers take as the point of the whole exercise, and others as a man patching a hole. Both readings are available in the text. The complete book is here, including that preface, so the reader can settle it themselves.
