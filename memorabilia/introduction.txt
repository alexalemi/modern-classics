In 399 BC an Athenian jury convicted Socrates of refusing to recognize the gods of the city, of bringing in new divinities of his own, and of corrupting the young, and sentenced him to death. Xenophon, who had spent time with him as a young man, was not in Athens for the trial. He had gone east in 401 with a Greek mercenary army hired by a Persian prince, the prince had been killed, the generals had been murdered at a parley, and Xenophon had ended up leading ten thousand men home through hostile country — the march he wrote up afterwards as the Anabasis. He was an Athenian cavalryman turned soldier of fortune, later an exile living on an estate in the Peloponnese, and he wrote books about horsemanship, hunting, household management and Spartan government. This one, written around 371 BC, is his defense of his old teacher.

It opens by quoting the indictment and taking it apart clause by clause. Socrates sacrificed publicly and often; the divine sign he spoke of was nothing more exotic than what any Athenian claimed from omens; far from corrupting the young, he made them better at everything they tried. Once that is done, Xenophon spends the remaining three and a half books proving it the only way he can, by reporting conversation after conversation.

The reason to read it is the other portrait. Plato's dialogues are in this collection, and Plato's Socrates professes to know nothing, dismantles other men's definitions without supplying his own, and in the later dialogues is carrying Plato's philosophy. Xenophon's Socrates gives advice and expects it to be taken. He tells a shy, competent man that he owes the city his service and should go into politics, and tells a brilliant twenty-year-old that he is nowhere near ready. He talks a man whose household has filled up with refugee relatives into putting them to work. He patches up two quarreling brothers, discusses friendship with a courtesan named Theodote, and explains why a general needs the same skills as a good estate manager. Which of the two men is the real Socrates is an argument that has run for two thousand years without settling, and Xenophon was a soldier rather than a philosopher, which shows in what he found worth remembering.

Book Two has the best-known passage: the fable Prodicus told about the young Heracles meeting Virtue and Vice at a crossroads, each making her case for the life he should choose. John Adams proposed it as the design for the Great Seal of the United States.

This edition is the complete Memorabilia, all four books, modernized from H. G. Dakyns' English translation of 1897. Xenophon's other Socratic works — the Apology, the Symposium, the Oeconomicus — are separate books and are not included here.
