Chapter One: An Opinion That Has Never Been Put on Trial

(Part 1 of 2)

The object of this essay is to explain as clearly as I can the grounds of an opinion I have held ever since I first formed any opinions at all about social or political matters, and which, instead of being weakened or modified, has grown steadily stronger with reflection and with the experience of life. It is this: that the principle regulating the existing social relations between the two sexes — the legal subordination of one sex to the other — is wrong in itself, and is now one of the chief obstacles to human improvement; and that it ought to be replaced by a principle of perfect equality, allowing no power or privilege on the one side and no legal disability on the other.

The very words needed to state the task I have taken on show how hard it is. But it would be a mistake to suppose that the difficulty lies in the weakness or obscurity of the reasons my conviction rests on. The difficulty is the one that exists in every case where there is a mass of feeling to be contended with. So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability from having the weight of argument against it. If it had been accepted as the result of argument, refuting the argument might shake the conviction; but when it rests on feeling alone, the worse it fares in argument the more its adherents are persuaded that their feeling must have some deeper ground the arguments do not reach — and while the feeling lasts it keeps throwing up fresh entrenchments of argument to repair any breach made in the old ones. And there are so many causes tending to make the feelings on this particular subject the most intense and deeply rooted of all the feelings that gather round old institutions and customs to protect them, that we need not be surprised to find them less undermined and loosened so far than any of the rest by the progress of the great modern spiritual and social transition — nor suppose that the barbarisms men cling to longest must be less barbarous than the ones they shake off earlier.

In every respect the burden is heavy on anyone attacking an almost universal opinion. They must be very lucky as well as unusually able to get a hearing at all. They have more trouble obtaining a trial than other litigants have obtaining a verdict. And if they do extort a hearing, they are subjected to a set of logical requirements entirely different from the ones demanded of anyone else. In every other case the burden of proof is supposed to rest with the affirmative. If a man is charged with murder, it rests with his accusers to prove his guilt, not with him to prove his innocence. If there is a difference of opinion about whether some alleged historical event really happened — the Siege of Troy, say, in which men's feelings are not much involved — those who maintain that it happened are expected to produce their proofs before the other side can be required to say anything at all; and even then the other side need do no more than show that the evidence produced is worthless. Again, in practical matters the burden of proof is supposed to lie with those who are against liberty, who argue for some restriction or prohibition, whether a limitation on the general freedom of human action or some disqualification or inequality of privilege affecting one person or one class of persons as against others. The *a priori* presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality. The rule is held to be that there should be no restraint the general good does not require, and that the law should be no respecter of persons but should treat everyone alike, except where positive reasons of justice or policy require otherwise.

But none of these rules of evidence will be allowed to benefit those who hold the opinion I profess. It is useless for me to point out that those who maintain the doctrine that men have a right to command and women a duty to obey, or that men are fit for government and women unfit, are on the affirmative side of the question, and are bound to produce positive evidence for their assertions or else see them rejected. It is equally useless for me to point out that those who deny women any freedom or privilege rightly allowed to men have a double presumption against them — they are opposing freedom and recommending partiality — and must therefore be held to the strictest proof of their case, so that unless they succeed beyond all doubt the judgment ought to go against them. These would be thought good pleas in any ordinary case; they will not be thought so in this one. Before I could hope to make any impression I should be expected not only to answer everything that has ever been said on the other side, but to imagine everything that could be said — to supply my opponents with reasons as well as answer all the reasons I find; and besides refuting every argument for the affirmative, I shall be called on for unanswerable positive arguments to prove a negative. And even if I could do all that, and leave the other party with a host of unanswered arguments against them and not a single unrefuted one on their side, I should be thought to have done very little; for a cause supported on the one hand by universal custom, and on the other by such a preponderance of popular feeling, is supposed to have a presumption in its favour stronger than any conviction an appeal to reason can produce in any but a few first-rate minds.

I do not mention these difficulties in order to complain of them. In the first place it would be useless: they are inseparable from having to contend, through people's understandings, against the hostility of their feelings and their practical habits. And truly the understandings of most of mankind would have to be much better cultivated than they have ever yet been before they could be asked to place such reliance on their own power of weighing arguments as to give up practical principles they were born and bred in, principles that much of the existing order of the world rests on, at the first argumentative attack they cannot logically resist. So I do not quarrel with them for having too little faith in argument, but for having too much faith in custom and general feeling. It is one of the characteristic prejudices of the nineteenth century's reaction against the eighteenth to grant to the unreasoning elements of human nature the infallibility that the eighteenth century is supposed to have ascribed to the reasoning ones. For the worship of Reason we have substituted the worship of Instinct — and we call everything instinct that we find in ourselves and cannot trace to any rational foundation. This idolatry, infinitely more degrading than the other, and the most pernicious of the false worships of the present day, of all of which it is now the main support, will probably hold its ground until it gives way to a sound psychology laying bare the real root of much that is bowed down to as the intention of Nature and the ordinance of God.

As far as the present question goes, I am willing to accept the unfavourable conditions that this prejudice assigns me. I consent that established custom and general feeling shall be taken as conclusive against me — unless that custom and feeling can be shown, age by age, to have owed their existence to something other than their soundness, and to have drawn their power from the worse rather than the better parts of human nature. I am willing that judgment should go against me unless I can show that my judge has been tampered with. The concession is not as great as it may look, because proving that is by far the easiest part of my task.

That a practice is general is in some cases a strong presumption that it is, or at any rate once was, useful for some praiseworthy end. That holds where the practice was adopted in the first place, or afterwards kept up, as a means to such an end, and was grounded on experience of how the end could best be reached. If the authority of men over women, when it was first established, had been the result of a conscientious comparison between different ways of constituting the government of society — if, after trying various other forms of social organisation, the government of women over men, equality between the two, and whatever mixed and divided arrangements might be devised, it had been decided on the evidence of experience that the arrangement in which women are wholly under the rule of men, with no share at all in public affairs, and each of them privately under a legal obligation to obey the man she has joined her destiny to, was the arrangement most conducive to the happiness and well-being of both — then its general adoption might fairly be thought some evidence that at the time it was adopted it was the best. Even then, the considerations that recommended it might, like so many other primeval social facts of the greatest importance, have ceased in the course of ages to exist.

But the state of the case is the reverse of this in every respect. In the first place, the opinion in favour of the present system, which entirely subordinates the weaker sex to the stronger, rests on theory only, since no other system has ever been tried. So experience, in the sense in which it is commonly contrasted with theory, cannot be claimed to have delivered any verdict at all. And in the second place, the adoption of this system of inequality was never the result of deliberation, or forethought, or any social theory, or any notion whatever of what would benefit humanity or the good order of society. It arose simply from the fact that from the very earliest twilight of human society every woman — because of the value men set on her, combined with her inferiority in muscular strength — was found in a state of bondage to some man. Laws and systems of government always begin by recognising the relations they find already existing between individuals. They convert what was a mere physical fact into a legal right, give it the sanction of society, and chiefly aim at substituting public and organised means of asserting and protecting these rights for the irregular and lawless conflict of physical strength. Those who had already been compelled to obey became in this way legally bound to obey. Slavery, from being a mere matter of force between master and slave, became regularised and a matter of agreement among the masters, who bound themselves to one another for common protection and guaranteed by their collective strength the private possessions of each, slaves included.

In early times the great majority of the male sex were slaves, as well as the whole of the female sex. And many ages went by, some of them ages of high cultivation, before any thinker was bold enough to question the rightfulness — and the absolute social necessity — of either the one slavery or the other. By degrees such thinkers did appear; and, with the general progress of society helping, the slavery of the male sex has at last been abolished in all the countries of Christian Europe, though in one of them only within the last few years, and that of the female sex has been gradually changed into a milder form of dependence. But that dependence as it now exists is not an original institution, taking a fresh start from considerations of justice and social expediency. It is the primitive state of slavery lasting on, through a succession of mitigations and modifications brought about by the same causes that have softened manners in general and brought all human relations more under the control of justice and the influence of humanity. It has not lost the taint of its brutal origin. No presumption in its favour, therefore, can be drawn from the fact that it exists. The only presumption it could be supposed to have must rest on its having lasted until now, when so many other things that came down from the same odious source have been done away with. And that is what makes it sound strange to ordinary ears to hear it asserted that the inequality of rights between men and women has no other source than the law of the strongest.

That this statement should have the effect of a paradox is in some ways creditable to the progress of civilisation and the improvement of mankind's moral feelings. We now live — that is, one or two of the most advanced nations of the world now live — in a state in which the law of the strongest seems to have been entirely abandoned as the regulating principle of the world's affairs. Nobody professes it, and in most of the relations between human beings nobody is permitted to practise it. When anyone does succeed in practising it, it is under cover of some pretext that gives him the appearance of having some general social interest on his side. This being how things look, people flatter themselves that the rule of mere force is over, and that the law of the strongest cannot be the reason for the existence of anything that has remained in full operation down to the present day. However any of our present institutions may have begun, they think, it can only have been preserved into this period of advanced civilisation by a well-grounded sense of its fitness for human nature and its usefulness to the general good. They do not understand the great vitality and durability of institutions that put right on the side of might: how intensely such institutions are clung to; how the good propensities of those who hold power, as well as the bad ones, become identified with keeping it; how slowly these bad institutions give way, one at a time, the weakest first, beginning with the ones least interwoven with the daily habits of life; and how very rarely those who obtained legal power because they first had physical power have ever lost their grip on it until the physical power had passed to the other side. No such shift of physical force has taken place in the case of women; and this fact, combined with all the peculiar and characteristic features of the particular case, made it certain from the first that this branch of the system of right founded on might would be the very last to disappear, though its most atrocious features were softened earlier than those of several of the others. It was inevitable that this one case of a social relation grounded on force would survive through generations of institutions grounded on equal justice, an almost solitary exception to the general character of their laws and customs — but one which, so long as it does not proclaim its own origin, and so long as discussion has not brought out its true character, is not felt to jar with modern civilisation, any more than domestic slavery among the Greeks jarred with their idea of themselves as a free people.

The truth is that people of the present generation and the last two or three have lost all practical sense of the primitive condition of humanity, and only the few who have studied history accurately, or have spent much time in the parts of the world occupied by the living representatives of ages long past, can form any mental picture of what society then was. People are not aware how completely, in former ages, the law of superior strength was the rule of life; how publicly and openly it was avowed — I do not say cynically or shamelessly, for those words imply a feeling that there was something in it to be ashamed of, and no such notion could find a place in the mind of anyone in those ages except a philosopher or a saint. History gives a cruel education in human nature by showing how exactly the regard owed to the life, the possessions and the whole earthly happiness of any class of people was measured by what they had the power to enforce; how all who made any resistance to authorities that had weapons in their hands, however dreadful the provocation, had not only the law of force but every other law and every notion of social obligation against them, and in the eyes of those they resisted were guilty not merely of a crime but of the worst of all crimes, deserving the cruellest punishment human beings could inflict. The first small trace of a feeling of obligation in a superior to acknowledge any right in his inferiors appeared when he had been induced, for convenience, to make them some promise. Though such promises, even when sanctioned by the most solemn oaths, were for many ages revoked or violated on the most trifling provocation or temptation, it is probable that this was seldom done, except by people of worse than average morality, without some twinges of conscience.

The ancient republics, being mostly founded from the first on some kind of mutual compact, or at any rate formed by a union of people not very unequal in strength, gave the first instance of a portion of human relations fenced round and placed under a law other than that of force. And though the original law of force remained in full operation between them and their slaves, and also — except so far as express agreement limited it — between a commonwealth and its subjects or other independent commonwealths, the banishment of that primitive law even from so narrow a field began the regeneration of human nature. It gave birth to sentiments whose immense value even for material interests experience soon demonstrated, and which from then on only needed enlarging, not creating. Though slaves were no part of the commonwealth, it was in the free states that slaves were first felt to have rights as human beings. The Stoics were, I believe, the first, apart from the Jewish law, to teach as part of morality that men were bound by moral obligations to their slaves. After Christianity became dominant, nobody could ever again be a stranger to that belief in theory; nor, after the rise of the Catholic Church, was it ever without people to stand up for it. Yet enforcing it was the most arduous task Christianity ever had to perform. For more than a thousand years the Church kept up the contest with hardly any perceptible success. It was not for want of power over men's minds. Its power was prodigious. It could make kings and nobles hand over their most valued possessions to enrich the Church. It could make thousands, in the prime of life and at the height of worldly advantage, shut themselves up in convents to work out their salvation by poverty, fasting and prayer. It could send hundreds of thousands across land and sea, across Europe and Asia, to give their lives for the deliverance of the Holy Sepulchre. It could make kings give up wives they were passionately attached to, because the Church declared they were within the seventh degree of relationship — the fourteenth, by our way of counting. All this it did; but it could not make men fight one another less, or tyrannise less cruelly over the serfs and, when they could, over the townsmen. It could not make them renounce either use of force, force militant or force triumphant. They could never be induced to give that up until they were in their turn compelled by superior force. Only the growing power of kings put an end to fighting except between kings, or claimants to a throne; only the growth of a wealthy and warlike middle class in the fortified towns, and of a common infantry that proved more powerful in the field than the undisciplined chivalry, brought the insolent tyranny of the nobles over townsmen and peasantry within any bounds. And it was persisted in not only until, but long after, the oppressed had gained power enough to take conspicuous revenge; on the Continent much of it lasted until the French Revolution, though in England the earlier and better organisation of the democratic classes ended it sooner by establishing equal laws and free national institutions.

If people are mostly so little aware how completely, for the greater part of our species' existence, the law of force was the avowed rule of general conduct — any other rule being only a special and exceptional consequence of particular ties — and how very recent it is that the affairs of society in general have even been pretended to be regulated by any moral law, they are just as little inclined to remember or consider how institutions and customs that never had any ground but the law of force last on into ages and states of general opinion that would never have permitted them to be set up in the first place. Less than forty years ago, Englishmen might still legally hold human beings in bondage as saleable property; within the present century they might kidnap them and carry them off and work them literally to death. This absolutely extreme case of the law of force, condemned by people who can tolerate almost every other form of arbitrary power, and presenting features more revolting than any other to the feelings of everyone who looks at it from an impartial position, was the law of civilised and Christian England within the memory of people now living. And in one half of Anglo-Saxon America, three or four years ago, not only did slavery exist, but the slave trade, and the breeding of slaves expressly for it, was a general practice between slave states. Yet not only was there a greater strength of feeling against it than against any other of the customary abuses of force, but in England at least there was less feeling and less interest in favour of it than of any other — for its motive was the love of gain, unmixed and undisguised, and those who profited by it were a very small fraction of the country, while the natural feeling of everyone not personally interested in it was unmitigated abhorrence.

So extreme an instance makes it almost unnecessary to mention any other; but consider how long absolute monarchy lasted. In England at present it is almost universally agreed that military despotism is a case of the law of force with no other origin or justification. Yet in all the great nations of Europe except England it either still exists or has only just ceased to exist, and even now has a strong party in its favour in every rank of the people, especially among people of station and consequence. Such is the power of an established system, even one that is far from universal — even when in almost every period of history there have been great and well-known examples of the opposite system, and those examples have almost always been furnished by the most illustrious and most prosperous communities. In this case, too, the possessor of the undue power, the person directly interested in it, is only one person, while those who are subject to it and suffer from it are literally everybody else. The yoke is naturally and necessarily humiliating to everyone except the one man on the throne and, at most, the one who expects to succeed him.

How different are these cases from the power of men over women! I am not prejudging the question whether that power is justifiable. I am showing how much more permanent it was bound to be, even if it were not justifiable, than these other dominations that have nevertheless lasted into our own time. Whatever gratification of pride there is in possessing power, and whatever personal interest there is in exercising it, is in this case not confined to a limited class but shared by the whole male sex. Instead of being, for most of its supporters, something desirable chiefly in the abstract — or, like the political ends usually contended for by factions, of little private importance to anyone but the leaders — it comes home to the person and the hearth of every male head of a family, and of everyone who expects to be one. The farm labourer exercises his share of the power, or expects to, just as fully as the greatest nobleman. And it is the case in which the desire for power is strongest: for everyone who wants power wants it most over those nearest to him, the people his life is passed with, the people he has most concerns in common with, and the people whose independence of his authority is likeliest to interfere with his own preferences.

If, in the other cases I have specified, powers plainly grounded on nothing but force, and having so much less to support them, are so slowly and with such difficulty got rid of, this one must be far more so, even if it rested on no better foundation than they did. We should consider, too, that in this case the possessors of the power have greater facilities than in any other for preventing any rising against it. Every one of the subjects lives under the very eye, and almost, one might say, in the hands, of one of the masters — in closer intimacy with him than with any of her fellow subjects, with no means of combining against him, no power of even locally overmastering him, and, on the other side, with the strongest motives for seeking his favour and avoiding giving him offence. In struggles for political emancipation, everybody knows how often the champions are bought off by bribes or frightened off by threats. In the case of women, each individual of the subject class is in a chronic state of bribery and intimidation combined. In raising a standard of resistance, a large number of the leaders, and still more of the followers, must make an almost complete sacrifice of the pleasures or the small comforts of their own lot. If ever a system of privilege and enforced subjection had its yoke tightly riveted on the necks of those it keeps down, this one has. I have not yet shown that it is a wrong system; but anyone capable of thinking about the subject must see that even if it is, it was certain to outlast every other form of unjust authority. And when some of the grossest of those other forms still exist in many civilised countries, and have only recently been got rid of in others, it would be strange if the most deeply rooted of them all had been perceptibly shaken anywhere yet. There is more reason to wonder that the protests and testimonies against it have been as numerous and as weighty as they are.

Some will object that a comparison cannot fairly be made between the government of the male sex and the forms of unjust power I have brought in to illustrate it, since those are arbitrary and the product of mere usurpation, whereas this one is natural. But was there ever a domination that did not appear natural to those who possessed it? There was a time when the division of mankind into two classes, a small one of masters and a numerous one of slaves, appeared even to the most cultivated minds to be a natural condition of the human race, and the only natural one. No less an intellect, and one that contributed no less to the progress of human thought, than Aristotle held this opinion without doubt or misgiving; and he rested it on exactly the same premises on which the same assertion about the dominion of men over women is usually based — that there are different natures among mankind, free natures and slave natures; that the Greeks were of a free nature and the barbarian races of Thracians and Asiatics of a slave nature. But why need I go back to Aristotle? Did not the slave owners of the southern United States maintain the same doctrine, with all the fanaticism with which men cling to theories that justify their passions and legitimise their personal interests? Did they not call heaven and earth to witness that the dominion of the white man over the black is natural, that the black race is by nature incapable of freedom and marked out for slavery? Some of them went so far as to say that the freedom of manual labourers is an unnatural order of things anywhere. Again, the theorists of absolute monarchy have always maintained that it is the only natural form of government, issuing from the patriarchal, which was the primitive and spontaneous form of society, modelled on the paternal, which is older than society itself and, they argue, the most natural authority of all. Indeed, for that matter, the law of force itself, to those who could plead no other, has always seemed the most natural of all grounds for exercising authority. Conquering races hold it to be Nature's own dictate that the conquered should obey the conquerors — or, as they put it more agreeably, that the feebler and less warlike races should submit to the braver and manlier. The slightest acquaintance with human life in the Middle Ages shows how supremely natural the dominion of the feudal nobility over men of low condition seemed to the nobility themselves, and how unnatural the notion seemed of a person of the inferior class claiming equality with them, or exercising authority over them. It hardly seemed less unnatural to the class held in subjection. The emancipated serfs and townsmen, even in their most vigorous struggles, never claimed a share of authority; they only demanded some limit on the power of tyrannising over them.

So true is it that "unnatural" generally means only "uncustomary", and that whatever is usual appears natural. The subjection of women to men being a universal custom, any departure from it naturally appears unnatural. But how entirely the feeling depends on custom, even in this case, is shown by ample experience. Nothing astonishes people in distant parts of the world more, when they first learn anything about England, than being told that it is under a queen: the thing seems to them so unnatural as to be almost incredible. To Englishmen it does not seem in the least unnatural, because they are used to it — but they do feel it unnatural that women should be soldiers or members of parliament. In the feudal ages, on the contrary, war and politics were not thought unnatural for women, because they were not unusual; it seemed natural that women of the privileged classes should be of manly character, inferior in nothing but bodily strength to their husbands and fathers. The independence of women seemed rather less unnatural to the Greeks than to other ancient peoples, because of the fabulous Amazons, whom they believed to be historical, and the partial example of the Spartan women — who, though no less subordinate by law than women in the other Greek states, were more free in fact, and, being trained in bodily exercises in the same way as men, gave ample proof that they were not naturally disqualified for them. There can be little doubt that Spartan experience suggested to Plato, among many of his other doctrines, that of the social and political equality of the two sexes.

But, it will be said, the rule of men over women differs from all these others in not being a rule of force: it is accepted voluntarily; women make no complaint, and are consenting parties to it. In the first place, a great many women do not accept it. Ever since there have been women able to make their views known by their writing — the only form of publicity society permits them — an increasing number have recorded protests against their present social condition; and recently many thousands of them, led by the most eminent women known to the public, have petitioned Parliament to be admitted to the parliamentary suffrage. The claim of women to be educated as thoroughly, and in the same branches of knowledge, as men is urged with growing intensity and with a good prospect of success, while the demand for their admission to professions and occupations so far closed to them becomes more urgent every year. Though we have not in this country, as they have in the United States, periodic conventions and an organised party agitating for the Rights of Women, there is a numerous and active society organised and managed by women for the more limited object of obtaining the political franchise. Nor is it only in our own country and in America that women are beginning to protest, more or less collectively, against the legal disabilities they labour under. France, Italy, Switzerland and Russia now furnish examples of the same thing. How many more women silently cherish similar aspirations nobody can possibly know; but there are abundant signs how many *would* cherish them if they were not so strenuously taught to repress them as contrary to the proprieties of their sex.

It must be remembered, too, that no enslaved class ever asked for complete liberty all at once. When Simon de Montfort called the deputies of the commons to sit in Parliament for the first time, did any of them dream of demanding that an assembly elected by their constituents should make and unmake ministries and dictate to the king in affairs of state? No such thought entered the imagination of the most ambitious of them. The nobility already had those pretensions; the commons pretended to nothing but exemption from arbitrary taxation and from the gross individual oppression of the king's officers. It is a political law of nature that those under any power of ancient origin never begin by complaining of the power itself, but only of its oppressive exercise. There is never any shortage of women who complain of ill usage by their husbands. There would be infinitely more of them if complaint were not the greatest of all provocations to a repetition and increase of the ill usage. It is this that frustrates every attempt to maintain the power while protecting the woman against its abuses. In no other case, except that of a child, is a person who has been judicially proved to have suffered an injury placed back under the physical power of the culprit who inflicted it. Accordingly wives, even in the most extreme and prolonged cases of bodily ill usage, hardly ever dare make use of the laws made for their protection; and if, in a moment of irrepressible indignation, or through the interference of neighbours, they are induced to do so, their whole effort afterwards is to disclose as little as they can and to beg their tyrant off the punishment he has earned.
