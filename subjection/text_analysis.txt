THE SUBJECTION OF WOMEN (written 1861, published 1869)
======================================================
TRANSLATION STRATEGY

READ mill/text_analysis.txt FIRST. This is the same author, the same
decade, the same source pipeline and very largely the same problem, and
the rules established there carry over intact. What follows is what is
DIFFERENT.

THE JUSTIFICATION
-----------------
On Liberty's numbers: archaism 0.6 per 1,000 words, calque 34.6, 44% of
sentences over 35 words. This book sits in the same place — nothing in
it sounds old, and the block is Victorian periodic architecture plus a
vocabulary of terms of art used with total consistency. It is the hume/
class 3 again, and the same governing rule applies:

  IF A SENTENCE IS ALREADY CLEAR, LEAVE IT ALONE.

Mill's famous sentences here are already perfect and several
must_contain pins are pinned UNCHANGED for that reason. The obstruction
is the apparatus around the argument, not the argument.

BUT THERE IS A SECOND JUSTIFICATION, AND IT IS THE STRONGER ONE. This
book is far less read than On Liberty and is the more startling of the
two. Written in 1861, held back eight years because Mill judged the
public unready, published in 1869 — fifty years before British women
could vote. It says, in 1869, that the nature of women is unknown
because it has never been observed under conditions that would let it
show; that marriage as the law then stood was literal bondage; and that
the resistance is not really about capacity at all but about men not
being able to bear living with an equal. A reader who meets it in
Victorian prose files it as a period document. That is the misreading
the retelling exists to prevent.

FALSE FRIENDS — the mill/ list applies, plus these
--------------------------------------------------
    disability      = a legal incapacity imposed by law, NOT impairment.
                      This is the book's most dangerous false friend and
                      it is everywhere: "disabilities" are the legal bars
                      on women holding office, owning property, voting.
    condition       = social station
    education       = upbringing and formation, not schooling alone
    sentiment       = settled feeling
    animal          = physical, bodily (as in "animal strength")
    incapacity      = a legal bar, or an inability; watch which
    interested      = self-interested, having a stake
    generality of   = most of
    acquirements    = accomplishments, things learned
    equal           = an equal in standing, not merely a similar person

VOCABULARY THAT MUST NOT BE SOFTENED
------------------------------------
Mill chose the hardest available words on purpose and the argument
depends on them. KEEP: bondage, bondservant, slave, master, despotism,
subjection, subordination. He is not reaching for a metaphor; Chapter
Two is a point-by-point comparison of the legal position of a wife with
the legal position of a slave, and he is careful — twice — to say wives
are not in general TREATED as slaves are, which is exactly what lets him
insist that legally they are worse placed. Keep both halves. Dropping
the qualification makes him cruder than he is; dropping the claim makes
him tamer than he is.

"Odalisque" (Chapter One's last word) STAYS. It is the enslaved woman of
an Ottoman harem, and Mill is being deliberately savage about what an
English girl's education is FOR. Gloss it inside the sentence if it can
be done without deadening it; do not replace it.

SENSITIVE CONTENT — THE VERNE RULE, AND WHERE IT BITES
-------------------------------------------------------
The unusual thing about this book is that the Verne rule mostly protects
Mill's RADICALISM rather than his prejudice. Keep unhedged:
  - the sustained slavery comparison, including "there remain no legal
    slaves, except the mistress of every house";
  - the passage on a husband's legal right to his wife's body, which
    Mill states plainly and which is one of the earliest arguments in
    English for what would now be called marital rape being a wrong;
  - his account of what men actually want, which is not service but
    willing service — "not a forced slave but a willing one";
  - the closing argument that the whole arrangement dries up the
    principal fountain of human happiness.
And keep unhedged the places where he is a Victorian: the confidence
about "savages" and "barbarians" and the stages of civilisation, the
assumption that most wives will in fact choose the household as their
career, and the moments where the argument is pitched at what men stand
to gain. These are HIS claims and part of his subject. No notes.

A DATED WORD IS NOT A DATED CLAIM (the mill/ ruling): "Hindoo" ->
"Hindu", "Mahomedan" -> "Muslim", "negro" where it is doing no work in
the argument -> named by the relationship, as On Liberty's "planters and
the people they enslaved". Where Mill is describing American slavery as
an institution he is arguing ABOUT, the institution is named plainly.

EMPHASIS
--------
25 spans, far fewer than On Liberty's 52, and they mark logical stress.
Checked for parity per file and checked that each one RENDERS: an
opening asterisk may not follow a word character, so "can*not*" is not
emphasis at all and ships as literal asterisks. Do not invent spans.

THE FOOTNOTES
-------------
Three, all Mill's own, already inlined by prep.py as "Footnote: ..."
paragraphs. Translate them in his voice. Note 2 (the Hindu principality)
and note 3 (the quotation on "the same right turn of mind") are real
argumentative content, not citations.

REGISTER
--------
Angrier than On Liberty and much more personal. He is writing after his
wife's death, about a subject the two of them worked on together, and
the prose keeps breaking out of the courteous manner into something
harder — the passage on the brutal husband, the passage on what "the
generality of the male sex" cannot tolerate. Let it break out. The
courtesy is real too and must not be flattened into modern bluntness:
Mill concedes early, concedes often, and then does not move.
