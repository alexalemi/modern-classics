Chapter One: The Boy in the Capital

Dogen was born in the capital, Kyoto, on the second day of the first
month of the year 1200, the second year of the Shoji era, in the reign
of Emperor Tsuchimikado. He came of the Minamoto, the noble house that
descended from Emperor Murakami. On his father's side he was nine
generations down from that emperor, and on another line eight
generations down from the prince and poet known as the Later Chushoo.

While his mother was carrying him, a voice was heard in the air. It
said: this child has had no equal in five hundred years. He will be a
great sage, and he is born now to raise up the true teaching in Japan.

A fortune-teller who read faces was brought to look at the baby. He
said: this is no ordinary child. He will certainly be a sage. The seven
places of his body are full and even, his frame and his features are
remarkable, and there are double pupils in his eyes.

The emperor of the day was Tsuchimikado, the first son of the retired
emperor Go-Toba. The emperor's own mother was a daughter of Lord
Michichika, Minister of the Interior, head of the Koga branch of the
Minamoto, and it is said that a daughter of the same Lord Michichika
was married to the shogun at Kamakura. The Founder's family had many
branches at court and in the east, and from that you may judge how
great a house it was.

In 1203, at four years old, he read the hundred poems of the Chinese
poet Li Qiao. A famous old scholar saw him do it and praised him. This
is not the ordinary run of children, he said. In time he will be a
great vessel. He may properly be called a prodigy.

In 1206, at seven, he read the Book of Songs and the Zuo Commentary,
two of the oldest and hardest books in the Chinese classics.

In 1207, in the winter of his eighth year, his mother died. At her
funeral he sat and watched the smoke go up from the incense, and there,
quietly, he understood that nothing in the world lasts. A deep wish to
find the truth rose up in him.

In 1208, at nine, he read the Abhidharmakosa, the great encyclopedia of
Buddhist philosophy by the Indian master Vasubandhu. People said his
mind was as sharp as the bodhisattva of wisdom's. When he studied he
copied the scholars of old who kept themselves awake by pricking their
thighs with an awl, and he drove himself day and night beyond anything
usual.

There was at that time a nobleman called Lord Matsudono, who had held
the highest offices in the land, regent and chancellor both. He was a
scholar without an equal, a clear mirror of past and present, as
people said, and a teacher to kings and ministers. This man decided to
adopt the Founder as his son. He meant to teach him the art of
government, hand the great affairs of his house over to him, and make
him one of the chief ministers of the court. He was already planning
the boy's coming-of-age ceremony.

But the Founder said to himself: this is not what I want. Somehow or
other I must leave the world and study the way.

So in the spring of 1212, the second year of Kenryaku, when he was
thirteen, he slipped away in the middle of the night. He went first to
the family's mountain villa at Kohata, and from there to the foot of
Mount Hiei, the great monastery-mountain north-east of the capital,
where he presented himself at the rooms of a monk named Ryokan Hogen.
Ryokan was his mother's brother.

He asked his uncle to let him become a monk. Ryokan was astonished.
Your coming-of-age is almost upon you, he said, and your family will
be furious. What about that?

The boy answered:

	When my mother died she gave me this charge: that I should find
	a way to leave the world and study the truth, and pray for her
	in the world to come. And I owe my grandmother and my aunts a
	very heavy debt for bringing me up. I mean to become a monk and
	pray for their salvation.

Ryokan wept, and gave his consent on the spot. He took the boy in, and
sent him up the mountain to the Senko hermitage in Hannya valley, in
the Shuryogon-in at Yokawa.
