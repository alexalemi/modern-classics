Chapter Seven: Rujing

The Founder went first to find Myozen, and found him gravely ill,
lying in the Liaoran quarters. When Myozen saw him come in he wept,
over and over, out of gladness and grief together.

Then he asked: when you were with our late master Eisai, did you
settle the question of whether there is any delusion in the
dharma-body, the true body of the Buddha?

The Founder said: I would not presume to say.

Myozen said: try me. Tell it.

The Founder said: once, when the master was sitting in his room, I
came forward and asked him the question that had driven me from Mount
Hiei. If we are all, from the very beginning, the Buddha-nature just as
we are, why is it that the buddhas of the three ages had to make up
their minds to seek awakening, and go looking for it? And the master
said: the buddhas of the three ages do not know that they have it. Cats
and white oxen know. At that I understood all at once, and the sweat
ran off my whole body.

Myozen quietly approved this, and wrote out a paper to certify it.

His illness grew steadily worse, and at about eight in the morning on
the seventh day of the fifth month of 1225 he died in the Liaoran
quarters. He was forty-two.

The Founder could not bear his grief. He had the body cremated,
gathered up the relics, and made the offerings for the dead.

Then he wrote to Rujing. The letter survives:

	That I have been able to take a place at your dharma seat is
	surely the happy fortune of merit laid up in former lives. You
	are all kindness and all compassion. What this small man from a
	far country asks is this: that without regard to the hour, and
	without full formal dress, I may come up to the abbot's quarters
	again and again, to bow and to ask about what puzzles me.
	Impermanence is swift. The matter of birth and death is great.
	Time waits for no man, and to leave the presence of a sage is
	certainly to regret it. Great master of this hall, my own
	teacher, in your great kindness and compassion, take pity on me
	and give Dogen leave to ask about the dharma. I bow to this and
	hope for your consent. Your junior, Dogen, making a hundred
	prostrations and knocking his head on the floor, writes up to
	you.

Rujing read it, and was moved by the earnestness of it, and wrote back:

	Yuan, my son: from this day on, without regard to day or night
	or the hour, come to my quarters in your everyday robe and ask
	about the way. There is no objection. This old monk will overlook
	your want of ceremony exactly as a father overlooks his own
	child's.

On the second day of the seventh month the Founder went up to the
abbot's quarters and asked his first question. Teachers everywhere, he
said, speak of Zen as "a transmission outside the teachings," and take
that to be the whole point of the first ancestor's coming from India.
What of it?

Rujing said: how should the great way of the buddhas and ancestors
have anything to do with inside the teachings or outside them? They
call it a transmission outside the teachings for a reason. Ever since
the first missionaries brought the sutras and the commentaries over
from India in the Yongping years of the Han, China had only masters of
sutras and masters of commentaries, and no line of true heirs. Only
Bodhidharma, sitting alone at Shaolin, brought the thing itself.

It was Rujing, too, who told him how their own line had nearly died,
two hundred years before. The Caodong school, which in Japan would be
called Soto, had come down to an old master named Dayang Jingxuan, and
Dayang had found nobody fit to be his heir. There was one boy, Yiqing,
who had the gift, but he was far too young to be given the seal. So
Dayang did an extraordinary thing. He entrusted the whole line to a
master of a different school, Fushan, with instructions to hold it
until the boy was grown. His letter survives, and Rujing knew it by
heart:

	There is no lack of men to weed and plough, but what is to be
	done when there is no seed? After the grass has grown thick the
	confusion begins, and so, as I come to the end, my thoughts run
	on ahead of me. You are a monk of long standing and a descendant
	of this line. What I now entrust to you alone is the young hawk I
	have lately taken in. He is only a fledgling, but has he not
	merit to spare? He is the fortunate son of my dharma. If I gave
	him the seal now he might find it hard to bear, and so I entrust
	it to you instead. Take it. With this verse, and the leather
	sandals and the one robe and the eighteen articles, I hand my
	word over to you; and hereafter Yiqing is certainly to receive
	it. Keep it in charge, and wait for Yiqing.

	Grass on the ridge at Yangguang:
	I leave it to you to wait until its price is known.
	Where the strange shoot grows thick,
	the sacred root holds fast in the dark.

	He who gets the dharma must be ten years with the Linji house
	before he may open it out.

Would the rest of them, Rujing said, all have died before Dayang did?
It was because Dayang knew how to plan for his grandsons, and his eye
was clear, that he set the boy down as his heir although he was so
young, and had the proof of it kept in another house, and so the
family business was carried on. And he added: the men who wrote the
later histories of the monasteries set down pilfered and unreliable
things, and get the old worthies wrong oftener than not.

Later, the paper Rujing gave the Founder that day was copied out
reverently from his own scroll by the old man Yunxi, six generations
down the line. What the Founder of Eiheiji received face to face in
the abbot's room at Tiantong was handed on in the direct line and set
down at last in writing. It is the pure secret of the Soto abbot's
room, and a thing the other schools do not know.

One day, in the monks' hall, old Rujing was rebuking a monk who had
fallen asleep at his sitting. In zazen, he said, body and mind must
drop away. What is the good of just sitting there sleeping?

The Founder was sitting in the next place. At those words he was
suddenly and completely awakened.

He went straight up to the abbot's room and burned incense.

Rujing asked: what is the incense for?

The Founder said: body and mind have dropped away, and I have come.

Rujing said: body and mind dropped away. Dropped away body and mind.

The Founder said: this is only a passing knack. Do not set your seal
on me carelessly.

Rujing said: I am not setting my seal on you carelessly.

The Founder said: and what is it that is not careless sealing?

Rujing said: dropped away body and mind.

The Founder bowed.

Guangping, the attendant from Fuzhou, was standing beside them, and
said: that a man from another country should get this far is truly no
small matter.

The Founder withdrew with respect.

In the winter of 1227, the third year of Baoqing, he took his leave of
Tiantong to go home. That same night, at midnight, he was called into
the abbot's room, and Rujing gave him the robe that had belonged to
the old ancestor Furong Daokai, the text of the Precious Mirror
Samadhi, the secret teaching of the Five Ranks, and a portrait of
himself with his own inscription on it. And he said:

	I give these to you, a man from a barbarous country, as a token
	of trust. Go back to your own country and spread the teaching
	there, and do good widely to gods and men. Do not live in cities
	and towns. Do not go near kings and ministers. Live in the deep
	mountains and the quiet valleys, and take in one man, or half a
	man, and do not let my school be cut off.
