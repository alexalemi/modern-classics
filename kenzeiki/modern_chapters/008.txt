Chapter Eight: The Road Home

He came down from Mount Taibai and went west into Jiangxi. At
nightfall on the road a tiger came at him. He threw his staff at it and
the tiger took fright and ran off. There is a saying to this day that a
tiger fears a staff.

Further along the same road he fell ill. His companion on the journey
was the monk who later became the master of Doshoan in Kyoto. As the
Founder lay in the grass, an old man came along the road, leaning on a
stick, and gave him a pill of medicine, and he recovered. That old man
was the god Inari of Japan, who had come to look after him.

On the evening before he sailed he got hold of a copy of the Blue
Cliff Record, the great collection of Zen cases with the commentary of
the master Foguo. He opened it out and began to copy it by hand, and
grieved in his heart that he would never get it all down before the
ship left. Then a figure in white robes came in, took up a brush, and
wrote alongside him until it was finished. This was the god of Hakusan,
the White Mountain of Echizen. The manuscript is still kept at Daijoji
in Kaga, where it is called the One-Night Blue Cliff.

That winter he sailed from China. On the voyage the ship ran into a
black wind, and he was up on deck. He sat down in silence, and all at
once the bodhisattva Kannon appeared on the surface of the sea, riding
on a lotus leaf, and the wind and the waves grew quiet.

So it was that in 1227, the first year of Antei by Japanese reckoning,
he came ashore at Kawajiri in the province of Higo. The picture called
Kannon on a Single Leaf, which is still painted, is a picture of that
appearance.

He went back to Kenninji in the capital. There, in the Karoku years,
he wrote out his Universal Recommendation for Zazen, the short set of
instructions for sitting which is still the first thing a Soto monk
learns. He says so himself in his Talk on Pursuing the Way: you should
practise, he writes, according to the Recommendation I compiled in the
Karoku years.

In the Kangi years, from 1230, he was living quietly at Fukakusa,
south of the capital. It was there that he wrote the Talk on Pursuing
the Way, in the autumn of 1231, and it was there that he made the six
poems on living in retirement, one of which everybody remembers:

	One thing only stays with me on waking:
	the sound of night rain over the quiet lodging at Fukakusa.
