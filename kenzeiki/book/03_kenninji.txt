Chapter Three: Kenninji, and Myozen

In 1215, when the Founder was sixteen, Eisai died, on the fifth day of
the seventh month, at the age of seventy-five. The Founder at once
attached himself to Eisai's heir, Myozen, who was also called
Butsuju-bo.

Eiheiji still has a portrait of Eisai and a portrait of Myozen. The
Founder himself wrote the inscription on Myozen's portrait:

	All his life he practised the way, and went straight through to
	what is close at hand.
	Since he was consumed, the face of things has been new.
	Tell me: how is it today?
	After the diamond flame, the true body shows itself.

	His junior, Dogen, bows and inscribes this.

In Dogen's collected teachings there is a discourse he gave on the
anniversary of Myozen's death, and from it you can see that Myozen was
his own master, and that Myozen was the man he went to China with.

On the twelfth day of the ninth month of 1221, the third year of Jokyu,
at Kenninji, he received from Myozen the certificate that a master
gives an heir. Gikai, who was later the third abbot of Eiheiji, used to
say that he had this from Ejo, the second abbot, who had heard it told.

In 1222, the first year of Joo, the Founder was twenty-three. By then he
had already read the whole of the Buddhist canon twice through. In the
same year Myozen gave him the great precepts that the buddhas and
patriarchs hand down directly, one to the next. The discourse on the
anniversary of Myozen's death is really a thanks for that gift of the
precepts. You can see it in the words.

In 1223, when the Founder was twenty-four, Myozen made up his mind to
go to China. But there was a difficulty. Myozen's own teacher, Myoyu,
was dying, and begged him to stay. Years later the Founder told the
story himself, and it was written down. Myozen called his disciples
together and put the case to them.

	That I should think of going to China at all, he said, I owe
	entirely to this teacher's kindness. But he has fallen gravely
	ill and is not likely to last long, and he presses me hard to
	stay. It is not easy to go against a teacher's word. Yet I mean
	to go to China now, without regard for my own life, to seek the
	truth; and that too is the bodhisattva's work of compassion for
	all living beings. Is there any reason, or is there not, for
	going against my teacher's word? Let each of you say what you
	think.

	The disciples all said: give up this year's crossing. Your
	teacher's age and his illness have both reached their limit. Go
	next year instead. Then you will not have disobeyed him, and you
	will have kept faith with what you owe him. What harm can a
	year's delay do?

	Then I, who was the most junior of them all, said: if it seems
	to you that your understanding of the truth will be just the
	same whether you go now or later, then you ought to stay.

	Myozen said: just so. If the practice of the buddha-dharma is
	the same at the end as at the beginning, then I should expect to
	get free and reach the way by staying here. I said: if that is
	how it is with the rest of you, then he should stay.

	When they had all given their opinions, Myozen said: no. These
	two things cannot be reconciled. For this crossing to China I
	have made up my mind, once and for all.

And in the end he went. That this was truly the mind of the way, and
the reason of the thing, is plain.
