	The merit of it is great and deep, and it is handed on from
	country to country and given out to one person after another.

	Since I came back from Song I have carried the wish to found a
	temple, and the days have been long and the months deep with
	it. It was a thing my robe and bowl should have been laid down
	for. Now I have got one excellent site, out beyond Fukakusa, on
	the old ground of Gokurakuji, and it is called Kannon-dori-in.
	It is rank grass and no monastery yet. Here I mean to raise a
	temple of the first rank.

	The most necessary buildings of a temple are the buddha hall,
	the dharma hall and the monks' hall. Of these the buddha hall
	is here already; the dharma hall is not yet; and the monks'
	hall is the most needful of all. It is this I now mean to
	build. Its form is this: a hall of seven bays, with no
	partitions inside it, long platforms set along the walls, the
	assembly of monks living there together, and the practice of
	the way not interrupted for a moment day or night. In the
	middle the sacred monk is enshrined and the assembly sits round
	him. The custom of doing homage to the three treasures in one
	hall has been kept up a long while. The merit of it is great,
	and the work of the Buddha will spread by it.

	Though one might look for a single donor to finish the whole
	thing, I appeal widely in all ten directions, so that many may
	form a good connection with it. This is the excellent way of
	India and of China, and the manner of the monks' life in the
	age of the true dharma and the age of the semblance dharma.
