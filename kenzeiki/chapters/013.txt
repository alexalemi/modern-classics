Tokiyori pressed him hard to stay, and said again and again that he
wished to look to him as founding teacher of a temple he would build.
The Founder firmly refused, saying that his small temple in Echizen had
a patron of its own. The temple he was being invited to is the present
Kenchoji, and it was Chan master Lanxi Daolong who was invited to found
it instead.

A letter from Lanxi to the Founder:

	The second day. Daolong bows and, with an anxious breath,
	writes up to you. The hot golden wind is blowing everywhere and
	the jade sky is high and cold. Respectfully I think of you,
	sitting at your ease above fame and gain, waking gods and men;
	and I hope your health and your going out and coming in are
	excellent.

	Daolong is a late-born man of Song, mistaken and without
	learning, who hides his clumsiness at the bottom of the
	assembly and does as he is told. For years I was with my
	brothers of the immortal country at Taibai, where we lived
	together with nothing settled between us and made one household
	of it. One day after the meal, Kakumyo-bo brought out and showed
	me your dharma-words and verses. I took them up and read them
	over two or three times, and it was as if I were looking you in
	the face. Roads and rivers may lie between us, but in the
	treasury of great bright light there is no separation at all.

	At the end of spring I took ship and came to Hakata. I hear
	that in these last years you have moved away into the deep
	mountains and the far valleys, and are opening this way to
	those who come after, and do not care to be friends with
	vermilion gates and great houses. One can see in it the manner
	of high antiquity, and it makes a man reach after you and not
	be able to stop. Even if there be some looking at a leopard
	through a tube, some talk of long and short

from those who talk at length, what is that worth setting
	against you? The days go by and go by. I hope you will not tire
	of the mallet and the whisk, so that the manner of Damei may
	not fall to the ground and the line of Caodong stay cool and
	clear for ever. Lately, as it happens, I hear that the capital
	is in an uproar, civil and military both, and I imagine you and
	all the brothers are of one mind about it. I am living in a
	borrowed robe in a small room. I have not yet had the good
	fortune to wait on you. I feel it keenly, for the sake of the
	great dharma. The rest I do not set out.

	Respectfully presented. From a man of Western Shu under the
	Song, lodging at Enkakuji in Hakata at Dazaifu, the monk
	Daolong bows and writes up.

The Founder's reply:

	Dogen, with lowered eyes and an anxious breath, writes back.
	Before the desk of the abbot of Enkakuji, great Chan master.

	At this turn of the season, in the first cold of early winter,
	I trust that your health and spirit are in every way well.

	Twenty years ago I went to Great Song and hung

up my staff at Taibai. In the space of a moment, before I had
	been through the monasteries, I turned round and came back to
	this country — blown, no doubt, by the wind of my own karma.
	Wanting in both practice and understanding, I have kept my
	foolishness and let the days pass. These last years I have
	taken a lane into the steep mountains, shut my door, and
	thought to end what is left of my life there.

	Last winter the two Zen monks Senne and Etatsu, in the course
	of their wandering, respectfully brought me your letter. I
	burned incense and read it with reverence, glad and abashed
	together: it was exactly the warmth reaching a cold valley. I
	meant to come to your temple and thank you in person, but my
	poor wish has not been carried out. Unexpectedly, in the eighth
	month of this year, I was drawn away by a patron and found
	myself suddenly in Kamakura in Sagami. Two thousand miles of
	hills and rivers lie east and west between us, and one day of
	longing for a familiar wind is three autumns long.

	I hear you have already reached the capital. It is the turn of
	the times, and men's good fortune. Over the long, long
	distance, to come across the sea — it is just as it was in the
	far-off years of the Putong era. And I am glad that the wind
	and cloud of Jetavana should fan the line of Caoxi and carry it
	well. Written hastily. I hope for your kind understanding.

	Early winter, the first year of Hoji, the year of the sheep.

An old record in the abbot's quarters at Eiheiji says that while he was
at Kamakura in the first year of Hoji, at Tokiyori's request, he made a
poem on the meaning of "not setting up words":

	On the high rock that not even the waves
	of the rough shore can reach —
	if only the dharma were a thing
	one could write, and make stick there.

At that time everyone in Kamakura, high and low, monk and layman
alike, praised it, saying: the reverend Dogen has not only got hold of
the deepest meaning of the buddha-dharma, his skill in the arts of
poetry goes beyond the world as well. In the end they all wrote it on
their fan-faces and recited it with admiration.

Lord Tameie, hearing this poem, praised it and said: the words are
straight and the meaning clear; it puts the heart aside and yet the
feeling in it is all the deeper. That is what a Japanese poem is
properly for. Who among the accomplished men of our day comes near it?

On the thirteenth day of the third month he came back from Kamakura to
the temple, and on the fourteenth he took the high seat and said:

	This mountain monk went out from the mountain on the third day
	of the eighth month last year and travelled to Kamakura in
	Sagami, to preach the dharma for a lay disciple who is our
	patron. Yesterday, this month, this year, I came back to the
	temple, and this morning I take the seat. Some of you may have
	doubts about this business. Crossing how many mountains and
	rivers to preach the dharma for a lay disciple — it looks like
	putting laymen above monks. And you may wonder whether there
	was some dharma never yet spoken, never yet heard, that I
	preached for him alone. There was not. Those who do good rise
	and those who do evil sink; you cultivate the cause and you
	meet the effect. I threw out a brick to draw out a piece of
	jade, and that is all.

	Even so, in this one matter the old man of Eihei has made
	things clear and said them, believed them and carried them
	out. Do you all want to understand this principle?

	After a long silence he said:

	Putting up with Eihei's tongue-tip talk —
	speak of the cause and the effect has no ground.
	How many mistakes in the labour of ploughing the way!
	Today, pitiably, I have turned into a water buffalo.

	That is the verse for preaching the dharma. And the verse for
	coming home to the mountain? He said:

	This mountain monk has been away more than half a year,
	like a solitary wheel set in the vast sky.
	Today I come home to the mountain, and the clouds are glad;
	my love for the mountain is deeper than it was at first.

— worth two thousand koku, in Rokujo in Echizen, was offered as an
estate for Eiheiji; but the Founder in the end would not take it.

The monk sent with the deed of gift was the head monk Genmyo. He was
delighted at the grant of the fief and went about telling the assembly
of it. When the Founder heard of it, he said that this gladness of his
was filthy, and expelled him from the temple then and there; and it is
handed down that he had the very platform Genmyo had sat zazen on cut
out and taken away. Nothing like it had been known before.

As for that Genmyo: about a hundred and thirty years after the
Founder's death, a wandering monk met him on Mount Hakone in Izu. I am
Genmyo, he said, who was head monk of Eiheiji in Echizen — and he told
the pilgrim stories of the Founder's lifetime, leaning on a bamboo
staff and standing there as he talked. The pilgrim afterwards came to
Eiheiji and told them of it.
