Dōgen brought the Sōtō school of Zen to Japan. He was born in Kyoto in 1200, sailed to China at twenty-four, came home in 1227, built his monastery in the mountains of Echizen in the 1240s, and died in Kyoto in 1253. Two of his own works are in this collection as The Practice Instructions: a manual for sitting still, and ten sections of advice to students. This book is the record of his life, and it is the oldest one that exists.

It was written around 1470 by Kenzei, the thirteenth abbot of Eiheiji, the monastery Dōgen founded. Kenzei says at the start why he wrote it as he did. The facts were already on record, in the temple chronicle and in Dōgen's collected teachings, but those were in Chinese, the learned language. He wrote his in plain Japanese, in the simple syllabary, so that "the children and the unlettered of this last age" could read it.

What makes it unusual is that it is built out of documents. Kenzei copied his evidence into the text and left it there: letters, the permits Dōgen travelled on, a sworn statement by farmers who saw a coloured cloud over the mountain, the kitchen and storehouse rules in Dōgen's own hand, the note his successor wrote when he stored the broken paper screens above a ceiling to prove what had happened, the custody record of a robe through four generations of monks. The miracles are in the record on the same terms as the receipts, and told in the same flat voice: a god with a walking stick who gives him medicine on a road in China, a mountain deity who sits down and helps him finish copying a book before his ship sails. A reader should know in advance that Kenzei does not sort the two kinds of thing, and that this edition has not sorted them either.

The text read here is the illustrated edition printed from woodblocks in 1806, in which the monk Menzan's corrected version of Kenzei was set out with seventy-one pictures. The men who made it explain themselves in a preface: they added the pictures so that people who could not read might see the founder's life too, and they say plainly that they composed the pictures rather than reporting them — "adding flowers where we render spring, bringing in the moon where we draw autumn." So a plate's season, its weather and its furniture are choices of the artist. The captions here describe what is drawn and do not claim it as fact about the event.

Everything Kenzei set down is here, with all the documents. What has been left out is Menzan's scholarly apparatus — his notes on sources and his arguments with other historians. The chapter divisions are new. Dates are given by their Japanese era name with the Western year beside them.
