[Preface]

Kenzei's record, written in the plain syllabary, was made in the first
place for lay believers, and his kindness in it is complete. Beyond
that, the old man of Menzan corrected and supplemented it, and made
good the gaps in the record with the greatest care.

And now we, quietly looking into what he meant by it, have gone further
and dressed it out with pictures, setting them side by side with the
text so that the unlettered may see it, and the sincerity of old times
be shown. The whole intention is that ordinary men and women should
have a way into it by this road — that they may be brought close to
him, and hear the founder's deeds with their own ears. We do not
presume to call it a repayment of kindness.

[[n003r]]
And if anyone should charge us with the offence of peddling talk, we
hope only that it may make a strong connection with them, whether they
take it well or ill.

	The first month of autumn, the third year of Bunka, the year of
	the tiger, on the day the summer retreat is released.
	Respectfully inscribed by a distant descendant, the monk
	Okachinju. [two seals]

[[n003l]]
Editorial principles

One. This undertaking fell, as it happened, in the year of the dog, the
second year of Kyowa, which was the five hundred and fiftieth
anniversary of the founder's death. Accordingly our kind teacher
Chinju, with Daiken, Shin, Jotsu, Zentatsu, Bankei of Echizen, Butsuma
and others, laid the plan beforehand, appealed for subscriptions to the
pure assembly gathered at the memorial feast, collected the offerings,
and put them in the hands of Shunryo of Oshu, setting him to the work
of cutting the blocks. But Ryo unfortunately died, and the work was
held up on that account. We then received our teacher's instructions
again, gathered up the drawings that had been let fall, and

[[n004r]]
made good what had been left out, and put the whole to the blocks
again, so discharging the earlier intention.

One. Kenzei's record is full and exact, to be trusted and to be checked
against; and Menzan's corrections and additions did their utmost to
make it good. Now, cutting these pictures together with the two texts,
we hope for the first time to show the true face of the mountain.

One. The pictures follow the order of the record as it stands. But
things that are set down in Menzan's appendix — the blood-lineage and
the deliverance of the spirit, for instance — belong with the house of
Hatano, who revered the founder and founded Eiheiji, and

[[n004l]]
— so we have worked out the years of these things and set them in
order, and fixed what each rests on. Readers, we ask you to bear this
intention in mind.

One. Things like Ejo's taking the whisk we have for the present left
unillustrated, because a picture of it would be confused with the
founder's own ceremonies. As for the rest, where a picture would add
little we have here and there left it out, for fear of clutter. Do not
charge us with carelessness.

One. Kenzei wrote in the plain syllabary for the sake of lay believers,
and his intention in it was deep and earnest. That we should now be
adding pictures on top of it is no better than the ugly woman of the
village trying to make good her looks by copying another's frown.

[[n005r]]
Still, we hope it may guide the crow-chasing novices and fledgling
monks of our own school in this country, and be of some use besides to
the children and the plain people among believers and patrons far and
near. If each goes from what is near him to what is far, it will come
in the end to a seed planted everywhere.

One. The poem in the picture and the picture in the poem: we wanted
from the first that they should be closely fused. For that reason we
have put a little of our own into the plates — making what was long
there short here, or what was small there large; adding flowers where
we render spring, and bringing in the moon where we draw autumn, and
things of that kind. Why should anyone call this the illustrator's
groping? Because the whole figure, from first to last, is not falsified.

[[n005l]]
One. Somebody has found fault with this undertaking, saying: what an
extreme piece of idle amusement. We cannot think that is right. The
deeds of the sages and worthies of former ages — everything that
writing cannot reach — have always been supplied by pictures. Are the
mandalas of the Diamond and Womb realms, or the paintings of the
nirvana at Kusinagara, to be slandered as idle amusement too? Writing
and painting alike turn back a contrary current; they are provisions
made to meet the occasion. Would those upstream make sport of a
breaking of rules like this one?

One. A small view of the founder's mountain is appended at the end of
the volume because

[[n006r]]
people in far corners of the country who have never been there may
picture to themselves the antiquity of the founder's grave, and may
form here and now the good cause of coming to it. Ah — nearly six
hundred frosts and stars have passed since the founding; and shall the
mountain ever be ground down to a whetstone, or the river shrink to a
sash?

	burning incense and bowing nine times. Hakuryu.

The Kenzeiki, Corrected and Supplemented, with Illustrations. Volume
One.

	Corrected and supplemented by Menzan of Eifukuji.
	Illustrated by Rinno Daiken and others.

The first ancestor of the Soto school in Japan, and founder of Eiheiji
on Mount Kichijo in the Shibi estate in the district of Yoshida in
Echizen, was the reverend Dogen. The events of his life from his birth
onward are plainly set down in the chronicle and in the Extensive
Record and elsewhere; but so that the children and the unlettered of
this last age may read them easily, they have been written out here in
the plain syllabary. It was written at the request of Genchu, the
seventh in descent of the lay patrons of Eiheiji. Recorded by his
dharma-descendant, the monk Kenzei.

The reverend Dogen was born on the second day of the first month of
the second year of Shoji, the year of the monkey, in the reign of
Tsuchimikado, eighty-third of the sovereigns. He was a man of the
capital, a scion of the Minamoto in the ninth generation from Emperor
Murakami, and a descendant in the eighth generation of the Later
Chushoo.

While his mother was carrying him a voice was heard in the air, saying:
this child has had no equal these five hundred years. He will be a
great sage, and he is born now to raise up the true dharma in Japan.

A physiognomist too looked at him and said: this child is unlike
ordinary children. He will certainly be a sage. The seven places of his
body are full and level,

[[n007r]]
his frame and features were remarkable, and there were double pupils in
his eyes: this, they said, is no ordinary child.

[[n010l]]
The emperor of the day was Tsuchimikado, whose personal name was
Tamehito, the first son of the retired emperor Go-Toba; his mother was
Shomeimon-in, daughter of the Minister of the Interior, Lord
Michichika — in fact the daughter of the Dharma-seal Noen. It is also
said that the Lady of the West, wife of Munetaka the Kamakura shogun,
was a daughter of that same Lord Michichika of Tsuchimikado. The
Founder's house had many branches, and later ages may judge from that
how flourishing it was.

In the third year of Kennin, the year of the boar, at four years old,
he read Li Qiao's hundred poems. An old and famous scholar saw it and
praised him, saying: this is not the ordinary run of children. He will
be a great vessel in time to come. He may properly be called a prodigy.

In the first year of Kenei, the year of the tiger, at seven, he read
the Book of Songs and the Zuo Zhuan.

In the first year of Jogen, the year of the hare, in the winter of his
eighth year, his mother died. Meeting his beloved mother's funeral then,
and watching the smoke of the incense, he quietly understood the
impermanence of the world, and there rose in him a deep

[[n011r]]
vow to seek the dharma.

In the second year of Jogen, the year of the dragon, at nine, he read
Vasubandhu's Abhidharmakosa. People of the time said of him that his
faculties were as sharp as Manjushri's. When he was at his studies he
followed the men of old who pricked their own thighs with an awl to
keep themselves awake, and he drove himself day and night beyond what
is usual.

At that time there was Lord Matsudono, who held the high offices of
regent and chancellor. He was a scholar without an equal under heaven,
and it is handed down that he was a clear mirror of past and present
and a teacher to kings and ministers. This man wished to take the
Founder and make him his adopted son, to teach him the essentials of
government and hand on the great affairs of the house, and so to make
him one of the chief ministers of the court, and he was planning to
have him come of age.

But the Founder said to himself: this is not what I want. Somehow or
other I must leave home and study the way.

[[n015r]]
In the second year of Kenryaku, the year of the monkey, in the spring
of his thirteenth year, he slipped away in the middle of the night and
went to the mountain villa at Kohata, and from there made his way to
the foot of Mount Hiei and went into the room of Ryokan Hogen. Ryokan
was the Founder's own uncle on his mother's side.

The Founder asked Ryokan to let him leave home. Ryokan was greatly
startled, and asked him: your coming of age is close at hand, and your
family will certainly be angry about this. What of that?

The Founder said:

[[n015l]]
When my beloved mother died she left me this charge: you must contrive
to leave home and study the way, and pray for me in the world to come.
And the kindness of my grandmother and my aunts in bringing me up is a
very heavy debt. I mean to leave home and pray for their awakening.

Ryokan was moved to tears, and gave his consent at once, and took him
into his room and sent him up to the Senko-bo in Hannya valley, at the
Shuryogon-in on Yokawa.

In the first year of Kenpo, the year of the rooster, at fourteen, on
the ninth day of the fourth month, he had his head shaved for the first
time by the abbot Koen. On the tenth of the same month, at the
ordination platform of Enryakuji, with Koen as his preceptor, he
received the bodhisattva precepts and became a monk. From then on he
learned the whole style of the Tendai school, the secret teaching that
came from southern India, and the doctrines of the great and the lesser
vehicles.

Now all monks ask to have their heads shaved on the eighth day of the
fourth month. That the Founder was shaved on the ninth was because the
whole mountain deliberated over it and held it back, so that the
shaving took place at dawn on the ninth.

[[n016r]]
This abbot Koen was of the Myohoin, at the foot of Yokawa in the
village of Okugi. He was the head of the school in his generation, and
in that day a scholar without equal in the exoteric and esoteric
teachings alike, and a monk of pure conduct who kept the discipline.

In the second year of Kenpo, the year of the dog, the Founder was
fifteen. He had gone right through the sutras and treatises, and a
doubt of his own came up in him. He put it like this: both the
exoteric and the esoteric teachings say that we are from the beginning
the original dharma-nature, the self-nature body just as it is. If that
is so, then on what grounds did the buddhas of the three times ever
raise the aspiration and go looking for awakening?

He put the question to those about him, and nobody had an answer. So he
was sent to consult Koin, the abbot of Miidera, and laid it before him.

[[n018l]]
So he went and put the question to Koin. Koin answered: this is not a
question to be answered lightly. Our school has its doctrine, but I am
afraid the doctrine does not get to the bottom of it. You should go to
Kenninji and study with Eisai. That was his direction.

In the same year he entered the room of Senko Zenji, the founder of
Kenninji, and heard the style of the Linji school for the first time.

[[n019r]]
In the third year of the same era, the year of the boar, when the
Founder was sixteen, Senko Zenji died at seventy-five, on the fifth day
of the seventh month. He attended at once upon Myozen, the heir to the
dharma. Myozen was called Butsuju-bo.

At Eiheiji there is a portrait of Senko Zenji and a portrait of Myozen.
The Founder's inscription on the portrait of Myozen runs:

	All his life he practised the way, and went straight through to
		what is close at hand.
	Since he was consumed, the face of things has been new.
	Tell me: how is it today?
	After the adamantine flame, the true body shows itself.

signed: his junior, Dogen, bows and inscribes this.

In the dharma-words of the Eihei Record there is a discourse given on
the anniversary of the death of his late teacher Butsuju. From that we
may judge that Butsuju was the Founder's own master, and that he went
with him when he went to Song China.

[[n021l]]
In the third year of Jokyu, the year of the snake, on the twelfth day
of the ninth month, at Kenninji, he received from Myozen the
certificate that passes from master to heir. Gikai used to say that he
had this from the second abbot of Eiheiji himself, who had heard it
told.

In the first year of Joo, the year of the horse, the Founder was
twenty-three. By then he had already been through the whole of the
Buddhist canon twice. He also received from Myozen the great precepts
that the buddhas and patriarchs transmit directly.

[[n022r]]
There is a discourse given on the anniversary of Butsuju's death. What
it means is that he was thanking him for the gift of the precepts; you
can see it in the dharma-words.

In the second year of Joo, the year of the sheep, the Founder was
twenty-four. That spring Myozen set about going to Song China.

[[n022l]]
That I should think of going to Song at all is owing entirely to this
teacher's kindness. But he has fallen gravely ill and is not likely to
last long, and he presses me hard to stay. It is not easy to go against
a teacher's word. Yet I mean to go to Song now without regard for my
own life, for the sake of seeking the dharma; and that too is to seek
the bodhisattva's work of great compassion for the good of living
beings. Is there any reason, or is there not, for going to Song against
my teacher's word? Let each of you say what you think.

At this the disciples said: this year's crossing should be given up.
The teacher's age and illness have both come to their limit. If you
cross next year instead, then first of all you will not have gone
against his word, and you will have kept faith with what you owe him;
and what harm can there be in a crossing put off by a year or half a
year?

Then I, who was the most junior of them, said: if it seems to you that
the understanding of the buddha-dharma will be just the same later on,
then you ought to stay.

The late master said: just so. If the practice of the buddha-dharma is
the same at the end as at the beginning, then I should expect to get
free and reach the way by staying. I said: if that is how it is with
each of you, then you should stay. When they had all given their
opinions in this way, he said to them:

[[n023l]]
it cannot be reconciled; and so for this crossing to Song I have made
up my mind once for all. And in the end he did go to Song. That this
was truly the mind of the way, and the reason of the thing, is plain.

The Founder accordingly went in company with the reverend Myozen. They
left Higashiyama on the twenty-second day of the second month, made for
the port of Hakata in Chikuzen on the western sea, and in the last ten
days of the third month boarded a merchant ship and cast off.

The two travel permits for that voyage were kept in the treasury at
Eiheiji until they were burnt on the ninth day of the third month of
the year of the horse, in the Shotoku era. They read:

	Myozen, resident monk of Kenninji, accompanied by two or three
	of his disciples, is going to the port of Hakata in order to
	cross to Tang. On the roads of the Saikaido, at the harbours
	and the barriers and the rest, he is to be passed through
	without hindrance. Issued by command of the retired emperor's
	office. So it stands.

	The twenty-first day of the second month, the second year of
	Joo. Sealed by the lieutenant of the Left Guard.

[[n024r]]
And again:

	The residents of Kenninji — Myozen, Dogen, Kakunen, Ryobi and
	the rest — are going down to the western sea to cross over. Let
	them pass the barriers along the way and put in at the harbours
	without hindrance. So ordered.

	The twenty-first day of the second month, the second year of Joo.
	Sealed, the Governor of Musashi.
	Sealed, the Governor of Sagami.

[[n024l]]
The monk Dogen went to Song at twenty-four. He had in mind the poem
that the Great Master Chisho of Miidera made when he set out for Tang:

	I am one who goes out in the boat of the dharma —
	let all the gods and all the buddhas look on me.

and he was of the same mind.

At Tsu in Ise there was a great merchant named Shibahara Shirozaemon.
An ancestor of his some twenty generations back, when he was trading
overseas by ship, carried the founder of Eihei across to China, and
there is a record of it in that family's genealogy.

At the beginning of the fourth month of the sixteenth year of Jiading
in Great Song — the second year of Joo in Japan, the year of the sheep
— they reached the coast at Mingzhou. While he was still aboard the
ship he first went round looking at the mountains and asking after the
customs of the country.

In the middle of the fifth month, while the Founder was still on the
ship, the cook of Ayuwangshan came and met him, and they talked

[[n026l]]
In the autumn, in the seventh month, he went up Mount Tiantong for the
first time and was given a place in the hall. The abbot at that time
was Wuji, whose name was Liaopai; he had received the dharma from
Fozhao Guang, and was a dharma-grandson of Chan master Dahui Gao.

But because the Founder and his companions were foreigners from a
barbarous country, they were ranked in the row of the newly ordained.
Myozen was forty at the time. The Founder spoke, and said: in the
house of Shakyamuni, precedence goes by the years since ordination —

[[n027r]]
— not by a man's age in the world. How is it that the seating in the
great Chan monasteries of China should be turned upside down like this?

The whole assembly of the mountain heard him, but nothing was done. The
Founder pressed the point and would not let it drop. The senior monks
said: it is handed down that when monks came from Japan under the
former dynasty — Kukai, Saicho, Eisai and the rest — every one of them
was ranked with the newly ordained on entering a Chinese monastery.
That is the old precedent, and it is not to be altered now.

At that the Founder sighed to himself and said: that the standard of a
thousand buddhas should fall to the level of ordinary human feeling is
a thing to grieve over. Shall I not put it right?

And he drew up a memorial and laid his case before the Emperor of Song.

[[n027l]]
He submitted a second memorial. The substance of it ran:

	I say again: the buddha-dharma reaches through worlds as many
	as the sands, and the light of the precepts shines in the ten
	directions. The sutra says, "This threefold world is all mine,
	and the beings in it are all my children." If it is all his,
	then this world of ours is the country of Shakyamuni Buddha.
	The country is already a buddha-country and the people are all
	the children of a buddha. Brothers are brothers, and the natural
	order between them is not to be confused. As I see it, the
	dharma of the buddhas and the law of the world both follow from
	this principle. The gods of heaven and the spirits of earth do
	not tolerate what is against principle. How should the judgment
	of heaven be partial? I respectfully ask that the order of
	precedence by the precepts be set right.

The emperor at that time was Ningzong. He read the memorial himself,
and was moved by it more than once, and in the end he sent down an
edict to Tiantong that the order of seating by the precepts be fixed
according to the dharma. That Japanese monks entering Chinese
monasteries were not put out of their proper standing after that is the
Founder's own doing, and from this his name went out through the Five
Mountains.

That same month the cook of Ayuwang came again, and they met and talked
and he went away. The Founder reported it to Myozen, and Myozen was
delighted.

[[n030l]]
At the beginning of the Jiading era, Ryuzen — a senior monk, and a man
of Japan — nursed the librarian of that monastery through an illness,
and took great trouble over him. To thank him for the trouble, the
librarian brought out his succession document and let him bow to it.
It is a thing hardly ever seen. So he bowed to it.

Eight years after that, in the autumn of the sixteenth year of Jiading,
the year of the sheep, when Dogen first took up lodging on Mount
Tiantong, Ryuzen quietly asked the librarian on his behalf, and had the
succession document shown to Dogen.

[[n031r]]
And through Weiyi of the West Hall he was allowed to bow to a
succession document of the line of Fayan.

[[n031l]]
And through Elder Zongyue he was allowed to bow to a succession
document of the line of Yunmen.

[[n032l]]
and made his thanks with incense. The reverend Wuji said to him then:
this is a matter few men get to see or know. Now, elder brother, you
have come to know it, and that is the true resort of one who studies
the way. At this Dogen's gladness was more than he could contain.

While he was in the monks' hall he saw the monk on the next place put
his kesa on his head every morning before putting it on, and he wept
and made a vow. And when a monk came in from Korea who did not so much
as know what robe and bowl meant, and looked like a layman, he was more
ashamed than ever, and took himself in hand.

[[n034r]]
In the first year of Baoqing, the year of the rooster, the Founder was
lodging at Tiantong and coming up to twenty-six. Wuji had approved him
several times over, but he would not accept it of himself.

He left Tiantong and went to see Zheweng Ruyan at Jingshan.

Ruyan asked: when did you get here?
The Founder said: the fourth month of last year.
Ruyan said: you came along with the crowd, just so.
The Founder said: since I came along with the crowd, just so — then
what is it that does not come along with the crowd?
Ruyan said: sit down a while and drink your tea.

He went on to Cuiyan in Taizhou, met Zhuo, and asked at once: what is
the Buddha?
Zhuo said: the one inside the hall.
The Founder said: if he is the one inside the hall, how is it that he
fills the sands of the Ganges?
Zhuo said: he fills the sands of the Ganges.
The Founder said: you have fallen into your own words.

He travelled to Ayuwang again, and seeing the round moon of Nagarjuna
drawn on a stone plinth there, he put Chenggui the guest-prefect to the
test.

[[n036r]]
He went on to the Wannian monastery at Pingtian, met the abbot Yuanzi,
and was allowed to look at his succession document and bow to it.
Afterwards Yuanzi had a dream about him, and thought a great deal of
him as a vessel for the dharma. There was a second dream as well, and
the Founder was deeply moved by the connection between them.

[[n036l]]
— the transmission document, or not? Dogen said: how might I be allowed
to see it?

The elder then rose himself, held up the document and said: this is a
thing not shown even to a man's own kin, nor to an attendant monk of
many years' standing. That is the rule of the buddhas and ancestors.

But yesterday, when I had come out of the city and was staying in the
prefectural town, I had a dream. There was an eminent monk in it who
seemed to be Chan master Fachang of Damei, and he held up a branch of
plum blossom and said: if there should come a real man who has already
crossed the ship's rail, do not begrudge him the flower. And he gave
the plum blossom to me. Without knowing what I did, I recited in the
dream: thirty good blows for the man who has not yet crossed the ship's
rail. And this document has plum blossom in its damask, besides.

Now, meeting you, elder brother, who have already crossed the ship's
rail — this must be what Damei was teaching. It agrees with the dream.

[[n038l]]
The Founder went round from place to place and met the teachers there,
but because he found no one of surpassing capacity fit to be his
master, he set out to visit Wuji again and go back to Tiantong. On the
road he heard that Wuji had died. He grieved over it greatly, and from
then on the thought of going home began to come up in him. So he went
to call on Myozen, who was lodging at Tiantong, and climbed the
mountain a second time.

On the way there was a monk called Laogui, who said to him: of all the
masters under heaven today there is none beyond Chan master Rujing. He
has lately taken up residence at Tiantong at the emperor's request. You
should go up the mountain at once and see him.

The Founder took this for good fortune, and hurried up to Tiantong.

[[n041l]]
He went to call on Myozen, and found him gravely ill and lying in the
Liaoran quarters. When Myozen saw him come in he wept, over and over,
out of gladness and grief together.

Then he asked the Founder: when you were with our late master, did you
understand whether there is delusion in the dharma-body or not?

The Founder said: I would not presume to say.

Myozen said: try me. Tell it.

The Founder said: once the late master was sitting upright in his room,
and I came forward and asked him — if we are from the beginning the
original dharma-nature, the self-nature body just as it is, why is it
that the buddhas of the three times raise the aspiration and attain the
way? And the master said: the buddhas of the three times do not know
that they have it; cats and white oxen do know. At that I understood
all at once, and the sweat ran off my whole body.

Myozen quietly approved this, and wrote out a piece to certify it.

His illness grew steadily heavier, and at the hour of the dragon on the
seventh day of the fifth month he died in the Liaoran quarters. He was
forty-two.

The Founder could not bear his grief. He made him into a wisp of smoke,
gathered up the relics, and made the offerings for him.

[[n042l]]
That I have been able to take a place at your dharma seat is surely the
happy fortune of merit laid up in former lives. You are of great
kindness and great compassion. What this small man from a far country
asks is this: that without regard to the hour, and without full formal
dress, I may come up to the abbot's quarters again and again to bow and
ask about what puzzles me. Impermanence is swift; the matter of birth
and death is great; time waits for no man; and to leave the sage's
presence is certainly to regret it. Great reverend of the hall, great
Chan master, my own teacher, of great kindness and great compassion:
take pity on me and grant Dogen leave to ask about the dharma. I bow to
this and hope for your kind consent. Your junior, Dogen, making a
hundred prostrations and knocking his head, writes up to you.

Rujing read it, and being moved by the earnestness of it, wrote back:
Yuan, my son, from this day on, without regard to day or night or the
hour, come to the abbot's quarters in your everyday robe and ask about
the way; there is no objection. This old monk will overlook your want
of ceremony exactly as a father overlooks his own child's.

On the second day of the seventh month of that year he went up to the
abbot's quarters and asked: at present teachers everywhere speak of a
transmission outside the teachings, and take that to be the point of
the ancestor's coming from the west. What of it?

Rujing said: how should the great way of the buddhas and ancestors have
anything to do with inside the teachings or outside them? They call it
a transmission outside the teachings because, ever since Kasyapa
Matanga and the rest brought the sutras and treatises over in the
Yongping years of the Han, there were only masters of sutras and
masters of treatises and no lineage of true heirs, and the high
ancestor at Shaolin alone

One day the old man Rujing was rebuking a monk who had fallen asleep
sitting. He said: in zazen body and mind must drop away. What is the
good of just sitting there sleeping?

The Founder, sitting beside him, was suddenly and greatly awakened.

He went straight up to the abbot's room and burned incense.

Rujing asked: what is the incense for?

The Founder said: body and mind have dropped away, and I have come.

Rujing said: body and mind dropped away. Dropped away body and mind.

The Pure Rules of the Zen Garden say: in the manner of serving a meal
to monks, reverence is the essence. Far back, this transmits the way of
India; nearer to hand, it transmits the way of China. In the Tathagata's
own time and after his passing, the gods offered the food of heaven to
the Buddha and to the monks, and kings set their own royal dishes
before the Buddha and the monks, and offerings came besides from the
houses of the great and of laymen, and from the houses of merchants and
of labourers as well. In every one of these offerings the reverence
went into the smallest particular. They used the deepest bow known
among gods or men, they kept to the most respectful forms of speech,
and it was in that spirit of reverence that they prepared the rice and
the dishes and all the vessels of the offering. The observances of the
kitchen office, and the very words used there, are what ought to be
handed on correctly. Those who study the buddha-dharma of gods and men

[[n083l]]
should do the same. The gruel is to be called the honourable gruel, and
the midday meal the honourable meal; you may say the hour of the meal,
but never simply the hour. When you send word about the rice, say that
the rice is to be washed clean and served, and never that it is to be
rinsed. Say that the honourable soup is to be made and served, and
never simply soup. Say that the honourable broth is to be made and
served. Say of the gruel that it has been brought to the boil for them.
When you are serving, never blow with your breath on the rice or the
greens or on anything else. Even if your sleeve is dry, do not let it
touch the food. If your hand has touched your face and you have not yet
washed it, do not lay it on the bowls of gruel or meal, or on the gruel
or meal itself. From the picking over of the rice and greens, through
to the making up of the rice and broth and the serving of them — if at
any point in the work you have scratched an itch anywhere on your body,
you must wash that hand. In the place where the meal and gruel are
made ready and served,

[[n084r]]
recite passages of the sutras and chant the words of the ancestral
teachers; keep off worldly talk and all coarse and filthy speech. Of
the rice, the greens, the salt, the bean paste and everything else,
say that it has been spilt; do not say only that there is rice or
greens. When you pass the place where the meal and gruel are set out,
monks and lay workers alike should make a bow. Odd greens and odd
grains of rice may be used, but only after the meal and the gruel are
done; nothing is to encroach on the meal and the gruel while they are
being served. The utensils used to make ready and serve them are to be
kept for that and guarded in every particular, and used for nothing
else. Anyone coming from a lay household whose hands are not yet
purified must not touch the greens or the sweetmeats. Only after water
has been sprinkled, incense offered and the fire tended may the food be
offered to the Three Treasures and the assembly. At the mountains and
monasteries of Great Song today, buns and milk-cakes and steamed cakes
that come in from lay households are steamed a second time before being
set out for the assembly. Steaming is a purifying. Until they have been
steamed they are not offered.

These instructions are a very few out of many. Take the gist of them
and run the storehouse and the kitchen accordingly. Let nothing be done
out of due form.

The fourth year of Kencho, the year of the rat.

From about the summer of that year he sat with a slight illness. His
last teaching was the fascicle of the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye
on the Eight Awakenings of Great Beings. It takes the Sutra of the Last
Teaching for its ground, and reads as his testament.

	The first is having few wants. What this means is: do not go
	after fame and gain. Where there is no wanting there is no
	grief or care, and all the virtues arise of themselves.

	The second is knowing when you have enough. What this means is:
	you wish to escape the sufferings of the world. The rich and
	the high-born are so by birth, and the poor man is poor by
	birth. Have pity on those poorer than yourself, and do not
	crave what is above you. If a man will only think about his own
	condition, he will find there is nothing he lacks.

	The third is delighting in stillness. This means that if you
	give up the company of people and live deep in the mountains
	and woods, you will be honoured and respected by gods and men
	alike.

[[n104r]]
The fourth is diligent effort. That is: if one who has left
	home keeps up the practice well and does not slacken, then
	everything he seeks will be fulfilled.

	The fifth is not forgetting mindfulness. If you mean to follow
	a good teacher, do not set the thought aside for a single
	moment. Then the afflictions will not come of themselves.

	The sixth is practising meditation. This is gathering the mind
	quietly and sitting zazen. At such a time you come of yourself
	to the truth of the world's impermanence.

	The seventh is practising wisdom. A man who has wisdom does not
	covet anything at all. Look well into this principle, and do
	not lose it. To do as the teaching says is like getting a lamp
	in the dark.

	The eighth is not indulging in idle argument. What this means
	is: do not argue frivolously about anything anywhere. Idle
	argument disturbs the mind. Only think of the fact that you
	must die.

	Whoever does not know the meaning of these Eight Awakenings of
	Great Beings, he said, is not a disciple of the Buddha.

Ejo, the second abbot, wrote: the above is the draft made by my late
master, the founding abbot, during his last illness. He said: all the
fascicles of the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye written before this in
Japanese are to be rewritten, and new ones drafted with them, and the
whole made up to a hundred fascicles.

[[n104l]]
After that his illness grew heavier and heavier by degrees, and
	so this was the teaching he left as his command. It is our
	misfortune, and the thing I most regret, that we shall never
	see the draft of the hundred fascicles. Anyone who loves and
	longs for our late master must copy this fascicle out and keep
	it safe. This is the Buddha's last command, and it is our late
	master's last testament.

	Now, on the fourteenth day of the seventh month of the seventh
	year of Kencho, the year of the hare, I have had Gien write it
	out, and the copying is finished; and I have collated it
	against the original at the same time. Recorded by Ejo.

The colophon of the original reads: written from the end of the fourth
year of Kencho to the sixth day of the first month of the fifth year.
Eiheiji.

From old times, it is said, those who have looked into this fascicle
have been moved to tears, and an abbot expounding it has wept aloud.
If in the last age this testament is kept, the manner of our school
will be fanned into flame for ever, the line will flow on, and it will
not fall away.

Since the fascicle is long, only the names of the eight are set down
here.

The fifth year of Kencho, the year of the ox. On the fourteenth day of
the seventh month, while the founder was still alive, Ejo, the second
abbot, at Eiheiji —

[[n105r]]
— entered the abbot's quarters.

At that installation the Founder handed on to the second abbot a
kesa he had sewn himself. It passed down from hand to hand as far as
the reverend Daichi, and the old record of it survives in his own hand
at Kofukuji in Higo, the temple Daichi founded. It reads, word for
word:

	One robe, handed on in succession, together with its kesa bag.

	This robe is the kesa of the first ancestor who founded Eihei.
	The cloth of it is fine linen. Among the first ancestor's lay
	disciples there was a man of the province of Yamashiro called
	Renbo. His wife, whose faith in the first ancestor was
	undivided, prepared and wove it herself under a vow of
	abstinence, and carried it the whole long way to Eiheiji in
	Echizen and offered it there. The first ancestor was moved by
	the undividedness of her faith, cut and sewed the robe himself,
	and wore it every day. At last, in the summer of the second
	year of Hoji, he sewed a bag for it and put it away.

	In the seventh month of the fifth year of Kencho, the year of
	the ox, when he handed the abbacy of Eihei to the second abbot,
	at the moment of handing it over he entrusted this kesa to the
	second abbot along with it. Accordingly, through the eighteen
	years of his abbacy, the second abbot wore it at the discourses
	in the hall and at the recitation of the precepts. And for
	twenty-eight years after the first ancestor's death he never
	once passed a night apart from the robe, but guarded it and
	received it on his head.

[[n105l]]
Afterwards, in his own illness, on the fifteenth day of the
	eighth month of the third year of Koan, he called Gikai and
	said to him: you are Ejo's eldest heir. This robe is the kesa
	our late master entrusted to me together with the abbacy. It is
	the most precious thing I own. Many have received the dharma
	from me, but you are the eldest heir, and so I mean to hand it
	on to you. Gikai made three bows and received it.

	For sixteen years after that he received it on his head and
	guarded it. On the fourteenth day of the first month of the
	third year of Einin, the year of the sheep, when he transmitted
	the dharma to Jokin, he handed on this robe with it, and said:
	this robe has now been received by three generations. It is a
	robe to be held in the highest reverence. Apart from entering
	the abbot's quarters, opening the hall and transmitting the
	dharma, you are not to wear it once in your life. Receive it on
	your head with reverence.

	Now, in the ninth month of the second year of Enkyo, the year
	of the cock, I write this from my sickbed.

	Fifty-third in descent from Shakyamuni Buddha; third of Eihei;
	founder of Daijoji: Gikai. [cipher]

	Received by Jokin, second abbot of Daijoji.

	Jokin declares: I now hand on this transmission robe, together
	with the abbacy of this temple, the sacred texts and the
	implements, and the deeds of gift and conveyance of this
	temple, to my successor. The tenth day of the tenth month of
	the first year of Ocho, the year of the boar. Recorded by
	Jokin, second abbot of Daijoji.

[[n106l]]
On the fifth day of the eighth month of that year the Founder went up
to the capital. Because of his illness the patron, the governor of
Izumo, had pressed him again and again to come up, and the second abbot
went with him; the object was that he should be seen by physicians.

The record of his last words says: on the sixth day of the eighth month
Gikai, at the lodging at Wakimoto, was given leave to go, and took the
occasion to ask him: hard as it is that I should not attend you this
time, what I really wish is to follow you back to the temple; and if
there is any delay, may I come up to the capital to see you? Will you
permit it?

The reverend master said: yes, certainly, that is quite right; there is
nothing to discuss. But it is because I am thinking of the temple that
I am leaving you behind. See to it, and look after the temple well. You
are a man of this province, and many in it know you, and there are
particular reasons, within the temple and outside it, why you should
stay.

Gikai received this with awe. It was his last sight of his face and his
last instruction, and he never forgot it.

There is a verse he made on going up to the capital:

	Ten years eating my rice on Eihei's ground;
	seven months now lying on a sickbed.
	To look for medicine I go out among men a while, over the
	ridge —

[[n107r]]
to see the King of Physicians.

And a Japanese poem:

	Setting out in a robe of grass
	over Kinobe mountain,
	it feels to me
	as though there were a road through the clouds.

He went up by the Tanba road.

On reaching the capital he lodged first at the house of a lay disciple,
Kakunen, at Takatsuji and Nishinotoin. His condition neither improved
nor worsened.

One day he walked slowly round the room and recited under his breath:

	Whether in a garden, or in a wood, or under a tree, or in a
	monk's quarters, or in a layman's house, or in a palace hall,
	or in a mountain valley or an empty moor — in every one of
	these places a stupa should be raised and offerings made. Why?
	Because you should know that this place is a place of the way.
	Here the buddhas attain the unsurpassed complete awakening;
	here the buddhas turn the wheel of the dharma; here the buddhas
	enter final nirvana.

[[n108l]]
When he had finished, he wrote that passage on the pillar in front of
him, and wrote beside it: the Hermitage of the Sutra of the Lotus of
the Wonderful Law. His meaning in setting out that passage of the Lotus
was that, since he was now dying in a layman's house, the buddhas of
old had said that even such a place is this.

On the twenty-eighth day of the eighth month of the fifth year of
Kencho, the year of the ox, in the hour of the rat, he wrote his own
verse and entered nirvana. The verse:

	Fifty-four years
	lighting up the first heaven.
	With this one leap
	I shatter the thousandfold worlds.
	Ah —
	my whole body has nowhere to rest.
	Living, I plunge into the Yellow Springs.

He threw down the brush and passed away.

Yoshishige of Izumo looked up at the sky and threw himself on the
ground, and there was nothing like his grief at a man dying so early,
at fifty-four. Kakunen and the other monks and lay people and the
disciples he left could not stop their crying. Ejo was shattered, and
for the better part of an hour he was as one dead.

After his death they carried him first to a small hut in Naka no Koji
at Tenjin in the capital, and made the arrangements there. Then the
governor of Izumo looked for a suitable place, and finding a small
temple at Akatsuji in Higashiyama, they moved the coffin there and
cremated him according to the rites. (Akatsuji is the place called
Akatsuki.)

[[n109r]]
The verse said at the lighting of the pyre, as it is set down in the
travel-record of the reverend Denshi Rinteki who founded Ryuchoin in
Higo. Whose composition it is has not been established.

	He searched the dark road everywhere and hid nothing at all;
	the dream broke after fifty-four frosts and stars.
	The wood goes into the ashes; the tears fall like rain on
	blossom;
	and for one night the whole city smells of running water.

On the sixth day of the ninth month they gathered the relics and left
the capital, and on the tenth, at the hour of the cock, reached Mount
Kichijo in the Shibi estate in Echizen. On the twelfth, at the hour of
the monkey, the rites of entering nirvana were performed in the abbot's
quarters. Tea and sweets and fine food and candles were set out
according to the rule, offerings were made, and the services and the
observances of mourning were all carried out as though he were present.
The stupa is at the north-west corner of the main temple: it is the
present Joyo-an.

Kakunen afterwards took away the pillar on which the passage of the
Sutra of the Lotus of the Wonderful Law had been written, cut it free
and carried it down to Echizen, and at the foot of Mount Tsukio in what
is now the south-east district he raised a stupa for the first time,
making that pillar the central post of it, and it is said he made
offerings there every day. That is the present branch temple.
