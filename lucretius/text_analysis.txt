LUCRETIUS: ON THE NATURE OF THINGS (De Rerum Natura)
Translation strategy. From the LATIN (Perseus' text), with W. E. Leonard's
1916 blank verse under reference/ as a per-file crib ONLY, aligned card by
card.

READ FIRST: ovid/text_analysis.txt (Latin hexameter -> vivid modern PROSE,
the ratio band, cult-title resolution) and odyssey/text_analysis.txt (a
verse epic rendered as prose, and what the verse was carrying that must
be kept instead).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHY THIS BOOK
--------------------------------------------------------------------
The one Roman poem the Founders read as philosophy: Jefferson owned five
copies and called himself an Epicurean. It is the fullest statement of
atomism to survive from antiquity -- matter and void, atoms swerving,
the soul as a material thing that dies with the body, the gods as
uninvolved, the world as one of many, lightning and magnets and the
plague explained without a god in them -- and it is also a great poem,
which is what nobody who has only heard of it expects. The public-domain
English is Munro's 1864 prose (a crib, grammatical and dead), Leonard's
1916 blank verse (readable but padded to the metre, full of "thee" and
"'tis"), and Watson. None of them lets a reader FOLLOW THE ARGUMENT,
which is the one thing Lucretius asks: "attend with unbusied ears".

--------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE FORM: PROSE
--------------------------------------------------------------------
The ovid/ and odyssey/ precedent. The hexameter has no English
equivalent that is not padding, and this is a 7,400-line ARGUMENT. What
the verse carried is kept instead, and it is a great deal:
  - THE PROEMS to each book (Venus, Epicurus as the hero who trampled
    religion, the sweetness of watching a storm from the shore, the
    honey on the cup) are set-pieces and get full imaginative weight.
  - THE SIMILES are the poem's method: motes in a sunbeam, sheep on a
    hillside seen from far off as a white blur, letters rearranged into
    different words as atoms into things, the cow searching the pastures
    for her sacrificed calf. Every one is kept, at full length, exact.
  - THE HORROR set-pieces -- Iphigenia at the altar with her father's
    knife, the plague at Athens closing Book Six with no consolation
    after it -- are rendered as vividly as the Latin, and the plague
    ENDS THE POEM, as it does in the manuscripts. No softening, no note.
  - THE ARGUMENT'S JOINTS stay visible: "first... next... besides...
    again..." Lucretius signposts constantly and the signposts are what
    make 7,400 lines followable. Paragraph at his joints (the cards are
    Perseus' paragraphs and are a good guide).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
3. VOICE
--------------------------------------------------------------------
A man explaining, urgently, to one friend (Memmius) whom he addresses
by name and as "you", why he need not be afraid. He is impatient with
stupidity, contemptuous of religion (religio -- "superstition" is
wrong, it means the whole system of dread), tender about animals and
children, and exact about everything physical. The tone is a brilliant
teacher's: confident, occasionally hectoring ("do you not see?"), often
funny (the lover who praises his girl's every defect in Greek), and at
its height -- the end of Book Three on why death is nothing to us --
grave without being solemn. Write it as SPEECH to one listener.
Keep the second person singular throughout as "you"; Memmius is named
where Lucretius names him.
THE hume RULE applies to the famous lines: "so much evil could religion
prompt" (tantum religio potuit suadere malorum), "nothing can be made
from nothing", "death is nothing to us" -- plain, and pinned.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
4. LOCKED VOCABULARY -- the physics must be CONSISTENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Lucretius has no word "atom" and uses a dozen periphrases (primordia,
semina rerum, corpora prima, genitalia corpora, elementa, exordia,
figurae). RULING: render them ALL as "atoms" where the sense is the
particle, with the metaphor kept alongside where it is doing work
("the seeds of things" where he means generation; "first-beginnings"
NEVER -- that is Munro). This is the spinoza/ethics locked-glossary
pattern, and the ledger is running_notes.txt.
  inane            "void" (never "vacuum", never "empty space" alone).
  religio          "religion" -- his target is the system, and
                   "superstition" lets the modern reader off the hook
                   he means to catch them on. Keep it.
  natura           "nature"; rerum natura "the nature of things".
  animus / anima   MIND / SOUL -- he distinguishes them (Book Three)
                   and the argument depends on it. Never merge.
  clinamen         "the swerve".
  simulacra        "images" (the films thrown off by surfaces, Book
                   Four); never "idols".
  foedus / foedera naturae   "the laws of nature" ("the treaties" is
                   the dead metaphor and dead).
  voluptas         "pleasure"; the Epicurean good.
  Names: Roman gods by Roman names (Venus, Jupiter, Neptune, Ceres,
  Bacchus for Liber); Greek philosophers by their familiar forms;
  Iphianassa -> Iphigenia (he uses the Homeric form; the reader knows
  the other); Memmius kept.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE TEXT HAS GAPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Twenty lines are lost in transmission (<gap> in Perseus, 8 in Book
One). Translate what is there; where a gap breaks a sentence, complete
the sense from the surrounding argument in the plainest way and do NOT
mark it -- a bracket would be the wink the Verne rule forbids, and the
crib shows what Leonard did. Book Four's opening repeats Book One's
lines 926-950 nearly verbatim (the honey-on-the-cup passage); translate
both, and make them match word for word (the nights/ rule for a
repeated formula).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
6. THE VERNE RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------
No editorial note anywhere. His physics is his: the sun the size it
looks, the moon possibly lit from within, thunder from clouds
colliding, the earth flat-ish at the centre of a world that will one
day fall apart, spontaneous generation, the magnet explained by
streams of atoms. All rendered as HIS claims, confident as he is. The
attack on love in Book Four (sex as an itch, the lover's delusions
catalogued in Greek diminutives) and the dismissal of an afterlife
stand unhedged. Nothing is corrected against modern physics, and the
places where he is astonishingly right (the atoms, the conservation of
matter, natural selection of a sort in Book Five) get no wink either.
