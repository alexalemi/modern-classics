Book One: Matter and Void
(Part 2 of 2)

Besides, if nature had set no limit at all to the breaking of things,
the bodies of matter would by now have been ground so far down by the
breaking of ages gone by that nothing conceived out of them could reach
the full end of its life at its appointed time. For we see that anything
at all can be pulled apart faster than it can be put back together. So
whatever the long and endless age of all past time had already broken,
disturbing it and dissolving it, could never be repaired in the time
that was left. As it is, plainly a fixed limit to the breaking stands
where it was set, since we see each thing repaired, and see that fixed
periods are laid down for things, kind by kind, in which they can come
to the flower of their age.

Add this as well. Though the bodies of matter are utterly solid, we can
still give an account of everything that turns out soft — air, water,
earth, hot vapours — of how they come to be and by what force each of
them is carried on, since void has once and for all been mixed into
things. But suppose instead that the atoms were soft. Then no account
could be given of where hard flint and iron come from, because their
whole nature will be short of a foundation to start from. So the atoms
are strong in solid simplicity, and it is by packing them into a denser
combination that everything can be pulled tight and made to show
unbeatable strength.

Then again, if no limit has been set to the breaking of bodies, still
some bodies must have survived out of everlasting time down to this
moment, bodies not yet reported to have been tried by any danger. But
since the things they talk about are furnished with a breakable nature,
it does not fit that they could have lasted through everlasting time,
worried as they are age after age by countless blows.

And finally: since a limit has been set, kind by kind, on how far things
grow and how long they keep their life; since it stands laid down what
each thing can do by the laws of nature and what it cannot; since
nothing is changed about, but everything holds so firm that all the
different birds show, one after another, the markings of their own kind
on their bodies — then they must also have a body of unchangeable
matter. For if atoms could be beaten and changed in some way or other,
then it would be uncertain as well what could come to be and what could
not, and on what principle each thing has its power limited and a
boundary stone driven deep. Nor could the generations, kind after kind,
bring back so regularly the nature, the habits, the food and the
movements of their parents.

Then, further. Since there is an outermost tip to each of those bodies
which our senses can no longer make out, that tip plainly exists without
parts and is of the smallest nature there is. It never was separate on
its own, and it never will be able to be, because it is itself a part of
something else, its first and single part; and after it other parts, and
others like them, in order, fill out the nature of the body in a packed
column. Since these cannot exist on their own, they must cling together
in a way that lets nothing prise them apart.

So the atoms are of solid simplicity, packed tight and holding closely
together by these least parts — not put together out of any coming
together of those parts, but strong instead in an everlasting
simplicity, out of which nature, keeping seeds in reserve for things,
allows nothing whatever to be torn away or taken off.

Besides, if there is no least thing, the very smallest bodies will
consist of infinite parts, since a half will always have a half of its
half and nothing will ever set a limit to it. What difference will there
be, then, between the sum of things and the smallest thing in it? None
at all. However utterly infinite the whole sum is, still the smallest
things there are will consist of infinite parts just the same. Since
true reasoning shouts this down and denies that the mind can believe it,
you have to give in and admit that there are things which have no parts
and are of the smallest nature; and since these exist, you must admit
that the atoms themselves are solid and everlasting too.

Finally, if nature, the creator of things, were in the habit of forcing
everything to be dissolved into least parts, she would not then be able
to build anything up again out of them. The reason is that things not
enlarged with parts cannot have the properties that begetting material
has to have: the various connections, weights, blows, meetings and
motions by which everything is carried on.

So those who thought that the material of things is fire, and that the
sum of things consists of fire alone, plainly slipped a long way from
true reasoning. The leader who goes into that battle first is
Heraclitus, famous for his dark speech more among the empty-headed than
among the serious Greeks, who are looking for the truth. For fools
admire and love above everything the things they see hiding under
upside-down words, and they set down as true whatever can prettily
tickle the ear and is painted over with a charming sound.

For how, I ask, could things be so varied, if they are created out of
one pure fire? It would be no help for hot fire to be packed close or
thinned out, if the parts of fire had the same nature that the whole
fire above them has. The burning would be sharper with the parts drawn
together and slacker with them scattered and spread apart, and that is
the whole of what you can suppose might come about from causes like
these. Still less could so great a variety of things arise out of fires
dense and rare.

There is this too. If they allow void to be mixed into things, then
fires can be packed close and left rare. But because they see plenty of
things rising up against them, and shrink from leaving pure void in
things, they are afraid of the steep road and lose the true way. They do
not see, on the other hand, that if void is taken out of things
everything is packed close and one body is made out of them all, a body
with no power to shoot anything off itself quickly, the way the
heat-carrying fire throws out light and warmth. That lets you see that
fire is not made of parts crammed tight.

But suppose they believe that fires can be put out in some other way
when they combine, and can change their body. If they will not hold back
from doing so in any part at all, then plainly all the burning will die
away utterly into nothing, and whatever is created will be created out
of nothing. For whenever anything is changed and steps outside its own
limits, that is at once the death of what it was before. So something
must survive unharmed in those fires, or everything will fall back into
nothing for you, and the supply of things will be born again out of
nothing and flourish.

Now then, since there are certain bodies of the surest kind which keep
their nature always the same, and by whose going out or coming in and
change of order things change their nature and bodies are turned into
other bodies, you can see that these bodies of things are not fiery. It
would make no difference for some of them to withdraw and go away, and
others to be added, and some to be changed in order, if they all still
kept the nature of heat: whatever they created would in any case be
fire. The truth, I think, is this. There are certain bodies whose
meetings, motions, order, position and shapes produce fire, and which,
when their order is changed, change the nature of what they make; and
they are not like fire, nor like anything else which can send bodies to
the senses and touch our sense of touch by striking it.

To go further and say that everything is fire, and that no true thing
exists in the number of things except fire, which is what this same man
does, seems raving madness. He fights against the senses out of the
senses themselves, and shakes loose the very thing on which all belief
hangs, and by which the thing he calls fire has become known to him in
the first place. For he believes that the senses know fire truly, and
does not believe them about everything else, which is no less clear.
That seems to me both empty and mad. Where shall we refer things to?
What can be more certain for us than the senses themselves, by which we
mark off true from false?

Besides, why should anyone do away with everything else and want to
leave the nature of burning standing alone, rather than deny that fire
exists and leave some other nature standing? It looks like an equal
madness to say either.

So those who thought that the material of things is fire, and that the
sum of things can consist of fire; and those who set air up as the
beginning for the begetting of things; and any who thought that water
shapes things all by itself, or that earth creates everything and turns
into every nature of things — all of them plainly wandered a long way
off from the truth. Add as well those who double the atoms, joining air
to fire and earth to water, and those who think everything can grow out
of four things: out of fire, earth, breath and rain.

First among them is Empedocles of Acragas, whom that three-cornered
island carried on the coasts of its lands: the island that the Ionian
sea flows round in great windings, splashing salt spray from its
grey-green waves, and where a narrow channel divides with its rushing
water the coasts of that island's territory from the shores of Aeolia.
Here is the wasting whirlpool of Charybdis, and here the rumblings of
Etna threaten to gather their angry flames together again, so that its
force may spew out once more the fires torn from its throat and carry
the lightning of its flame up to the sky once more. Now that region
seems in many ways a great wonder to the peoples of men, and is said to
be worth going to see, rich in good things and fortified with a great
strength of men; and yet it plainly held in it nothing more famous than
this man, nothing more holy, more wonderful, more dear. What is more,
the songs of his godlike heart cry out and set forth his glorious
discoveries, so that he hardly seems to have been created out of human
stock.

Yet he, and the men I mentioned before, who are far beneath him and much
smaller in a great many ways, though they found out many fine and
godlike things and gave their answers as if out of the shrine of the
heart, more holy and far more surely reasoned than what the Pythia
pronounces from the tripod and the laurel of Apollo, still made their
ruin over the atoms; and there, great as they were, they fell greatly
and heavily.

First, because they set up motion with void taken out of things, and
leave things soft and rare in existence — air, sun, fire, earth,
animals, crops — and yet mix no void into their bodies. Next, because
they will have it that there is no end at all to the cutting of bodies,
and no stopping place for the breaking, and no least thing standing firm
in things at all — when we see that the outermost tip of each thing is
what seems least to our senses, from which you can work out that the
things whose outermost tip you cannot see have one all the same, and
that there is a least standing firm among things.

Add to this that since they set up soft atoms, which we see are born and
have a mortal body, the whole sum of things would have to go back
utterly into nothing by now, and the supply of things be born again out
of nothing and flourish. You can grasp by now how far each of those is
from the truth. Then again, the four are enemies to each other in many
ways and poison to each other, so that when they meet they will either
die or fly apart in the way we see lightnings and rains and winds fly
apart when a storm has driven them together.

Finally, if everything is created out of four things, and everything is
dissolved back into those things, why should they be called the atoms of
things any more than things should be thought, backwards and the other
way round, the atoms of them? They are begotten out of each other by
turns, and they exchange colour and their whole nature with each other,
and always have.

But suppose you think that the body of fire and earth, and the breezes
of air, and the dew of water can meet in such a way that nothing changes
its nature in the joining. Then nothing can be created for you out of
them: nothing that breathes, and nothing with a lifeless body either,
like a tree. In that mixed heap each of them will show its own nature,
and air will plainly be seen staying mixed with earth, and heat staying
mixed with a certain dew. But atoms, in the begetting of things, must
bring in a hidden and unseen nature, so that nothing sticks out that
fights against it and stops whatever is created from being properly
itself.

What is more, they go back to the sky and its fires. First they make
fire turn itself into the breezes of air; from there rain is begotten,
and earth created out of rain; and then everything goes back the other
way from earth, moisture first, then air, then heat; and these never
stop changing into one another and travelling from sky to earth and from
earth to the stars of the world. That is exactly what atoms must never
do. Something unchangeable has to survive, or everything will be brought
down utterly to nothing; for whenever anything is changed and steps
outside its own limits, that is at once the death of what it was before.
So, since the things I named a little earlier do come into a state of
change, they must consist of other things which can never be turned into
anything else, or everything will fall back into nothing for you. Why
not rather set up certain bodies furnished with such a nature that, if
they happen to have created fire, the same bodies can make the breezes
of air, with a few taken away and a few given, and their order and
motion changed, and all other things be changed in the same way into all
others?

Here you object. The facts, you say, plainly show that all things grow
up out of the earth into the breezes of air and are fed there; and
unless the weather is kind at a lucky season with rains, so that the
trees rock under the melting of the storm clouds, and unless the sun for
its part warms them and gives them heat, crops and trees and living
things could not grow.

Quite so. And unless dry food and soft moisture helped us, our body
would be lost and all our life would be loosened out of all our sinews
and bones. Beyond any doubt we are helped and fed by certain things, and
other things are helped and fed by others. Plainly it is because many
atoms, held in common in many ways by many things, are mixed together in
things that different things are fed by different things. And it often
makes a great difference which other atoms the same atoms are held
together with, and in what arrangement, and what motions they give and
take among themselves. For the same atoms make up sky, sea, lands,
rivers, sun; the same make up crops, trees, living things; but they are
mixed with different atoms and in a different way and so they move
differently. Indeed you can see, scattered all through these verses of
mine, many letters shared by many words, and yet you have to admit that
the verses and the words differ from one another both in what they mean
and in the sound they make. That is how much letters can do by nothing
but a change of order. But the atoms of things can bring more than that
into play, and out of them all the different things there are can be
created.

Now let us also examine the homoeomeria of Anaxagoras, as the Greeks
call it — the poverty of our native speech does not let us say the word
in our own language, though it is easy enough to set the thing itself
out in words. To begin with, what he calls the homoeomeria of things is
this. Bones, he thinks, are begotten out of tiny little bones; flesh out
of tiny little scraps of flesh; blood is created when many drops of
blood run together with each other; he supposes that gold can consist of
specks of gold, and earth grow together out of little earths, fire out
of fires, moisture out of moistures; and he imagines and supposes the
rest on a similar principle. And yet he does not allow that there is
void in any part of things, or that there is any end to the cutting of
bodies. So on both counts alike he seems to me to go wrong just as much
as the men I spoke of before.

Add that the atoms he imagines are far too weak, if they are atoms which
are furnished with the same nature as the things themselves, and suffer
and perish just as those do, with nothing to rein them back from
destruction. Which of them will hold out under a hard crushing, and
escape death under the very teeth of destruction? Fire? Water? Air?
Which of them? Blood, or bone? Not one, I think, when everything will
be, on the same footing, as utterly mortal as the things we plainly see
beaten by some force and perishing in front of our eyes. But that things
cannot fall back into nothing, and cannot grow out of nothing either, I
call to witness what has been proved above.

Besides, since food increases the body and feeds it, you can see that
our veins and blood and bones and sinews are made of particles of a
different kind from themselves. Or if they will say that all foods have
a mixed body and hold in themselves small bodies of sinew, and bones,
and veins as well, and parts of gore, it will follow that every food,
dry or liquid alike, is to be thought to consist of things of another
kind: of bones and sinews and matter and blood mixed together.

Besides, whatever bodies grow out of the earth, if they are in the
earth, then the earth must consist of things of another kind which rise
up out of the earth. Shift the argument across, and you may use the very
same words: if flame and smoke and ash lie hidden in wood, then the wood
must consist of things of another kind. Besides, whatever bodies the
ground feeds and increases must be of another kind, things which rise up
out of the wood.

At this point a certain slender chance of hiding is left, and Anaxagoras
takes it for himself. He thinks that all things lie hidden mixed in with
all things, and that the one thing which shows itself is the one whose
mixed particles are most numerous, and lie more ready to hand and set in
the front rank. That, though, is driven far away from true reasoning.
For then it would be fitting that grains of corn, when they are crushed
under the threatening strength of a stone, should often let out some
sign of blood, or of something else that is fed in our body; and when we
grind stone on stone, gore ought to ooze out. On the same principle
grasses too ought often to let out sweet drops with the same taste as
the milk in the udders of woolly sheep. Certainly, when clods of earth
are crumbled up, we ought to see kinds of grasses and grains and leaves
hidden there, scattered small among the soil; and finally, when wood is
broken apart, we ought to see ash and smoke in it, and tiny fires lying
hidden. Since plain fact teaches that none of this happens, you can see
that things are not mixed with things in that way, but that seeds common
to many things must lie hidden in things, mixed in many ways.

Here you object again. It often happens on great mountains, you say,
that the topmost tips of tall trees close to one another are rubbed
together, with strong south winds forcing them to it, until the flower
of flame springs up and they blaze.

Quite so. And yet fire is not planted in the wood. There are many seeds
of heat, and when the rubbing has made them flow together they create
the fires in the forests. If ready-made flame lay hidden away in the
forests, the fires could not be kept out of sight for any length of
time; they would be finishing off the woods everywhere and burning the
trees up. So do you see now, as I said a little while ago, that it makes
an enormous difference which other atoms these same atoms are held
together with, and in what arrangement, and what motions they give and
take among themselves; and that the same atoms, changed a little among
themselves, create fires and create wood? It is just like the words
themselves, which are made by changing their letters a little among
themselves: ligna, wood, and ignes, fires, are almost the same letters,
and we mark them off with different sounds.

Finally, if you think that whatever you make out among things in the
open cannot happen unless you imagine bodies of matter furnished with a
nature like the thing they make, then on that reckoning the atoms of
things are destroyed for you. It will come about that they are shaken by
fits of laughter and roar out loud, and wet their faces and cheeks with
salt tears.

Now come: learn what is left, and hear it more clearly. I am not blind
to how dark these matters are. But the great hope of praise has struck
my heart with its sharp staff of ivy, and at the same moment struck
sweet love of the Muses into my breast; and driven on by that, with my
mind in full strength, I go out on the road.

I am travelling over the pathless places of the Muses, where nobody's
foot has trodden before. I love coming on springs nobody has touched,
and drinking them dry; I love picking new flowers, and hunting from
there a glorious crown for my head, from ground where the Muses have
never yet veiled anyone's temples. First, because I am teaching about
great matters, and going on to loose the mind from the tight knots of
religion; and then because on so dark a subject I am making verses so
clear, touching everything with the charm of the Muses. That too seems
to have a reason behind it. It is like doctors trying to give children
foul wormwood: first they brush the rim of the cup all round with the
sweet yellow liquid of honey, so that the unthinking years of childhood
are fooled as far as the lips, and meanwhile the bitter draught of
wormwood is drunk right down. The children are tricked, but they are not
trapped; instead they are put back on their feet that way and grow
strong again. In the same way now, since this reasoning generally seems
rather grim to those who have not handled it, and the crowd shrinks back
from it, I have wanted to set our reasoning out for you in the
sweet-speaking song of the Muses, and to touch it, so to speak, with
their sweet honey, in the hope of holding your mind on my verses that
way, until you see right through the whole nature of things and the
shape in which it is put together.

But since I have taught that the utterly solid bodies of matter fly
about unbeaten through all time, come now, let us unroll the question
whether there is any limit to the sum of them or not. And likewise,
about the void that has been found, place and space, in which everything
is carried on, let us see clearly whether the whole of it is bounded
through and through, or lies open immeasurable and vastly deep.

The whole of what exists, then, is bounded in no region of its ways;
because it would have to have an outermost point. And plainly nothing
can have an outermost point unless there is something beyond to set the
boundary, so that a place can be seen past which the nature of our sense
does not follow it. Now, since we have to admit that there is nothing
outside the sum of things, it has no outermost point, and so it lacks
any end or limit. And it makes no difference in which of its regions you
take your stand. Whatever place a man has taken, he leaves the whole
infinity just as great in every direction from himself.

Besides, suppose for the moment that the whole space that exists is set
down as bounded. If someone ran out to the furthest coasts, right to the
edge, and threw a flying spear, would you rather have it that the spear,
hurled with strong force, goes where it was sent and flies far off, or
do you think something could get in the way and block it? You have to
admit and take up one or the other of the two, and each of them shuts
off your escape and forces you to grant that the whole lies open with
its boundary taken away. For whether there is something to check the
spear and stop it coming where it was sent and planting itself at the
boundary, or whether it is carried on outside, it did not start from a
boundary. In this way I shall follow you, and wherever you put the
furthest coasts I shall ask: what in the end becomes of the spear? It
will turn out that a boundary can stand nowhere, and that the chance of
further flight always puts the flight off further.

Besides, if the whole space of the entire sum were shut in on every side
and stood inside fixed coasts, and were bounded, the supply of matter
would by now have flowed together from every side to the bottom by its
solid weights, and nothing could be carried on under the roof of the
sky. Indeed there would be no sky at all and no light of the sun, since
all the material would be lying in a heap, having been settling for an
infinity of time already. As it is, plainly there is no rest given to
the bodies of the atoms, because there is no bottom at all for them to
flow together to, so to speak, and no place where they might take up a
seat. Everything is always carried on in ceaseless motion, in every
part, and swift bodies of matter are supplied from below, out of the
infinite.

Finally, in front of our eyes one thing is seen to bound another. Air
walls off the hills and mountains wall off the air; land bounds the sea,
and the sea in turn bounds all lands. But as for the whole, there is
nothing outside it to bound it.

So the nature of place and the space of the deep is such that the bright
thunderbolts could not run through it in their course, gliding along the
everlasting stretch of time, and could not bring it about at all that
there is less left to go as they travel. So widely, in all directions,
does a huge extent lie open for things, with boundaries taken away, on
every side.

Nature keeps the sum of things from being able to set a measure to
itself: she forces body to be bounded by void, and what is void to be
bounded by body, and so by these alternations she makes the whole of it
infinite. Or else, if one of the two does not set a boundary to the
other, the one that is left would still stretch out unmeasured by its
own single nature. Nothing holds together in any other way. Neither sea,
nor earth, nor the bright regions of the sky, nor the race of mortals,
nor the holy bodies of the gods could hold their place for the smallest
space of an hour. For, driven out of its own gathering, the supply of
matter would be carried, dissolved, through the great void; or rather it
would never have come together and created anything at all, since
scattered as it was it could not have been driven together.

For certainly the atoms of things did not place themselves each in their
own order by design, with a shrewd mind, and they certainly did not make
a bargain about what motions each of them should make. But because many
of them, changed in many ways, are knocked about and driven by blows out
of the infinite through the whole, they at last, by trying out every
kind of motion and combination, come into the sort of arrangements in
which this created sum of things stands. And once the sum of things had
been thrown into fitting motions, and kept in them through many great
years, it brings it about that the rivers fill the greedy sea again with
the generous waters of their streams, and that the earth, warmed by the
heat of the sun, renews what it brings forth, and that the race of
living things springs up and flowers, and that the gliding fires of the
upper air stay alive.

They would do none of this in any way at all if a supply of matter could
not rise up out of the infinite, out of which they are used to repairing
their losses in due season. For just as the nature of living things,
robbed of food, wastes away and loses its body, so everything must be
broken up as soon as material, turned aside from its path in one way or
another, fails to be supplied. Nor can blows from outside keep the whole
of every sum safe, whatever has been put together. They can hammer
often, certainly, and hold part of it back until others arrive and the
sum can be filled up. But now and then they are forced to bounce back
and, in doing it, to grant the atoms of things both room and time for
flight, so that they can be carried off free of the gathering. So again
and again: many atoms must rise up. And even for the blows themselves to
be able to keep coming, there is need of an infinite force of matter on
every side.

In these matters, Memmius, run far from believing this: that everything
presses towards the centre of the sum, as they say it does, and that the
nature of the world therefore stands firm with no blows from outside,
and that top and bottom cannot be broken apart in any direction because
everything presses towards the centre — if you believe that anything at
all can rest on itself. They say that the heavy bodies under the earth
all press upwards and come to rest set backwards against the earth, like
the reflections of things we now see in water. On the same principle
they insist that living things wander about upside down and cannot fall
out of the earth into the regions of the sky below, any more than our
own bodies can fly of their own accord into the regions of the sky
above; that when those creatures are looking at the sun, we are looking
at the stars of the night; and that they share the seasons of the sky
with us turn by turn, and pass nights matched with our days.

But an empty mistake has handed these dreams to fools, who have taken
them up on a crooked argument. There can be no centre, when the whole is
infinite; and even if there were a centre now, nothing could stand fixed
there any more than it might be driven away from there for some quite
different reason. For all place and space, which we call void, has to
give way through the centre and through what is not the centre alike,
wherever weights are carried by their motions. There is no place where
bodies, when they have come there, can lose the force of their weight
and stand still in the void; and what is void must not hold anything up,
but must go on giving way, as its own nature demands. So things cannot
be held together in a gathering in that way, beaten by a craving for a
centre.

Besides, since they do not pretend that all bodies press towards the
centre, but only those of earth and water — the moisture of the sea, and
the great waves off the mountains, and whatever is held in, so to speak,
by an earthen body — while on the other side they set out how the thin
breezes of air and the hot fires alike are carried away from the centre,
and how for that reason the whole upper air trembles all round with
stars and the flame of the sun is fed through the blue of the sky,
because all the heat fleeing from the centre gathers itself there; and
how the topmost branches on the trees could not put out leaves at all,
unless food came up to each of them, little by little, from the earth —
since all this is what they pretend, all of it is patched together for
one reason only. It is so that the walls of the world shall not suddenly
fly apart in the manner of winged flames, dissolved through the great
void, and so that the rest shall not follow them in the same way; so
that the thundering regions of the sky shall not rush down from above,
and the earth shall not snatch itself out from under our feet, and
everything shall not go off, loosing its bodies, through the deep void,
among the mingled wrecks of things and of the sky, so that in a point of
time nothing at all is left over except deserted space and unseen atoms.
For on whatever side you decide that bodies are lacking first, that side
will be the door of death for things, and through it the whole crowd of
matter will hurl itself out.

You will come to know all this thoroughly, led on by a little effort.
One thing will grow clear from another, and blind night will not take
the road away from you and stop you seeing all the way to the last
things of nature. So things will kindle lights for other things.
