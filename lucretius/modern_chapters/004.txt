Book Three: The Mind, the Soul, and the Fear of Death
(Part 1 of 2)

You, who first were able to raise so clear a light out of such
darkness, and to light up what is good in life: you I follow, glory of
the Greek people, and I set my own feet now in the marks your feet
pressed into the ground. It is not so much that I want to compete with
you. It is love, and the longing to copy you. What can a swallow do
against swans? What could kids on their shaky legs manage in a race
against the strength of a powerful horse? You are our father, the man
who found out the nature of things; you supply us with a father's
teaching; and out of your pages, famous one, we feed on all your
golden sayings, the way bees in flowering meadows sip from everything.
Golden sayings, and always the most worthy of everlasting life.

For the moment your reasoning begins to cry out and to declare the
nature of things, born from a mind like a god's, the terrors of the
mind scatter, the walls of the world part, and I see things going on
all through the void. The divine power of the gods comes into view,
and their quiet dwellings, which no winds shake, which no rain clouds
spatter, which no snow packed hard by sharp frost harms as it falls
white; a sky forever cloudless roofs them over and laughs with light
poured out wide. Nature supplies them with everything, and nothing at
any time nibbles at the peace of their minds. But the regions of
Acheron are nowhere in sight, and the earth is no bar to seeing
everything that goes on below, under our feet, through the void. At
all this a kind of divine pleasure takes hold of me, and a shudder
with it, because by your power nature has been laid open like this,
so plainly, uncovered on every side.

And since I have taught you what the atoms of all things are like, how
they differ in their many shapes, how they fly about of their own
accord, driven by an everlasting motion, and in what way each thing
can be created out of them, the next thing after that is plainly this:
the nature of the mind and of the soul must now be made clear in my
verses, and that fear of Acheron must be driven headlong out of doors,
the fear that stirs up human life from the very bottom and washes
everything over with the blackness of death, and leaves no pleasure
clear and pure.

Men will often tell you that they are more afraid of diseases and of a
life of disgrace than they are of the underworld of death; that they
know the nature of the mind is blood, or wind if that is the way the
whim takes them; and that they have no need of this reasoning of ours
at all. From what follows you can see that all of this is thrown out
for the sake of praise rather than because the thing itself has
convinced them. Take those same men, driven out of their country,
banished far from the sight of other people, fouled with some
disgraceful charge, worn down in the end by every kind of misery: they
go on living, and wherever the poor wretches come, they still make the
offerings to their ancestors, still slaughter black sheep, still send
gifts down to the gods of the dead; and in their bitter state they
turn their minds far more sharply to religion.

That is why it is better to watch a man in doubt and in danger, and to
learn who he is when things go against him. Only then are true words
drawn up out of the bottom of his chest; the mask is torn off, and
what is real is left. Then again, greed, and the blind hunger for high
office, which drive miserable men to step over the boundaries of law
and sometimes to be partners and servants in crime, straining night
and day with enormous effort to come out on top of the heap: these
wounds of life are fed in no small part by the fear of death.

For disgrace and contempt and grinding poverty generally look a long
way from a sweet and settled life, and look like a kind of loitering
already at the gates of death. So while men are driven by a false
terror and want to escape those things and put a great distance
between themselves and them, they build up a fortune out of the blood
of their fellow citizens, they greedily double their riches, piling
killing on killing; they take a cruel delight in the sad funeral of a
brother, and they hate and fear the tables of their own kin. In just
the same way, and out of the same fear, envy wastes them away: that
man is powerful in front of their eyes, that man is stared at, walking
in bright office, while they, they complain, are rolling about in
darkness and mud. Some of them destroy themselves for the sake of
statues and a name.

And often the fear of death takes such a hold on people that a hatred
of living and of seeing the light comes over them, and with grief in
their hearts they put themselves to death, forgetting that this fear
is the spring their troubles rise from. It is this fear that eats away
their sense of shame, this that talks them into breaking the ties of
friendship and overturning duty altogether. Before now men have
betrayed their country and their dear parents in the attempt to keep
clear of the regions of Acheron. For just as children tremble and are
afraid of everything in the blind dark, so we are sometimes afraid in
the light of things that are no more to be feared than the ones
children shudder at in the dark, and imagine are coming for them. So
this terror of the mind and this darkness have to be scattered. Not by
the rays of the sun and the bright arrows of daylight, but by looking
at nature and working out how it runs.

First, then, I say that the mind, which we often call the
intelligence, and in which the planning and the steering of life is
located, is a part of a man no less than the hand and the foot and the
eyes are parts of a whole living creature. There are people who hold
that the mind's power of feeling is not located in any fixed part, but
is a kind of living condition of the body, which the Greeks call a
harmony: that this is what makes us live with feeling, though the
intelligence is not in any one part. Just as we often say that a body
is in good health, and yet the health is not one part of the healthy
man, so they refuse to put the mind's feeling in a fixed part. In this
they seem to me to go badly wrong, and in several directions at once.

For often the body, which is out where we can see it, is sick, while
we are enjoying ourselves in some other part that is hidden away; and
often it happens the other way round in turn, when a man who is
wretched in his mind is enjoying himself all through his body. It is
exactly like a sick man whose foot is in pain while his head, all the
while, happens to be in no pain at all. Besides, when the limbs are
given up to soft sleep, and the loaded body lies sprawled and without
feeling, there is still something else in us which at that very time is
stirred in all sorts of ways and takes into itself every motion of joy
and all the heart's empty cares.

Now, so that you can also recognise that there is a soul in the limbs,
and that the body is not in the habit of feeling by any harmony: to
begin with, a great deal of the body is often taken away, and yet life
stays on in what is left of the limbs. And then again, when a few
bodies of heat have scattered and some air has been let out through the
mouth, that same life abandons the veins on the spot and leaves the
bones. From this you can see that not all the bodies in us play equal
parts, or hold up our welfare equally; it is rather these, the seeds
of wind and of warm vapour, that see to it that life stays in the
limbs. So there is a life-giving heat and wind in the body itself, and
it is this that leaves our limbs when we are dying. Therefore, since
the nature of mind and of soul has been found to be a part of a man,
so to speak, hand back the name harmony, which was brought down to the
musicians from high Helicon; or else the musicians got it from
somewhere else themselves and carried it across to this thing, which
at the time had no name of its own. Whatever the truth of that is,
they are welcome to it. You take in the rest of what I have to say.

Now, I say that mind and soul are held joined to each other and make
one nature out of themselves, but that the head of it, so to speak,
the thing that rules in the whole body, is the planning power which we
call the mind and the intelligence, and that this stays fixed in the
middle region of the chest. This is where fear and dread leap; around
this place delight strokes us soothingly; here, then, is where the
intelligence and the mind are. The rest of the soul, spread out
through the whole body, obeys, and moves at the mind's nod and
impulse. The mind, by itself and through itself alone, has
understanding for itself and takes joy for itself, at times when
nothing at all is stirring either soul or body.

And just as, when the head or an eye is hurt in us by the attack of
pain, we are not tortured all through the body, so the mind is
sometimes hurt on its own, or thrives with delight, while the rest of
the soul through the limbs and joints is not stirred by anything new.
But when the mind has been shaken by a more violent fear, we see the
whole soul feeling it too, all along the limbs. Sweat and paleness
break out over the whole body; the tongue is broken and the voice
fails; the eyes go dark, the ears ring, the joints give way. In fact
we often see men drop down from terror of the mind. So anybody can
easily learn from this that the soul is joined to the mind, and that
when it has been struck by the mind's force it shoves the body in
turn, and hits it.

This same reasoning teaches that the nature of the mind and of the
soul is bodily. For when it is seen to drive the limbs on, to snatch
the body out of sleep, to change the face, to steer and turn a whole
man about, and when we see that none of this can happen without touch,
and no touch without a body, must we not admit that mind and soul are
made of bodily stuff? Besides, you can see for yourself that the mind
suffers along with the body and feels along with the body in us. If
the horrible force of a weapon, driven inside, does not reach the life
even though it has laid the bones and sinews open, still a faintness
comes on afterwards, and a welcome sinking to the ground, and once the
man is on the ground a churning that rises in his mind, and now and
then a half-hearted wish to get up. So the nature of the mind must be
bodily, since it suffers under bodily weapons and blows.

What sort of body this mind of yours has, and what it was put together
out of, I shall now go on to explain to you. To begin with, I say that
it is extremely fine and is made of extremely small bodies. That this
is so you can see by turning your mind to what follows.

Nothing is seen to happen as fast as what the mind proposes to itself
and starts by itself. So the mind stirs itself more quickly than any
of the things whose nature is out in front of our eyes. But what is as
mobile as that must be made of seeds that are exceedingly round and
exceedingly small, so that they can be moved by a push that is small.
Water is moved and flows at the tiniest push, because it is created of
shapes that roll and are small. Honey, on the other hand, has a more
settled nature; its liquid is more sluggish and its movement more
hesitant, because the whole store of its material clings together
more, and for the obvious reason: it is not made of bodies as light,
or as fine and round.

A light breath of air, hanging over a high heap of poppy seed, is
enough to make the pile pour away from the top for you; but the same
breath cannot do it at all to a pile of stones or of ears of corn. So,
just in proportion as bodies are the smallest and the lightest, they
enjoy mobility; and on the other hand, whatever things are found to be
heavier and rougher, the more stable they are. Now then: since the
nature of the mind has been found to be outstandingly mobile, it must
be made of bodies that are small and smooth and round. Once you know
this, my good friend, you will find it useful in many matters, and it
will prove to have come at a good time.

Here is one more thing that shows what the nature of the mind is: how
fine the weave it is made of, and in how small a space it would hold
itself if it could be rolled up into a ball. As soon as the untroubled
quiet of death has taken hold of a man, and the nature of his mind and
soul has gone away, you cannot make out that anything at all has been
taken from the whole body, either in looks or in weight: death gives
you everything except the feeling of life and the warm vapour. So the
whole soul must be knitted together out of exceedingly small seeds
through the veins and flesh and sinews, so much so that when the whole
of it has already left the whole body, the outermost outline of the
limbs still keeps itself intact and not a scrap of weight is missing.

It is the same when the bouquet of wine has faded, or when the sweet
breath of a perfume has escaped into the air, or when the flavour has
gone out of some body. The thing itself does not look at all smaller
to the eye for that, and nothing has been taken away from its weight;
and for the obvious reason, that many tiny seeds make up the flavours
and the smell in the whole body of a thing. So again and again: you
can know that the nature of mind and soul is created out of
exceedingly tiny seeds, since when it flies away it carries off
nothing of the weight.

All the same, we must not think this nature of theirs is a simple one.
A thin sort of breath leaves the dying, mixed with heat, and the heat
in its turn draws air along with it; and there is no heat at all which
does not have air mixed into it, since its nature is loose in texture
and a great many atoms of air must be moving about among it. So the
nature of the mind has now been found to be threefold. And yet all
these together are still not enough to create feeling, since the mind
does not accept that any of them can create the motions that carry
feeling, let alone the thoughts that a man turns over in his mind.

So some fourth nature has to be added to these as well. It is
altogether without a name. Nothing exists more mobile than it, or
finer, or made of smaller and lighter elements, and it is the first to
spread the motions that carry feeling through the frame. For it is
stirred first, being finished out of small shapes; then the heat and
the unseen power of the wind take up the motion, then the air, then
everything is set moving. The blood is shaken; then all the flesh
feels it through; last of all it is handed on to the bones and the
marrow, whether it is pleasure or the opposite burning. And pain
cannot get in this far, nor a sharp trouble seep through, without
everything being thrown into such disorder that there is no room left
for life and the parts of the soul scatter away through every pore of
the body. But usually these motions come to an end at about the
surface of the body, and that is why we are able to keep hold of our
lives.

Now, how they are mixed with each other and by what arrangement they
work: the poverty of my native language drags me away from setting
that out, much as I want to. Still, I shall touch on it, as far as I
am able to touch on it in a summary.

For the atoms of these first things run about among each other with
their own motions, so that no single one of them can be separated off,
and its power cannot act divided off in a space of its own. They are
like many powers in a single body. In the flesh of any living creature
there is generally a smell and a certain colour and a taste, and yet
out of all of these one complete bulk of body is made. In the same
way, heat and air and the unseen power of the wind, mixed together,
create one nature, along with that mobile force which shares out the
start of motion from itself to them, and it is from there that the
motion which carries feeling first rises through the flesh.

For this nature lies right down deep and underneath. There is nothing
further down than this in our body, and it is itself the soul of the
whole soul. Just as in our limbs and in our whole body the force of
the mind and the power of the soul are mixed in and hidden, because
they are created out of small and few bodies, so this nameless force,
made of tiny bodies, is hidden away, and is itself the soul, so to
speak, of the whole soul, and rules in the whole body. In the same way
wind and air and heat must work mixed together through the limbs, with
one of them sinking below the others and one standing out above them,
so that some one thing seems to be made out of them all. Otherwise
heat and wind separately, and the power of air separately, would kill
feeling off and break it up by being pulled apart.

There is also that heat in the mind which it takes on when it boils up
in anger, and the fire flashes more sharply out of the eyes. There is
also a great deal of that cold breath, the companion of dread, which
starts a shudder in the limbs and shakes the joints. And there is also
that condition of settled air, which comes with a calm chest and a
clear face. But there is more of the hot in those whose hearts are
sharp and whose angry minds boil over easily into rage. The chief
example of that kind is the violent strength of lions, who often burst
their chests with their roaring and groaning and cannot hold the waves
of anger inside them.

The cold mind of deer, on the other hand, has more wind in it, and
stirs up the icy currents through their flesh more quickly, which is
what makes the trembling motion rise in their limbs. But the nature of
cattle lives more by quiet air. The smoking torch of anger is never
put under them to prod them too far, spreading over them the shadow of
a blinding fog; and they are not stiffened through by the icy weapons
of fear. They stand between the two, the deer and the savage lions.
The human race is like this as well. However much training may polish
some men to the same finish, it still leaves behind those first traces
of the nature of each man's mind. And we must not think that these
faults can be pulled out by the roots, so that one man will not run
downhill more readily into sharp fits of anger, and another will not
be got at by fear a little sooner, and a third will not take some
things more mildly than he ought.

In a great many other matters as well, the various natures of men and
the habits that follow from them must differ. I cannot set out the
hidden causes of these now, or find as many names for the shapes of
the atoms as there are shapes, and it is from those shapes that this
variety in things arises. What I can see myself able to settle here is
this: the traces of these natures which reason cannot drive out of us
are left so very small that there is nothing to stop a man living a
life worthy of the gods.

This nature, then, is held in by the whole body, and is itself the
body's guard and the cause of its welfare; for they cling to each
other by roots they hold in common, and plainly cannot be torn apart
without both being destroyed. It is not easy to tear the smell out of
lumps of frankincense without the nature of the frankincense being
destroyed as well; and in the same way it is not easy to draw the
nature of mind and soul out of the whole body without everything
falling to pieces. Their atoms are so interwoven from the very start
that they are furnished with a life shared between them, and neither
the power of the body nor that of the mind seems able to feel
separately, on its own, without the other's force. Feeling is kindled
in us and blown into flame through our flesh by motions that the two
of them share from both sides.

Besides, the body by itself is never born, and never grows, and is not
seen to last after death. It is not with the body as it is with water,
which often gives off the heat that has been put into it, and is not
torn apart for that reason but stays intact. It is not like that, I
say. The limbs, once they have been left behind, cannot bear the
tearing away of the soul; they are destroyed root and branch, torn
apart and rotting away together. From the very beginning of their
existence, the body and the soul learn the motions of life by touching
each other, even while they are still shut away in the mother's limbs
and womb, so that they cannot be separated without ruin and harm. From
this you can see that, since the cause of their welfare is joined,
their nature must be joined as well.

As for the rest, if anyone denies that the body feels, and believes
that it is the soul, mixed through the whole body, that takes on this
motion which we call feeling, then he is fighting against facts that
are plain and true. For who will ever explain what it is for the body
to feel, if not the thing itself, which has given it to us openly and
taught it to us? "But when the soul has been sent away, the body has
no feeling anywhere in it." Of course it has none: it is losing what
was never its own property while it lived, and it loses a great deal
else besides when it is driven out of that life.

To say, further, that the eyes cannot see anything, but that the mind
looks out through them as through doors thrown open, is hard to
maintain, since the feeling of the eyes leads the other way. That
feeling drags us on and pushes us right out to the eyeballs
themselves; and it does this especially when we often cannot look at
bright things, because our eyes are blocked up by their light. That
does not happen with doors: doorways, standing open, do not take on
any work of the sort, because it is we ourselves who do the seeing.
Besides, if our eyes are standing in for doors, then it seems the mind
ought to see things better if the eyes were taken out and the
doorposts themselves lifted away.

On this subject there is one thing you can in no way take up: the
proposition which the holy judgement of Democritus lays down, that the
atoms of the body and the atoms of the mind are set alternately, one
against one, and so vary and knit our limbs together. For the elements
of the soul are much smaller than the ones our body and our flesh are
made of, and they are also fewer in number and scattered thinly
through the frame. So much so that you can guarantee this much: the
atoms of the soul keep intervals between them at least as large as the
smallest bodies which, when they are thrown at us, can stir motions of
feeling in the body.

For sometimes we do not feel dust clinging to the body, or chalk
shaken onto the limbs and settling there. We do not feel mist at
night, or the thin threads of a spider we walk into and get netted in
as we go, or its withered web falling on our head. We do not feel the
feathers of birds, or the flying down of plants, which are usually so
exceedingly light that they fall only with difficulty. We do not feel
the going of every crawling creature, or the separate footprints that
gnats and the rest set down on our body. That is how much has to be
stirred in us before the atoms of the soul, mixed in among the bodies
of our frame, feel that they have been shaken, and before they can
bang together across the gaps between them, and collide, and combine,
and leap apart again.

And the mind holds the bars of life shut more firmly, and is more the
master of life, than the force of the soul is. For without the
intelligence and the mind, not one part of the soul can stay on in the
frame for the smallest part of time. It follows easily as a companion,
and goes off into the air, and leaves the limbs cold in the chill of
death. But the man whose mind and intelligence have stayed stays
alive, however much he is mangled, with the limbs hacked away all
round him. A trunk with the soul taken away from it and the limbs
removed all round still lives, and takes in the life-giving air of the
sky; and even if it is robbed of the soul, not entirely but in great
part, it still hangs on and clings to life.

It is like an eye that has been torn about but has kept its pupil
unhurt in the middle: the living power of sight stands, provided you
do not destroy the whole ball of the eye and cut round the pupil and
leave that behind alone, because even that cannot be done without
destroying them. But if that tiny part in the middle of the eye is
eaten through, the light goes out on the spot and darkness follows,
however bright and unhurt the ball may be in every other way. It is by
a law like that that the soul and the mind are always bound together.

Now then, so that you can recognise that the minds and the light souls
of living creatures are born and are mortal, I shall go on setting out
verses worthy of your way of life, verses long hunted for and found
out with sweet labour. See that you yoke the two of them together
under one name; and when I go on to speak of the soul, say, and to
teach that it is mortal, take it that I am speaking of the mind as
well, since so far as this goes they are one thing and joined
together.

To begin with, I have taught you that the soul is fine and is made of
tiny bodies, and is created out of atoms much smaller than the clear
wet of water, or than mist, or smoke; for it goes far beyond them in
mobility and is moved by a slighter cause when it is struck, seeing
that it is moved even by the images of smoke and mist, as when we are
sunk in sleep and see altars breathing out steam and carrying smoke
aloft, for there is no doubt that these images are being brought to us
from outside. Now then: since you see that when vessels are shaken to
pieces on every side the liquid flows away and the water departs, and
since mist and smoke depart into the air, believe that the soul too is
poured out and away, and perishes much more quickly, and is broken
down more swiftly into its atoms, once it has been taken from a man's
limbs and has gone. For if the body, which stood as a kind of jar for
it, cannot hold it in when the body has been shattered by something or
thinned out by the loss of blood from the veins, how are you to
believe that it can be held in by any air, which is looser in texture
than our body is and holds things in less?

Besides, we feel that the intelligence is born along with the body,
grows along with it, and ages along with it. For just as children go
about with a weak and tender body, so a thin power of judgement goes
with it in their minds. Then, when their age has grown up into strong
powers, their judgement is greater too, and the force of their minds
is enlarged. Afterwards, when the body has been shaken by the strong
powers of time and the limbs have collapsed with their strength
blunted, the wits limp, the tongue rambles, the mind gives way;
everything fails, and everything is missing at one and the same
moment. So it is right that the whole nature of the soul should be
dissolved as well, like smoke, into the high winds of the air, since
we see it born along with the body, and grow along with it, and, as I
have taught you, crack and give way along with it, worn out by age.

Add to this that we see the body itself take on monstrous diseases and
hard pain, and the mind take on sharp cares and grief and fear in the
same way; so it is right that the mind should have its share of death
as well. What is more, in diseases of the body the mind often wanders
off the road: it goes out of its senses and talks madly, and sometimes
it is carried by a heavy lethargy down into a deep and unending sleep,
with the eyes and the head dropping. From there it does not hear the
voices, and cannot make out the faces of the people standing round
calling it back to life, with tears wetting their faces and cheeks. So
you must admit that the mind is dissolved too, since the infection of
disease works its way into it; for pain and disease are both makers of
death, as we have been well taught before now by the destruction of
many.

Then again, when the strong force of wine has got into a man and its
burning heat has spread and gone off into the veins, a heaviness of
the limbs follows; his legs get tangled as he staggers; his tongue
goes slow; his mind is soaked; his eyes swim; shouting and hiccuping
and quarrelling grow. Why are these things so, and all the rest of the
same kind that follows, if not because the fierce violence of the wine
is in the habit of throwing the soul into confusion inside the body
itself? And whatever things can be thrown into confusion and blocked
show that if a slightly harder cause worked its way in, they would
perish, robbed of any future life.

What is more, a man is often seized suddenly by the force of a
disease and drops down in front of our eyes, as though struck by
lightning. He foams at the mouth, he groans, his limbs shake, he goes
out of his senses, his sinews stretch tight, he is twisted about, he
gasps unevenly, and he wears out his limbs with his thrashing.
Naturally: the force of the disease, pulled apart through his limbs,
drives the soul about and throws it into confusion, the way the waves
on the salt sea boil up and foam under the strong power of the winds.
Then a groan is forced out of him, because his limbs are gripped by
pain, and above all because the seeds of the voice are drawn out and
carried in a mass out through the mouth, the way they are used to
going, where they have a made road.

He loses his senses because the force of mind and soul is thrown into
confusion and, as I have taught you, is divided and split apart and
thrown about in pieces by that same poison. Then, when the cause of
the disease has turned back, and the sharp fluid of the spoiled body
has retreated into its hiding places, the man gets up as though
staggering at first, and gradually comes back into all his senses and
takes his soul back again. So when mind and soul are tossed about like
this by such great diseases inside the body itself, and are torn apart
and labour in such wretched ways, why do you believe that the same
things could go on living without a body, out in the open air, among
the strong winds?

And since we see that the mind is healed like a sick body, and see
that it can be turned about by medicine, that too is a warning that
the mind lives as a mortal thing. For anyone who sets about altering
the mind, or tries to turn any other nature whatsoever, must add parts
to it, or shift the order they are in, or take at least some little
thing away from the whole. But whatever is immortal will not have its
parts moved about, or anything added to it, or the slightest thing
flowing away from it; for whatever is changed and passes out of its
own boundaries is at that moment the death of what was there before.
So the mind, whether it falls sick or is turned about by medicine,
gives out signs of being mortal, as I have taught you. That is how
completely a true fact runs to meet a false argument, and blocks the
escape of the man who is running, and convicts him of falsehood with
a two-edged refutation.

And finally, we often see a man go little by little and lose the
feeling of life limb by limb: first the toes and the nails on the feet
go blue, then the feet and the legs die, and after that the tracks of
icy death go on step by step through the rest of the limbs. Since this
nature of the soul is split up, and does not come out whole at one
time, it must be counted mortal. And if you happen to think that the
soul can draw itself inwards through the frame and gather its parts
into one place, and that this is how it takes the feeling away from
all the limbs, then the place into which such a great store of the
soul is being gathered ought to be seen to have more feeling in it.
There is no such place anywhere; so, as we said before, it is plainly
torn to pieces and scattered abroad, and therefore it perishes.

What is more, even if I were willing to concede what is false, and to
grant that the soul can be balled up inside the bodies of those who
leave the light bit by bit as they die, you would still have to admit
that the soul is mortal. And it makes no difference whether it
perishes scattered through the air, or is drawn together out of its
own parts and goes numb, since more and more feeling fails the whole
man on every side, and less and less of life is left on every side.
