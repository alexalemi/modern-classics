Book Six: Storms, Earthquakes, Magnets, and the Plague at Athens
(Part 3 of 3)

Well water, next, turns colder in summer. The reason is that the earth
is thinned out by the heat and lets go into the air whatever seeds of
heat it happens to have of its own. So the more the earth is worn out
and emptied of its heat, the colder the water hidden inside it becomes.
When, on the other hand, the whole earth is pressed together by cold and
packs tight and, so to speak, sets hard, then plainly, in packing itself
together, it squeezes out into the wells whatever heat it is carrying.

There is said to be a spring at the shrine of Ammon, out in the Libyan
desert, which is cold in the daylight and hot in the night-time. People
marvel at this spring far too much. They think it comes to the boil down
under the earth, from the fierce sun, once night has covered the lands
with its terrifying darkness. That is a very long way from true
reasoning. When the sun, handling the naked body of the water from
above, has not been able to make it hot even on its upper side, and the
light up there enjoys such burning heat, how could the sun down below
cook the water through so thick a body of earth and warm it with hot
vapour? Especially when it can hardly work its heat in through the walls
of a house with all its burning rays. What is the explanation, then?
Obviously this. The ground round about that spring is more porous than
the rest of the earth, and there are many seeds of fire close to the
body of the water. So when night buries the land under its dew-bearing
waves, the earth cools deep down at once and packs together. In this
way, exactly as if a hand were closing on it, it squeezes into the
spring whatever seeds of fire it holds, and those are what make the
touch and the steam of the water hot. Then, when the sun comes up and
pushes the earth apart with its rays and thins it out, mixing warm
vapour into it, the atoms of fire go back to their old homes and all the
heat of the water withdraws into the earth. That is why the spring turns
cold in the daylight. Besides, the wet of the water is shaken about by
the rays of the sun and thinned out towards the light by the trembling
heat, and for that reason it lets go whatever seeds of fire it holds, in
the same way that it often lets go the frost it holds inside itself,
thawing the ice and loosening the knots.

There is also a cold spring above which a piece of tow, held over it,
throws out a flame and catches fire on the spot; and in the same way a
pine torch lights up and burns as it floats over the water, driven
wherever the breeze pushes it. No wonder. There are a great many seeds
of heat in the water, and bodies of fire must rise from the earth
itself, from deep down, all through the spring, and breathe out and pass
into the air at the same time — not in such numbers, though, that the
spring itself could become hot. Besides, some force makes them burst out
through the water, scattered as they are, and suddenly gather together
above it. It is like the spring out in the sea at Aradus, off the Syrian
coast, which bubbles up fresh water and pushes the salt waves aside all
round itself; and in many other places the open sea offers this timely
help to thirsty sailors, vomiting fresh water up in the middle of the
salt. So in the same way those seeds can burst out through that other
spring and bubble out of it; and when they come together in the tow, or
stick in the body of the torch, they easily blaze up at once, because
tow and torches also hold many seeds of fire in themselves. Have you
never seen that if you bring a wick that has just gone out near a lamp
at night, it catches fire before it has touched the flame — and that a
torch does the same? A great many other things, too, blaze up at a
distance, touched by the heat alone, before the fire soaks into them
close up. So we have to suppose that this is what happens at that spring
as well.

Now for what is left. I will begin to explain by what law of nature it
comes about that this stone can draw iron. The Greeks call it the
magnet, from the name of its homeland, because it is found in the
territory of the Magnesians. People marvel at this stone, since it will
often make a chain out of rings hanging from itself. Sometimes you can
see five of them, or more, hanging in a row and swinging in the light
air, one hanging from another and clinging on underneath it, each of
them learning from the one above the power of the stone and its bonds.
That is how thoroughly its force soaks all the way down.

In matters of this kind a great deal has to be settled before you can
give an account of the thing itself, and the approach has to be made by
very long ways round. All the more, then, I ask you for attentive ears
and an attentive mind.

To begin with, from all the things we see there must be a perpetual flow
of bodies streaming off, sent out and scattered about, bodies which
strike the eyes and provoke sight. From certain things smells flow
perpetually: coolness from rivers, heat from the sun, spray from the
waves of the sea, which eats away the walls along the shore. Sounds of
every kind never stop seeping through the air. And a wet salt taste
often comes into the mouth when we walk about beside the sea, and when
we watch wormwood being mixed with water the bitterness touches us. So
thoroughly does everything stream off from every thing and get sent out
on all sides in every direction, with no delay and no rest in the flow
allowed, since we are perpetually feeling, and are always able to see
everything and to smell it and to hear it sound.

Now I will go back over how porous a body all things have, and remind
you of it; it is clear in my first book as well. Although knowing this
matters for many things, it matters first of all for the very thing I am
setting out to discuss: I have to establish that there is nothing to
hand except body mixed with void. To begin with, in caves the rocks
overhead sweat with moisture and drip with running drops. Sweat likewise
runs out of the whole of our body. The beard grows, and hairs all over
our limbs and joints. Food is distributed into the veins, and increases
and feeds even the outermost parts of the body, right down to the little
nails. We feel cold and hot vapour pass through bronze; we feel them
pass through gold as well, and through silver, when we are holding full
cups. Voices fly through the stone partitions of houses; smell seeps
through, and cold, and the heat of fire, which is used to working its
way even through the strength of iron. And where the breastplate of the
sky closes round us, a diseased force gets in at the same time, when it
slips in from outside; and storms that gather on land and in the sky
withdraw again, as you would expect, into the sky and the lands, since
there is nothing that is not woven together out of porous body.

Add to this that not all the bodies thrown off by things are endowed
with the same effect on the senses, or fitted to all things in the same
way. To begin with, the sun bakes the earth and makes it dry, but it
melts ice, and forces the deep snows piled high on the high mountains to
waste away under its rays; and wax, put in the heat of it, turns liquid.
Fire likewise makes bronze liquid and dissolves gold, but it shrivels
hides and meat and draws them together. Water, again, hardens iron
straight from the fire, but softens hides and meat that heat has
hardened. The wild olive delights the bearded she-goats as much as if it
really ran with ambrosia and were steeped in nectar, and yet there is
nothing that grows in leaf more bitter than that sour foliage is for a
man. A pig runs away from marjoram oil and is afraid of every perfume;
to bristly pigs they are a fierce poison, though to us they sometimes
seem to bring us back to life. On the other hand, though filth is the
foulest possible dirt to us, that same filth seems so delightful to pigs
that they roll about in it all over and can never have enough of it.

There is one more point left before I go on to speak about the thing
itself, and it seems it should be said first. Since many different pores
are assigned to different things, they must be endowed with natures
unlike one another, and each must have its own character and its own
passages. There are, after all, different senses in living creatures,
and each of them takes in its own particular object. We see that sound
gets in by one way, and taste from juices by another, and the smell of a
scent by another. Plainly it is the nature of the passages that forces
this to happen, varying in many ways, as I showed a little earlier.
Besides, one thing seems to seep through rock, and another through wood,
and another to pass through gold, and another to make its way out
through silver and through glass; for through one path the look of
things seems to flow, and through another heat travels, and one thing
passes through the same paths faster than another. Again, it is the
nature of the passages that forces this to happen, varying in many ways,
as I showed a little earlier, because the natures and the weaves of
things are unlike each other.

So now that all this has been well established and settled and laid out
ready for us, what is left will easily be explained from it, and the
whole cause will lie open which draws on the strength of iron.

To begin with, a great many seeds must flow out of this stone, a tide of
them, which knocks the air apart with blows, all the air lying between
the stone and the iron. When this space is emptied out and a great deal
of the room in the middle is made void, at once the atoms of the iron
slip forward and fall into the vacuum, joined together, and the result
is that the ring itself follows and travels there with its whole body.
And there is nothing whose atoms are more entangled with one another and
hold together in a tighter weave than the nature of tough iron with its
cold roughness. So it is less surprising, given what I said, that when a
large number of bodies rise out of the iron and are carried into the
vacuum, the ring itself cannot help following. And follow it does, and
keeps on following, until it has reached the stone itself and clung to
it by fastenings you cannot see.

The same thing happens in every direction. Wherever a place is made
void, whether from the side or from above, the neighbouring bodies are
carried into the vacuum immediately; for they are set moving by blows
from elsewhere and cannot rise up into the air by themselves, of their
own accord.

There is something else besides which makes this easier, another motion
that helps the business along. As soon as the air in front of the ring
has been made thinner and the place there emptied and made void, at once
all the air standing behind the ring carries it forward, so to speak,
and drives it from the rear. The air placed round things is always
beating on them; but at a time like this it drives the iron forward,
because on one side there is empty space which takes the iron into it.
This air I am reminding you of works its way subtly in through the
crowded pores of the iron to its small parts, and shoves it and drives
it, as the wind shoves a ship and its sails. And finally all things must
hold air in their body, since they are made of porous body, and air is
placed round everything and set up against it. So this air shut away
deep inside the iron is always being tossed about in restless motion,
and beyond doubt it beats on the ring and stirs it from the inside; and
plainly it is carried in the same direction the ring has already once
plunged in, since it has already made its effort into the empty part.

It also happens sometimes that the nature of iron shrinks back from this
stone, since it is used to fleeing and following by turns. I have even
seen Samothracian iron rings leap up, and iron filings go wild inside
bronze bowls, when this magnet stone was put underneath them: the iron
seems so eager to run away from the rock. Such a quarrel as that is set
up by the bronze put in between, and for this reason, obviously. When
the tide of the bronze has taken possession first and holds the open
passages of the iron, the tide of the stone comes afterwards, and finds
everything full in the iron, and no longer has anywhere to swim through
as it had before. So it is forced to strike against the weave of the
iron and beat on it with its current; and in this way it spits the iron
away from itself and drives it off through the bronze, when without the
bronze it would often be sucking it back.

Do not be surprised, in all this, that the tide from this stone has no
power to move other things in the same way. Some of them stand still
because they rely on their weight, gold for one; and others cannot be
pushed anywhere at all because they are made of such porous body that
the tide flies right through them and never touches them, and wood seems
to be a thing of that kind. Iron, then, stands between the two, and once
it has taken in a few little bodies of bronze it is driven along by the
streams of the Magnesian stone.

And yet these things are not so foreign to everything else that I have
too few examples of the kind to draw on, cases I could mention of things
fitted to each other and to nothing else. You see, first of all, that
stones can be cemented by lime and by nothing else. Wood is joined to
wood by bull's glue alone, so firmly that the boards more often crack
open along a weak vein in the grain than those bonds of bull's glue can
loosen their hold. Juices born of the vine are bold enough to mix with
spring water, though heavy pitch cannot, and neither can light olive
oil. The purple colour of the shellfish joins with the body of wool
alone, so that it can never be parted from it again — not if you set to
work to restore the wool with Neptune's flood, not if the whole sea
wanted to wash it out with all its waves. And in the end, is there not
one substance and one only that binds gold to gold, and is it not by
white tin that bronze is joined to bronze? How many more examples one
could find! And what of it? You have no need of such long ways round
anywhere, and it is not right for me to spend so much labour here.
Better to take in many things briefly, in a few words. Where the
textures of two things have fallen out so as to match each other, so
that the hollows of the one fit the solid parts of the other, and the
hollows of that one fit the solid parts of this, that is the best joint
of all. There are also things that can be held coupled together, linked
as if by rings and hooks; and that seems to be more what happens with
this stone and with iron.

Now I will set out what the principle of diseases is, and where the
diseased force can rise from, suddenly, to blow up a deadly disaster for
the human race and for the herds of cattle. First, I taught above that
there are seeds of many things which are life-giving to us, and that on
the other hand many must be flying about which bring disease and death.
When these have happened to gather together by chance and have disturbed
the sky, the air becomes diseased. And all that force of diseases and
that plague either comes from outside, down through the sky, like clouds
and mists, or often gathers and rises up out of the earth itself, when
the earth has grown damp and caught a rot, struck by unseasonable rains
and suns.

Do you not see, too, that people who arrive far from their own country
and home are attacked by the strangeness of the sky and the water, just
because things differ so widely from place to place? What difference do
we suppose there is between the sky in Britain and the sky in Egypt,
where the axis of the world goes lame, or between the sky in Pontus and
the sky at Gades, and on to the black generations of men whose colour
has been baked into them? Just as we see these four skies differing from
one another under the four winds and the four quarters of heaven, so the
colour and the faces of men are seen to differ widely, and diseases of
fixed kinds to take hold of the races, kind by kind. There is elephant
disease, which is bred in the middle of Egypt beside the streams of the
Nile and nowhere else besides. In Attica it is the feet that are
attacked, and in Achaean country the eyes. And so one place is hostile
to one part of the body and another to another; it is the varying air
that arranges it.

So when a stretch of sky that happens to be foreign to us begins to
stir, and hostile air begins to crawl, it creeps along little by little
like mist and cloud, and throws everything it walks through into
confusion and forces it to change; and the result is that when it comes
at last into our own sky, it corrupts that too, and makes it like itself
and foreign to us. So this new disaster and this plague suddenly either
falls into the water, or settles into the crops themselves, or into the
other foods of men and the fodder of cattle; or else the force stays
hanging in the air itself, and when we draw the mixed air in as we
breathe, we cannot help sucking that in along with it, into our body. In
much the same way plague often comes on cattle, and sickness on the slow
bleating sheep. And it makes no difference whether we travel into places
that are against us and change the cloak of sky over us, or whether
nature brings a corrupted sky to us of its own accord, or something we
are not used to using, which can attack us as soon as it has arrived.

A principle of disease like that, a deadly tide like that, once made the
fields deadly in the country of Cecrops, emptied the roads and drained
the city of its citizens. It came from deep inland, rising in the
territory of Egypt, and after crossing a great stretch of air and the
floating plains of the sea it settled at last on the whole people of
Pandion. Then they were handed over to disease and death in crowds.

To begin with, they went about with their heads on fire with heat, and
both eyes red with a glare spread over them. The throat, black inside,
sweated blood; the pathway of the voice was blocked and closed up with
ulcers; and the tongue, the interpreter of the mind, ran with gore,
weakened by the suffering, heavy to move, rough to the touch. Then, when
the diseased force had come down through the throat and filled the chest
and flowed together into the sick man's grieving heart, then indeed all
the bars of life began to give way. The breath rolled a foul smell out
of the mouth, the way rotting corpses thrown out into the open stink.
And then the whole strength of the mind, and the whole body, went slack,
on the very threshold of death. And with those unbearable torments there
went a constant anxious anguish, a companion that never left, and
complaining mixed with groans; and a frequent retching, through the
night and often through the day as well, kept dragging at their muscles
and cramping their limbs, breaking down men who were worn out already,
wearing them out further.

And yet you could not have seen the top surface of anyone's body burning
with any great heat. It offered a lukewarm feeling to the hands instead,
and at the same time the whole body was red with ulcers, as if branded,
the way it is when the holy fire is spreading through the limbs. But the
inner parts of the man were burning right down to the bones; a flame was
burning in the stomach as it burns inside a furnace. There was nothing
you could put on their limbs light enough and thin enough to do them any
good; they wanted wind and cold, always. Some threw their limbs, on fire
with the disease, into icy rivers, hurling their naked bodies into the
water.

Many fell headlong, deep down into the water of the wells, arriving with
their mouths already wide open. An unquenchable thirst, drowning their
parched bodies, made a great downpour seem no more than a few drops.
There was no rest from the suffering. Their bodies lay worn out.
Medicine muttered, silent with fear, since again and again they rolled
their burning eyes wide open, with no share in sleep.

Many other signs of death were given besides. The intelligence
disordered in grief and fear; the sad brow; the wild and staring face;
ears full of noises and never quiet; breathing quick, or huge and coming
rarely; a shining wet of sweat down the neck; thin small gobs of
spittle, touched with the colour of saffron and salt, brought up through
the throat with difficulty by a hoarse cough. In the hands the sinews
went on contracting and the joints shaking, and from the feet the cold
came up, little by little; it never failed. Then, at the very last hour,
the nostrils pinched, the tip of the nose thin and pointed, the eyes
sunken, the temples hollow, the skin cold and hard on the face, the
mouth lying open in a grin, the skin of the forehead stretched tight.
And not long afterwards their limbs lay stiff in death. Generally on the
eighth shining light of the sun, or at most on the ninth flame of his
torch, they gave up their lives.

And if any of them had escaped the funeral rites of death, as some did,
a wasting away and death still waited for him later, from foul ulcers
and a black discharge of the bowels; or often corrupted blood ran out of
his stuffed nostrils, along with a bad pain in the head. The whole
strength of the man and his body flowed away that way.

And whoever had got past that sharp flow of foul blood, the disease went
into his sinews all the same, and into his joints, and into the sexual
parts of the body themselves. Some, in their heavy fear of the threshold
of death, went on living with their manhood taken off by the knife; and
some stayed alive without hands and feet, and some lost their eyes. That
is how fiercely the fear of death had come over them. And some were
seized by a forgetting of everything there is, so that they could not
even recognise themselves.

And though bodies lay unburied on the ground, body heaped on body, still
the race of birds and beasts either sprang far away to get clear of the
sharp stench, or, if they had tasted, went slack with death close by. In
fact in those days hardly any bird appeared at all, and the grim
generations of wild animals did not come out of the woods. Most of them
went slack with the disease and died. Above all the faithful strength of
dogs, stretched out in every street, laid down its life with difficulty,
since the diseased force was wrenching life out of their limbs. Funerals
were huge and unaccompanied, and people raced to have them hurried
through. And no sure principle of treatment was found that worked for
everybody: what had given one man the power to go on rolling the
life-giving breath of air in his mouth and to look at the regions of the
sky was the destruction of others, and prepared their death.

The one thing in all this that was most to be pitied and most wretched
was this. When a man saw that he was caught in the disease, and that he
was condemned to death, he would lose heart and lie there with a
grieving heart, and, looking ahead at his own funeral, he would let go
of his life on the spot. For at no time did the contagion of the greedy
disease stop passing from one to another and taking them, as it passes
through woolly flocks and horned cattle; and that above all was what
piled funeral on funeral. Anyone who kept away from visiting his own
sick, too greedy for life and too afraid of death, was punished a little
later by a shameful and evil death, deserted, with no help, killed by
the neglect he had shown. But those who had stayed at hand died of the
contagion, and of the labour that a sense of shame forced them to take
on, and the coaxing voice of the exhausted mixed with the voice of
complaint. So the best men were the ones who kept meeting that kind of
death.

By now the shepherds and every herdsman, and the sturdy driver of the
curved plough as well, were going slack; and their bodies lay crammed
deep in their huts, handed over to death by poverty and disease.
Sometimes you could have seen the lifeless bodies of parents lying on
lifeless children, and, the other way round, sons and daughters giving
up their lives on the bodies of their mothers and fathers.

And not the smallest part of that misery flowed into the city from the
fields, brought in by the sickening crowd of country people gathering
from every quarter, diseased. They filled all the places and all the
buildings, and packed together in the heat like that they were piled up
in heaps by death all the faster. Many bodies, thrown down by thirst and
rolled along the road, lay stretched out beside the water fountains, the
breath cut off by too much sweetness of water. And everywhere through
the open places of the people and along the streets you could see limbs
going slack on half-dead bodies, dreadful with filth and covered over
with rags, dying of the sheer dirt of the body, with nothing but skin
over the bones, nearly buried already under foul ulcers and squalor.

Death had filled all the holy shrines of the gods with lifeless bodies,
and all the temples of the heavenly ones were left loaded everywhere
with corpses — places which the keepers of the shrines had crammed with
guests. For by now neither the religion of the gods nor their powers
counted for much: the grief at hand was too strong for that. And the old
custom of burial no longer stayed in the city, the custom this people
had always been used to being buried by. They were all in a panic and
thrown into confusion, and each of them, grieving, buried his own dead
as circumstances allowed. And sudden need and rough poverty drove them
to many things. With a great shouting they would lay their own relatives
on funeral pyres built for other people, and put torches underneath, and
often fought over it and spilled a great deal of blood, rather than let
the bodies be abandoned — struggling to bury their own dead in other
people's pyres. Worn out with tears and mourning they would go home; and
then a good many of them took to their beds from grief. And nobody could
be found, at a time like that, whom disease or death or grief did not
touch.
