Thomas More published this book in Latin in 1516, when he was a London lawyer and scholar in his late thirties with a European reputation and a wide correspondence. Erasmus was a close friend and helped see it into print. More went on to become Lord Chancellor of England, resigned the office in 1532, and was beheaded in 1535 for refusing to accept Henry VIII as head of the church in England.

It comes in two books. The first is a conversation in a garden in Antwerp between More himself, who appears as a character in his own book, his friend Peter Giles, who was a real man and the town clerk there, and a traveller named Raphael Hythloday, who is not real and who says he sailed with Amerigo Vespucci and was left behind on the far side of the Atlantic. The second book is Hythloday talking almost without interruption: the geography, laws, work, meals, marriages, religion and warfare of an island commonwealth he says he lived in for five years.

The names are jokes and they are meant to be caught. Utopia is Greek for no place. Hythloday means something like dispenser of nonsense. The capital, Amaurot, is the dim city; the river running through it, the Anyder, is the no-water. This edition glosses them where they first appear, because More's readers got the joke for free.

The jokes do not make Book One a joke. Its attack on England is furious and entirely literal: landlords fencing common fields for sheep pasture and driving the farmers off, then the same farmers hanged as thieves when they steal to eat; kings who would rather conquer new territory than govern what they have; counsellors who tell those kings what they want to hear. More was writing about the country he lived in and he names the practices.

Readers who arrive expecting a blueprint find something stranger. Utopia has no private property, a six-hour working day, and gold so despised that it is made into chamber pots and the chains of slaves. It also has slaves, internal passports, punishment for sex before marriage, no privacy of any kind, and priests who visit the incurably sick to argue that they should stop eating. How much of this More meant, and how much he expected his readers to recoil, is genuinely unsettled and has been argued about for five centuries. Nothing here tries to settle it. His last sentence says there is much in Utopia that he would rather wish than hope to see in Europe, and that is as far as he goes.

The English here follows Gilbert Burnet's 1684 translation of the Latin rather than the Latin itself. Both books are complete.
