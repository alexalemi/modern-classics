"Professor Hoffmann" was the pen name of Angelo John Lewis (1839–1919), a London barrister who was also a skilled amateur conjurer. Modern Magic was first published in London in 1876 and was far more complete than any book on conjuring that had appeared in English before it. It explained, trick by trick and with drawings, how the professional magicians of the day did what they did. For a generation afterwards it was the book from which English-speaking magicians learned their art. He later translated the writings of the French conjurer Robert-Houdin, whom this book quotes and admires.

It is organised by the object in the conjurer's hand. After the equipment (the wand, the magician's table, the magician's dress) come cards, first the principles of sleight of hand and then the tricks, from those needing no skill to those needing prepared packs and apparatus. Then coins, watches, rings, handkerchiefs, dominoes and dice, the cups and balls, and hat tricks, and finally the large stage illusions. Every sleight is described step by step, with drawings of the hands. Every piece of apparatus is drawn, often in cut-away views that show the false bottom or the hidden spring. A concluding chapter offers advice on performing.

This edition includes the appendix bound into the American edition, "Ancient and Modern Magic" by Arprey Vere. It begins with the celebrated automata of the past, such as von Kempelen's chess player, and goes on to the specialties of Maskelyne and Cooke at the Egyptian Hall in London, among them their card-playing automaton "Psycho." It ends with chapters on clairvoyance, spiritualism and parlour magic.

A few things will read oddly. Money is English: half-crowns, florins, shillings and half-pence. Many tricks rely on a servant or assistant behind the scenes, as a Victorian stage performance did. Much of the apparatus could be bought ready-made, and Hoffmann often notes that a piece may be had at the conjuring depôts.

This is a restored edition, and the words are unchanged. The text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the American edition, compared word by word with scans of London printings of 1877 and 1885. In the one place where both agree against it, the print is followed. The transcription had renumbered the appendix's figures 1 to 4 as 321 to 324, and here they carry their printed numbers again. Every drawing is kept, the decorative head- and tailpieces included, and each now has a description of what it shows, so that the tricks can be followed without the pictures; the descriptions also serve as alt text. The edition also adds modern typesetting, an ebook, and this introduction.
