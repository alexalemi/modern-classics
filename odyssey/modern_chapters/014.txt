Book Fourteen

But Odysseus went up from the harbour by the rough path, over the woodland and through the heights, to the place where Athena had shown him he would find the good swineherd, who looked after his property better than any of the slaves noble Odysseus had got.

He found him sitting in the front porch of his house, where his yard was built high in a place with a wide view, a great fine yard with open ground round it. The swineherd had built it himself for the pigs of his master, who was gone, without the knowledge of his mistress or of the old man Laertes. He had built it with huge stones and set a coping of thorn on it. Outside he had driven stakes the whole length, this way and that, huge stakes set close together, which he had made by splitting an oak down to the black core. And inside the yard he had made twelve sties close to one another as beds for the pigs, and fifty wallowing sows were penned in each of them, breeding females; but the boars slept outside. They were far fewer in number, because the godlike suitors were feasting on them and thinning them out, since the swineherd always sent in the best of all the fatted hogs, which came to three hundred and sixty. And four dogs, savage as wild beasts, always slept beside them, which the swineherd had reared, a leader of men.

He was fitting boots round his feet, cutting an ox-hide of good colour, while the others had gone off, three of them, one here and one there, with the droves of pigs; and the fourth he had sent to the city to drive a boar for the insolent suitors, because he had to, so that they could kill it and satisfy themselves with meat.

Suddenly the baying hounds caught sight of Odysseus and rushed at him barking loudly; but Odysseus sat down, being cunning, and the staff dropped out of his hand. And then he would have suffered a cruel hurt on his own farm, but the swineherd came running after them with quick steps and hurried out through the gateway, and the hide fell out of his hand. He shouted at the dogs and drove them this way and that with a shower of stones, and said to his master:

"Old man, the dogs nearly tore you to pieces all of a sudden, and you would have poured reproach on me. And the gods have given me other griefs and sorrows already. Here I sit mourning and grieving for a godlike master, and rearing fat pigs for other men to eat, while he, it may be, wants for food and wanders over the land and the city of men of a strange language, if he is still alive at all and sees the light of the sun. But come with me, old man; let us go to the hut, so that when you have satisfied yourself with food and wine you can tell me where you are from and all the troubles you have gone through."

With that the good swineherd led him to the hut and brought him in and sat him down, spreading thick brushwood underneath and laying the skin of a shaggy wild goat over it, big and hairy, the one he slept on himself. Odysseus was glad that he gave him such a welcome, and said to him:

"Stranger, may Zeus and the other immortal gods grant you whatever you want most, since you have given me such a ready welcome."

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "Stranger, it would not be right for me to slight a stranger, not even if one came who was worse off than you are. All strangers and beggars come from Zeus, and a gift is welcome from people like us even when it is small; because this is the lot of slaves, always in fear when the masters who hold sway over them are young masters, as ours are. For the gods have blocked the homecoming of the man who would have loved me with real kindness and given me possessions of my own, a house and a bit of land and a wife whom many wanted — the kind of thing a kind master gives to a slave who has laboured hard for him, and whose work a god has made prosper, as this work of mine prospers, which I give my mind to. So my master would have rewarded me richly if he had grown old here at home. But he died — and I wish all the family of Helen had died in utter ruin, since she loosened the knees of so many fighting men. For he too went to Ilios, famous for horses, to win recompense for Agamemnon, so that he could fight the Trojans."

With that he quickly bound his tunic up with his belt and went off to the sties where the tribes of pigs were penned. He chose two out of them and brought them in and killed them both, and singed them and cut them up and spitted them. Then, when he had roasted it all, he brought it and set it in front of Odysseus, hot on the spits, and sprinkled white barley meal over it. Then he mixed honey-sweet wine in a bowl of ivy wood, and sat down opposite Odysseus himself, and invited him to eat.

"Eat now, stranger, the kind of food slaves have to offer, the meat of young pigs; the fatted hogs are eaten by the suitors, who take no thought in their hearts of the anger of the gods, and have no pity. The blessed gods do not love reckless doings; they honour justice and the righteous deeds of men. Even cruel enemies who set foot on another people's land, when Zeus gives them plunder and they fill their ships and go home — even on the hearts of those a great fear of the gods' anger falls. But these men here know something, and have heard some voice of a god about my master's pitiful death, seeing that they will not court righteously or go back to their own homes, but waste our substance at their ease in an insolent way, and there is no sparing. Every day and night that comes from Zeus they sacrifice, and not one victim or two either; and they draw the wine off and waste it insolently.

"His substance was great past telling. No lord has so much, either on the dark mainland or in Ithaca itself; not twenty men together have wealth as great. I will tell you the reckoning of it. Twelve herds of cattle he has on the mainland; as many flocks of sheep; as many droves of pigs; as many packed herds of goats, pastured by herdsmen, both foreigners and men of his own. And here too roving herds of goats graze on the edges of the island, eleven of them, and trusty men keep watch over them. And each of these men drives up one of his flock for the suitors every day, whichever of the fatted goats seems the best to him. And I guard and keep these pigs, and choose out the best of the boars carefully and send them in."

So he spoke, and Odysseus ate the meat eagerly and drank the wine, greedily, in silence, and was sowing the seeds of trouble for the suitors. But when he had dined and satisfied himself with food, the swineherd filled the bowl he drank from himself and gave it him brim full of wine, and he took it and was glad at heart; and he spoke to him with winged words:

"Friend, who was it who bought you with his wealth, a man so very rich and powerful, as you tell it? You said he died to win recompense for Agamemnon; tell me, in case I happen to know a man like that. Zeus and the other immortal gods know whether I have seen him and could bring you news; I have wandered a long way."

Then the swineherd, a leader of men, answered him: "Old man, no wanderer who came here bringing news of him could persuade his wife and his dear son. Vagabonds who need entertainment lie at random, and have no wish to speak the truth. Anyone who comes to the land of Ithaca in his wanderings goes to my mistress and tells her a lying tale. And she receives him kindly and entertains him and questions him about everything, and the tears fall from her eyelids as she weeps, the way a woman does when her husband has died far away. And you would soon make up a story yourself, old man, if somebody would give you a cloak and a tunic for clothes. But as for him, by now the dogs and the swift birds have most likely torn the flesh off his bones and his spirit has left him; or the fishes have eaten him in the sea, and his bones lie there on the shore wrapped in deep sand. So he died out there, and grief is appointed for his friends in the days to come, for all of them, and for me most of all. I shall never find so kind a master again, however far I go, not if I came back to the house of my father and mother, where I was born in the first place and they brought me up themselves. And it is not for them that I mourn so much now, eager as I am to see them with my eyes and to be in my own country; it is the longing for Odysseus, who is gone, that takes hold of me. I speak his name with awe, stranger, absent as he is, because he loved me greatly and cared for me at heart; but I call him my lord and my beloved, for all that he is not here."

Then much-enduring noble Odysseus answered him: "Friend, since you deny it altogether and say he will never come again, and your heart is always unbelieving, I will tell you — not at random, but with an oath — that Odysseus is coming back. And let me have a reward for bringing good news as soon as he comes and reaches his home: dress me in a cloak and a tunic, good clothes. Before that I will accept nothing, however badly off I am; because that man is as hateful to me as the gates of Hades who gives way to the pressure of poverty and tells a lying tale. Let Zeus be my witness above all gods, and this hospitable table, and the hearth of noble Odysseus that I have come to, that everything will happen exactly as I tell you. In the course of this very same year Odysseus will come here, as the old moon wanes and the new appears. He will come back, and take his revenge on all those who dishonour his wife and his glorious son here."

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "Old man, I do not think I shall be paying you this reward for good news, and Odysseus will never come home. Drink in peace, and let us turn our minds to something else, and do not bring this back to my mind; because the heart in my breast is grieved whenever anyone mentions my good master. As for your oath, let it be; but I wish Odysseus would come, as I want him to, and Penelope, and the old man Laertes, and godlike Telemachus.

"But now it is for his son that I grieve without stopping, for Telemachus, whom Odysseus fathered. When the gods had made him grow like a sapling, and I thought he would be no worse a man than his dear father, glorious in shape and looks, then one of the immortals damaged the good sense inside him, or some man did, and he went off to sacred Pylos after news of his father. And now the lordly suitors lie in wait for him on his way home, so that the line of godlike Arceisius may die out of Ithaca and leave no name. But let him be; he may be taken or he may get away, and the son of Cronos may stretch out his hand to guard him.

"But come, old man, tell me your own sorrows, and tell me the truth, so that I know for certain. Who are you among men, and where from? Where is your city, and where are your parents? What sort of ship did you come on, and how did sailors bring you to Ithaca? Who did they say they were? Because you certainly did not get here on foot."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "Then I will tell you all of it plainly. I wish the two of us had food and sweet wine for a good while, to feast on quietly here in your hut, with others going about their work; then I could easily talk for a whole year and still not finish the tale of the sorrows of my spirit, all the labours I have gone through by the will of the gods.

"I declare that I come by descent from broad Crete, the son of a wealthy man. And a great many other sons were born and brought up in his halls, true sons of a lawful wife; but the mother who bore me was bought, a concubine. Yet Castor, son of Hylax, whom I say I am sprung from, honoured me the same as his true-born sons. He was honoured like a god among the Cretans in that country in those days, for his good estate and his wealth and his glorious sons. But the fates of death carried him off to the house of Hades, and his proud sons divided his substance among them and cast lots for it. To me they gave a very small portion, and allotted me a dwelling. But I took a wife from a house with wide possessions, winning her by my own courage; because I was no weakling and no coward in a fight. All that strength is gone now; and yet even so, seeing the stubble, I think you can judge what the grain was; because troubles surround me in full measure.

"But in those days Ares and Athena gave me courage, and strength that breaks the ranks of men; and whenever I picked the best fighters for an ambush, sowing the seeds of trouble for the enemy, my proud spirit never foresaw death, but I would always leap out far ahead of the others and kill with my spear whichever of the enemy gave way in flight in front of me. That was the kind of man I was in war; but work in the fields was never to my liking, or the care of a household that rears fine children. Oared ships were always dear to me, and wars, and polished spears, and arrows — grievous things, which other men shudder at. But those were the things dear to me, which a god must have put in my heart; different men take joy in different work. Before the sons of the Achaeans set foot on the land of Troy I had nine times led fighting men and fast-going ships against foreign peoples, and great plunder had always fallen to me. I would choose what pleased my mind out of it, and got a great deal more afterwards by lot. So my house grew rich at once, and after that I became a man feared and honoured among the Cretans.

"But when Zeus, whose voice carries far, contrived that hateful journey which loosened the knees of so many fighting men, then they told me and glorious Idomeneus to lead the ships to Ilios, and there was no way of refusing, because the voice of the people pressed hard on us. There for nine years we sons of the Achaeans made war, and in the tenth we sacked the city of Priam and set out for home in our ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans.

"But for me, wretch that I am, Zeus the counsellor contrived trouble. I stayed only a month, taking joy in my children and my wedded wife and my wealth; and then my spirit told me to sail to Egypt with my godlike companions, when I had fitted my ships out carefully. I fitted out nine ships, and the crews gathered quickly. For six days my trusty companions feasted, and I gave them a great many victims so that they could sacrifice to the gods and prepare a feast for themselves; and on the seventh we embarked and set sail from broad Crete, with the north wind blowing fresh and fair, and ran on as easily as if we were going downstream. No harm came to any of my ships, but we sat there free of injury and of sickness, and the wind and the helmsmen guided the ships.

"On the fifth day we came to the fair-flowing Aegyptus, and I moored my curved ships in the river Aegyptus. Then I told my trusty companions to stay there by the ships and guard the ships, and I sent scouts out to go to places of outlook. But my companions gave way to their own wantonness, and, led on by their own strength, set about wasting the fine fields of the men of Egypt straight away; and they carried off the women and the little children and killed the men; and the cry went up to the city quickly.

"Then, hearing the shouting, the people came out at daybreak, and the whole plain was filled with foot-soldiers and chariots and the flashing of bronze. And Zeus, who hurls the thunderbolt, threw an evil panic on my companions, and not one of them had the courage to hold his ground and face the enemy; trouble surrounded us on every side. So they killed a great many of us with the sharp bronze, and led others up to their city alive, to work for them because they had to. But Zeus himself put this thought in my heart — I wish I had died and met my fate there in Egypt instead, because sorrow was still waiting to welcome me. Straight away I took my well-made helmet off my head, and the shield off my shoulders, and let the spear fall out of my hand, and went towards the king's chariot horses. I took hold of his knees and kissed them, and he saved me and took pity on me, and set me in his chariot and took me home weeping. A great many of them rushed at me with their ash spears, eager to kill me, because they were furious; but he kept them off, and had regard for the anger of Zeus, the stranger's god, who more than anyone is indignant at evil deeds.

"There I stayed seven years, and gathered a great deal of wealth among the Egyptians, because everyone gave me gifts. But when the eighth year came round in its circle, then a man of Phoenicia came, well versed in guile, a greedy rogue who had already done a great deal of harm among men. He talked me round with his cunning and took me with him, until we reached Phoenicia, where his house and his possessions were. There I stayed with him for a whole year. But when at last the months and the days were coming to fulfilment, as the year rolled round and the seasons came on, he put me on a seafaring ship bound for Libya, giving me lying advice that I should carry a cargo with him, but in truth so that when we got there he could sell me and get a huge price.

"So I went aboard the ship with him, suspecting his guile but having no choice. And she ran before the north wind, blowing fresh and fair, on a mid-sea course to windward of Crete; and Zeus contrived destruction for the men. But when we had left Crete and no other land appeared, but only sky and sea, then the son of Cronos set a black cloud over the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it. And Zeus thundered and hurled his bolt at the ship, and she shuddered from stem to stern, struck by the bolt of Zeus, and was filled with sulphurous smoke, and the whole crew fell out of the ship. They were carried on the waves round the black ship like sea-crows, and the god took their homecoming from them.

"But as for me, Zeus himself, when my heart was hemmed in with trouble, put the tossing mast of the dark-prowed ship into my hands, so that I might escape destruction again. I clung round it and was carried by the deadly winds. For nine days I was carried, and on the tenth black night the great rolling wave brought me to the land of the Thesprotians. There Pheidon, the king of the Thesprotians, took me in and asked no ransom, because his dear son came on me when I was overcome with cold and weariness and raised me up by the hand and led me until he came to his father's palace; and he dressed me in a cloak and a tunic for clothes.

"There I learned of Odysseus, because the king said he had entertained him and given him welcome on his way to his own country. And he showed me all the treasure Odysseus had gathered, bronze and gold and iron worked with labour; it would feed his children after him to the tenth generation, so great was the wealth lying stored for him in the king's halls. But Odysseus, he said, had gone to Dodona, to hear the will of Zeus from the high-crested oak of the god, and how he might come back to the rich country of Ithaca after so long an absence, openly or in secret. And besides that he swore in my own presence, as he poured offerings in his house, that the ship was launched and the men ready who were to convey him to his own dear country.

"But he sent me off first, because a ship of the Thesprotians happened to be setting out for Dulichium, rich in wheat. He told them to convey me there with kindly care, to king Acastus. But an evil plan about me found favour in their hearts, so that I should be brought into utter misery even yet. When the seafaring ship had sailed far from the land, they set about bringing on the day of slavery for me. They stripped me of my clothes, my cloak and tunic, and dressed me in other clothes, a vile ragged cloak and tunic, the tattered things you see in front of your eyes. And in the evening they reached the tilled fields of clear-seen Ithaca. Then they bound me fast in the benched ship with a twisted rope, and went ashore themselves and hurried to take their supper on the shore of the sea.

"But the gods themselves undid my bonds easily enough, and I wrapped the tattered cloak round my head and slid down the smooth loading-plank and brought my breast to the sea, and then struck out with both hands and swam, and was very soon out of the water and away from them. Then I went up to a place where there was a thicket of leafy wood and lay there crouching. And they went this way and that with loud cries; but as there seemed no profit in going any further with the search, they went back aboard their hollow ship. And the gods themselves hid me easily and led me and brought me to the farm of a wise man; because it seems it is still my lot to live."

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "Poor stranger, you have stirred my heart deeply, telling the whole tale of your suffering and your wanderings. But in this you have not spoken rightly, and you will not persuade me with your tale about Odysseus. Why should a man in your state lie for no purpose? I know well enough myself about my master's homecoming: he was hated utterly by all the gods, in that they did not kill him among the Trojans, or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound up the whole skein of the war. Then the whole army of the Achaeans would have made him a tomb, and he would have won great glory for his son as well in the days to come. As it is, the spirits of the storm have swept him away and left no news.

"For my part I live off by myself with the pigs, and do not go to the city, unless wise Penelope calls me there when news comes to her from somewhere. Then men sit round the one who has come and question him closely, both those who grieve for their lord, who has long been gone, and those who are glad, as they eat up his substance without paying for it. But I have no wish to ask or enquire, since the time when an Aetolian took me in with his story — a man who had killed somebody, and came to my house after wandering over the wide earth, and I gave him a kindly welcome. He said he had seen Odysseus among the Cretans, at the house of Idomeneus, mending his ships which storms had shattered. And he said he would come either by summer or by harvest time, bringing a great deal of treasure along with his godlike companions. So you too, old man of many sorrows, since a god has brought you to me — do not try to win my favour with lies, or to cajole me in any way. It is not for that that I shall show you respect or kindness, but out of fear of Zeus, the stranger's god, and out of pity for you."

Then Odysseus of the many tricks answered him and said: "You have a heart in your breast that is slow to believe, seeing that even with an oath I have not won you or persuaded you. But come, let us make an agreement, and the gods who hold Olympus shall be witnesses for us both in time to come. If your master comes back to this house, dress me in a cloak and a tunic for clothes and send me on my way to Dulichium, where I want to be. And if your master does not come as I say, set the slaves on me and throw me down from a great cliff, so that the next beggar takes care about deceiving people."

And the good swineherd answered him and said: "Yes, stranger — that would win me fine fame and prosperity among men, now and hereafter, if I first brought you to my hut and gave you hospitality and then killed you and took away your dear life. I should have a ready heart for praying to Zeus, son of Cronos, after that. But it is supper time now, and I hope my companions come soon, so that we can get a good supper ready in the hut."

So they talked to each other; and the pigs and the swineherds came up. They shut the sows in to sleep in their usual sties, and an extraordinary noise rose from them as they were penned in. Then the good swineherd called to his companions:

"Bring out the best of the boars, so that I can kill him for the stranger who comes from far away; and we shall have some profit from it ourselves, who have long borne labour and suffering for the sake of the white-tusked pigs, while others eat up our work without paying for it."

With that he split wood with the pitiless bronze, and the others brought in a fatted boar of five years old and set him by the hearth. And the swineherd did not forget the immortals, because he had an understanding heart, but as a first offering he threw bristles from the head of the white-tusked boar into the fire and prayed to all the gods that wise Odysseus might come back to his own house. Then he raised himself up and struck the boar with a billet of oak he had left over from splitting the wood, and the life left him. And the others cut his throat and singed him and cut him up quickly, and the swineherd took bits of raw flesh from all the limbs as first offerings and laid them in the rich fat. These he threw into the fire when he had sprinkled them with barley meal; and the rest they cut up and spitted and roasted carefully, and drew it all off the spits and threw it in a heap on the platters.

Then the swineherd stood up to carve, because his heart knew well what was fair, and he cut up the meat and divided it into seven portions. One he set aside with a prayer for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia, and the rest he handed round to each man. And he honoured Odysseus with the long chine of the white-tusked boar, and gladdened his master's heart; and Odysseus of the many tricks said to him:

"Eumaeus, may you be as dear to father Zeus as you are to me, since you honour me with a good portion, in the state I am in."

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "Eat, unhappy stranger, and enjoy such fare as there is here. It is the god who will give one thing and hold back another, whatever seems good to his heart; because he can do anything."

He spoke, and sacrificed the first pieces to the gods who live for ever; and when he had poured offerings of the flaming wine, he put the cup into the hands of Odysseus, the sacker of cities, and sat down by his own portion. And bread was served to them by Mesaulius, whom the swineherd had got by himself while his master was away, without the knowledge of his mistress or of old Laertes, buying him from the Taphians with his own goods. So they put out their hands to the good food in front of them. And when they had eaten and drunk as much as they wanted, Mesaulius took the food away, and they were ready to go to bed, full of bread and meat.

Now the night came on, foul and moonless, and Zeus rained the whole night through, and the west wind, always the rainy wind, blew hard. Then Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd, to see whether he would take off his own cloak and give it to him, or tell one of his companions to do it, since he cared for him so much:

"Listen to me now, Eumaeus, and all the rest of you, his men. There is a wish in my heart, and I will tell a tale; the wine tells me to, that fooling wine, which sets a man singing and laughing softly however wise he is, and makes him get up and dance, and brings out a word that would be better unsaid. Still, since I have spoken out once, I will hide nothing. I wish I were young and my strength firm, as when we made our ambush ready and led it up under the walls of Troy. The leaders were Odysseus and Menelaus, son of Atreus, and I was third in command with them; that was how they arranged it themselves.

"Now when we had come to the city and the steep wall, we lay round the town in the thick brushwood, among the reeds and the swamp land, crouching under our arms; and night came on, foul, when the north wind had dropped, and frosty, and snow came down on us from above, covering us like rime, bitterly cold, and ice formed on our shields. All the rest had cloaks and tunics and slept in peace, with their shields covering their shoulders; but I had left my cloak behind with my companions when I set out, in my folly, because I did not think I would be cold even so, and had come with only my shield and my bright kilt.

"But at the third watch of the night, when the stars had turned their course, I spoke to Odysseus, who was near me, nudging him with my elbow; and he listened at once. 'Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of the many tricks, I shall not be among the living much longer. The cold is killing me; I have no cloak. Some god beguiled me into wearing only my tunic, and now there is no way out.'

"So I spoke, and he thought up this plan in his heart — such a man he was both at planning and at fighting — and he said to me in a low voice: 'Be quiet now, in case one of the other Achaeans hears you.'

"With that he raised his head on his elbow and said: 'Listen, friends. A dream from the gods came to me in my sleep. We have come very far from the ships, and I wish there were somebody to take word to Agamemnon, son of Atreus, shepherd of the army, in the hope that he might order more men to come from the ships.'

"So he spoke, and Thoas, son of Andraemon, sprang up quickly and flung his purple cloak off and set out to run to the ships. And I lay gladly in his garment, and golden-throned Dawn appeared. I wish I were as young as I was then, and my strength as firm; then one of the swineherds on the farm would give me a cloak, both out of kindness and out of respect for a brave fighting man. As it is they look down on me, since I have foul clothes on me."

And you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd, and said: "Old man, the tale you have told is a good one, and you have said nothing amiss or unprofitable so far. So you shall not lack clothing or anything else a hard-pressed suppliant should get when he meets somebody — for this night at least; but in the morning you will shake those rags of yours about you again. There are not many cloaks here or changes of tunics to put on; each man has one only. But when the dear son of Odysseus comes, he will give you a cloak and a tunic for clothes himself, and will send you wherever your heart and spirit tell you to go."

With that he sprang up and put a bed for Odysseus near the fire and threw skins of sheep and goats on it. Odysseus lay down there, and the swineherd threw a great thick cloak over him, which he kept at hand for a change of clothes whenever a terrible storm should come on.

So Odysseus slept there, and the young men slept beside him. But the swineherd did not like a bed in that place, where he would be lying away from the boars, so he got ready to go outside. And Odysseus was glad that he took such care of his master's property while he was away. First Eumaeus flung his sharp sword over his strong shoulders, and then put on a cloak, very thick, to keep the wind off; and he picked up the fleece of a large well-fatted goat and took a sharp javelin to keep off dogs and men, and went out to lie down and sleep where the white-tusked boars slept, under a hollow rock in a place sheltered from the north wind.
