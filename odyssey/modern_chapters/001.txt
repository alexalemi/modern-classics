Book Two

As soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, the son of Odysseus got out of bed and put on his clothes. He slung his sharp sword over his shoulder, bound the fine sandals under his shining feet, and came out of his room looking like a god. Straight away he told the clear-voiced heralds to call the long-haired Achaeans to an assembly. They made the call and the men gathered quickly.

When they had come together, Telemachus went out to the meeting place with a bronze spear in his hand — not alone; two swift dogs came with him. And Athena poured a marvellous grace over him, so that the whole crowd stared as he came. He sat down in his father's seat, and the elders made room for him.

The first to speak among them was lord Aegyptius, an old man bent with age who knew a great deal. He spoke because a son of his had gone in the hollow ships to Ilium, famous for horses, with godlike Odysseus — Antiphus, who was a fighter. The savage Cyclops had killed him in his hollow cave and made his last meal of him. He had three other sons: one, Eurynomus, went about with the suitors, and two kept the family farm. Even so he could not stop thinking of the one who was gone, grieving and sorrowing for him, and it was with tears in his eyes that he spoke to the assembly.

"Listen to me, men of Ithaca, and hear what I have to say. We have not held an assembly or a session since the day noble Odysseus went off in the hollow ships. Who has called us together now? What need has come on him, on one of the young men or one of the older ones? Has he heard some news of the army coming back, that he could tell us plainly, having heard it first himself? Or is there some other public business he means to raise? He seems to me a good man, and a fortunate one. May Zeus bring to pass for him whatever good thing his heart is set on."

He spoke, and the son of Odysseus was glad at the words, and took them for an omen. He did not stay in his seat any longer; he was eager to speak. He stood up in the middle of the assembly, and the herald Peisenor, a man of good sense, put the staff into his hands. Then he spoke, turning first to the old man.

"Old man, the one who called this gathering is not far off, as you will see for yourself. It is I. Trouble has come on me more than on anyone. I have heard no news of the army coming home that I could tell you plainly, having heard it first myself, and there is no other public business I mean to raise. It is my own need, and the evil that has fallen on my house — two evils. First I have lost my noble father, who was once king over you here and was gentle as a father to you. And now a far greater one has come, which is going to wreck my whole house before long and destroy everything I live on.

"Suitors have fastened on my mother against her will, the sons of the very men who are the leading men here. They shrink from going to the house of her father Icarius, so that he can fix the bride-price for his daughter himself and give her to whichever of them he chooses, the one he likes. Instead they crowd into our house day after day, killing our oxen and our sheep and our fat goats, feasting, drinking the bright wine as though it were nothing — and all this wealth is being wasted, because there is no man here as Odysseus was, to keep ruin away from the house. I am nothing like him, not at all. If I try, I shall only be found weak and knowing nothing about fighting. Truly, I would defend myself if I had the strength, because what is being done now is past bearing, and my house has been destroyed past all decency.

"Be ashamed of yourselves. Think what the neighbours living round about will say. And be afraid of the anger of the gods, in case they turn on you, disgusted at what you are doing. I beg you by Olympian Zeus, and by Themis, who breaks up the assemblies of men and calls them together — stop, my friends, and leave me alone with my bitter grief. Unless, of course, my father, noble Odysseus, once did some deliberate harm to the well-greaved Achaeans, and you are paying me back for it deliberately now by egging these men on. It would be better for me if you ate up my treasures and my flocks yourselves. If you did, there might be some compensation one day: we would go up and down the town pressing our claim and demanding our goods back until every bit was returned. As it is, you are loading griefs on my heart that there is no cure for."

That is what he said, in anger, and he threw the staff on the ground and burst into tears; and pity ran through the whole crowd. Then they all sat silent, and nobody had the heart to answer Telemachus harshly.

Only Antinous answered him. "Telemachus, you are a big talker with no check on your nerve. What a thing to say — trying to shame us, wanting to hang the blame on us. It is not the Achaean suitors who are at fault here in any way. It is your own mother, who is cleverer than any woman alive. It is three years now, and the fourth is nearly gone, since she started deceiving the hearts of the Achaeans in their breasts. She holds out hope to all of us and makes promises to each man separately, sending messages round, when her mind is on something else entirely.

"And here is another trick she thought up. She set up a great loom in the hall and started weaving, on a web fine in the thread and very wide, and said to us straight away:

"'Young men, my suitors — since noble Odysseus is dead, be patient about marrying me, however eager you are, until I finish this cloth. I do not want my spinning to be wasted. It is a shroud for lord Laertes, for the time when the cruel fate of death lays him low, so that none of the Achaean women in the country can be angry with me for letting a man who won so much lie without a shroud.'

"That is what she said, and our proud hearts agreed to it. And every day she went on weaving at that great web, and every night she had torches set beside her and unravelled it. For three years she kept the Achaeans from finding out, and fooled us. But when the fourth year came round with the turning seasons, one of her women, who knew all about it, told us, and we caught her unravelling the splendid thing. So she had to finish it, whether she wanted to or not.

"So this is the answer the suitors give you, and you may know it yourself in your own heart, and let all the Achaeans know it. Send your mother away. Tell her to marry whichever man her father says, and whoever pleases her. But if she goes on much longer wearing out the sons of the Achaeans, keeping in mind what Athena has given her above other women — skill in beautiful work, and a fine mind, and cunning tricks such as we have never heard of even in the women of the old days, the fair-haired Achaean women, Tyro and Alcmene and Mycene with the lovely crown, not one of whom had Penelope's turn of mind — well, this at least she has not thought out right. Because for just as long as she keeps to the plan the gods have now put in her heart, men will go on eating up your living and your property. She is winning herself a great name, and winning you the loss of a great deal of substance. As for us, we are not going to our own lands or anywhere else until she marries whichever of the Achaeans she chooses."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Antinous, I cannot possibly throw the woman who bore me and brought me up out of the house against her will. My father is somewhere in another country, alive or dead. It would be a bad thing for me to pay Icarius back the great price I would owe him if I sent my mother away of my own accord. Her father would make me suffer for it, and heaven would send other troubles besides, because as my mother left the house she would call down the dread Avengers on me; and men would blame me too. So I will never say that word.

"And as for you — if your own hearts are angry at this, get out of my halls and make your feasts somewhere else. Eat your own food, and move from house to house. Or if this looks better to you and more profitable, that one man's living should be destroyed and nothing paid for it, then eat it. But I will call on the gods who live for ever, in the hope that Zeus grants that the work be paid for; and then you will die inside this house, and nothing will be paid for that either."

So spoke Telemachus, and in answer Zeus, whose voice carries far, sent out two eagles, flying down from a mountain peak. For a while they went on the wind, side by side, with their wings spread wide; but when they came over the middle of the noisy assembly they wheeled about, beating their wings hard, and looked down on the heads of all of them with death in their eyes. Then they tore at each other's cheeks and necks with their talons and darted away to the right, over the houses and over the city.

The men were astonished when they saw the birds, and turned over in their minds what was going to happen. Then the old lord Halitherses, son of Mastor, spoke among them, because there was no man of his generation who knew as much about birds and could say what was fated. He meant it kindly, and he spoke to the assembly.

"Listen to me, men of Ithaca, and hear what I have to say. It is to the suitors above all that I am declaring this, since it is on them that a great disaster is rolling. Odysseus is not going to be away from his own people much longer. I think he is near now, sowing death and doom for these men, every one of them. And there are many more of us here in clear-seen Ithaca whom he will ruin. Let us think, long before that, how we may put a stop to this — or rather let them stop it themselves, which is at once the better course for them. I am not prophesying as a man untried; I know what I am saying. Everything I told Odysseus has come out as I told it, when the Argives embarked for Ilium and Odysseus of the many tricks went with them. I said he would come home in the twentieth year after suffering a great deal and losing all his companions, and unrecognised by anyone. And now it is all coming to pass."

Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him. "Old man, get up and go home and prophesy to your children, in case something bad happens to them in the days to come. I am a great deal better than you at prophesying about this. Plenty of birds go to and fro under the rays of the sun, and not all of them mean anything. As for Odysseus, he has died a long way off, and I wish you had died with him. Then you would not be spouting so much interpretation, or stirring Telemachus up in his anger, hoping for some present for your house if he happens to give you one.

"But I will say this to you outright, and it is going to happen. If you, with all your old wisdom, talk a younger man round and set him on to be angry, then in the first place it will go worse for him, and there will be nothing whatever he can do against these men here; and on you, old man, we shall lay a fine that will hurt you to pay, and it will be bitter to bear.

"And here in front of everyone I will give Telemachus this advice myself. Let him tell his mother to go back to her father's house, and they will make the wedding there and put together the gifts, all of them, everything that ought to go with a much-loved daughter. Before that, I do not think the sons of the Achaeans are going to give up their hard courting, since we are not afraid of any man in any case — not of Telemachus, for all his talk — and we take no notice of any prophecy you may deliver, old man. It will come to nothing and you will be hated all the more for it. His possessions will be eaten up, and badly, and there will be no compensation for it ever, as long as she keeps putting the Achaeans off about her marriage. And we, waiting here day after day, are rivals for her because she is so remarkable, and we do not go after other women, any one of whom each of us might suitably marry."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Eurymachus, and the rest of you lordly suitors — I am not going to beg you about this any longer or say another word about it, because now the gods know and all the Achaeans know. But give me a fast ship and twenty companions who can take me there and back. I am going to Sparta and to sandy Pylos to ask after my father's homecoming, who has been gone so long — in case any mortal man can tell me something, or I hear a word from Zeus, which is what most often brings men news. If I hear my father is alive and on his way, then I could hold out for another year, hard as it is. And if I hear he is dead and gone, then I will come back to my own country, heap up a mound for him, pay him the funeral rites, all of them, as is proper, and give my mother to a husband."

With that he sat down, and Mentor stood up among them, a companion of noble Odysseus. When Odysseus sailed away he had put his whole household in the old man's charge, to obey him and to keep everything safe. He meant it kindly, and he spoke to the assembly.

"Listen to me, men of Ithaca, and hear what I have to say. From now on let no sceptred king be deliberately kind and gentle, or take any care for what is right in his heart. Let him be harsh, always, and do injustice — seeing that nobody remembers godlike Odysseus, among the people he was lord over, and he was gentle as a father. I do not grudge the arrogant suitors their acts of violence and the wickedness they think up, because it is their own lives they are risking when they eat up the house of Odysseus by force, telling themselves he will never come back. It is the rest of you I am angry with, that you sit here in silence, saying not one word to check the suitors, when you are many and they are few."

Then Leocritus, son of Euenor, answered him. "Mentor, you troublemaker, you have lost your wits. What a thing to say, telling men to stop us. It would be a hard business fighting over a dinner with men who outnumber you. Even if Odysseus of Ithaca came himself, and had it in his heart to drive the lordly suitors out of his hall while they were feasting in his house, his wife would get no joy from his coming, however much she longed for him. He would meet a shameful death right here, if he fought against greater numbers. What you have said is not right.

"Come, then — break up, all of you, and go to your own lands. Mentor and Halitherses will speed this fellow's journey; they are friends of his father's house from the old days. But I think he will sit here a long while and get his news in Ithaca, and never make this journey at all."

He spoke, and broke up the assembly in a hurry. They scattered, each man to his own house, and the suitors went to the house of godlike Odysseus.

But Telemachus went off by himself to the shore of the sea, and washed his hands in the grey water, and prayed to Athena. "Hear me, you who came yesterday to our house as a god and told me to go in a ship over the misty water to ask after my father's homecoming, who has been gone so long. The Achaeans are blocking every part of it, and the suitors most of all, in their wicked insolence."

That was his prayer, and Athena came up beside him in the likeness of Mentor, in his shape and in his voice, and spoke to him with winged words.

"Telemachus, you will not be a coward or a fool in the days ahead, if any of your father's fine spirit has been poured into you — the sort of man he was for carrying a thing through in word and deed. So this journey of yours will not be pointless or unfinished. But if you are not his son and Penelope's, then I have no hope you will get what you want. Few sons are like their fathers. Most are worse; a few are better. But since you are not going to be a coward or a fool in the days ahead, and the good sense of Odysseus has not failed in you altogether, there is hope you will bring this thing off.

"So never mind the will and the plans of the suitors. They are fools. There is nothing sensible or just about them, and they know nothing about the death and the black fate that is standing close beside them, so that they will all die in a single day. The journey you have set your heart on will not be put off long — I am such an old friend of your father's house that I will fit out a fast ship for you and come with you myself. Now go back to the house and mix with the suitors. Get the stores ready and put it all in containers: wine in jars, and barley meal, which is the marrow of men, in strong skins. And I will go round the town and quickly gather a crew of volunteers. There are plenty of ships in sea-girt Ithaca, new ones and old; I will pick out the best of them for you, and we will fit her out quickly and launch her on the open water."

So spoke Athena, daughter of Zeus, and Telemachus did not wait long after hearing the goddess's voice. He went off to the house with a heavy heart, and found the arrogant suitors in the halls, skinning goats and singeing pigs in the courtyard. Antinous came straight up to him, laughing, and took his hand, and said:

"Telemachus, you big talker with no check on your nerve, do not carry any more bad deeds or bad words in your heart. Eat and drink, I tell you, the way you used to. The Achaeans will get all this ready for you, the ship and the picked oarsmen, so that you can go quickly to sacred Pylos and ask after your noble father."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered him: "Antinous, there is no possible way I can sit down to dinner quietly in your overbearing company and enjoy myself with an easy mind. Was it not enough, you suitors, that in the past you wasted a great deal of my good property while I was still a child? Now that I am grown, and learn by listening to what others say, and my spirit rises in me, I am going to try to hurl an evil fate on you — going to Pylos, or staying here in this country. Go I will, and the journey I am talking about will not be wasted, though I sail in another man's ship, since you will not let me be master of a ship or oarsmen. That, I suppose, seemed more to your advantage."

With that he snatched his hand out of Antinous's hand, without any ceremony. The suitors were busy with the feast through the hall, and they mocked him and jeered at him as they talked. One of the arrogant young men would say:

"Telemachus is certainly planning to murder us. He will bring men to help him from sandy Pylos, or even from Sparta — he is set on it, terribly. Or he means to go to Ephyre, that rich country, and bring back deadly drugs from there to throw into the wine-bowl and destroy the lot of us."

And another of the arrogant young men would say: "Who knows? He may go off in the hollow ship and die himself, wandering far from his own people, the way Odysseus did. That would make more work for us, because we should have to divide up all his property, and give the house to his mother to keep, and to whoever married her."

That is how they talked. But Telemachus went down to his father's high-roofed store-room, a wide room where gold and bronze lay piled up, and clothes in chests, and plenty of sweet-smelling oil. Great jars of wine stood there too, old and sweet, with an unmixed divine drink inside them, ranged in order along the wall, in case Odysseus should ever come home after all his hard suffering. The double doors were shut, close-fitting, and a housekeeper stayed there night and day guarding everything with a careful mind — Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor. Telemachus called her into the store-room and said to her:

"Nurse, draw me off some wine in jars — the sweet wine, the best there is next to what you keep for that unlucky man, always thinking of him, in case Odysseus, born of Zeus, comes back from somewhere, having got clear of death and the fates. Fill twelve jars and fit lids to all of them, and pour me barley meal into well-sewn skins, twenty measures of ground meal. And keep it to yourself. Have it all put together, and I will come for it in the evening, when my mother goes up to her room and thinks about sleeping. I am going to Sparta and sandy Pylos to ask after my dear father's homecoming, in case I hear anything."

At that his dear nurse Eurycleia gave a shrill cry, and spoke to him in tears with winged words. "Dear child, how has this idea got into your head? Where do you mean to go, over the wide earth, an only son and loved as you are? He is dead, far from home, Odysseus, born of Zeus, in some strange country. And these men, the moment you are gone, will plot against you, so that you die by a trick, and they can divide all this property among themselves. Stay here and look after what is yours. There is no need for you to suffer and go wandering over the restless sea."

Then clear-headed Telemachus answered her: "Courage, nurse. This plan of mine did not come without a god behind it. But swear you will say nothing of it to my mother until the eleventh or twelfth day comes, or until she misses me herself and hears that I am gone — so that she does not spoil her lovely face with crying."

So he spoke, and the old woman swore a great oath by the gods to say nothing. And when she had sworn and finished the oath, she drew the wine off into jars for him at once and poured barley meal into well-sewn skins; and Telemachus went back to the hall and mixed with the suitors.

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena thought of something else. In the likeness of Telemachus she went all through the city, and came up to each of the men and spoke to him, telling them to gather at evening beside the fast ship. And she asked Noemon, the fine son of Phronius, for a fast ship, and he promised it willingly.

The sun went down and all the roads grew dark. Then she drew the fast ship to the sea and put into it all the gear that well-benched ships carry, and moored it at the mouth of the harbour, and the good crew gathered round it, and the goddess put heart into every man.

Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena thought of something else again. She went to the house of godlike Odysseus and began to pour sweet sleep over the suitors, so that they wandered in their drinking and the cups dropped out of their hands. They got up to go and rest through the city, and did not sit long, because sleep was falling on their eyelids. Then grey-eyed Athena spoke to Telemachus, calling him out in front of the stately hall, having made herself like Mentor in shape and in voice.

"Telemachus, your well-greaved companions are already sitting at the oars, waiting for you to start. Come, let us go, and not hold their journey up any longer."

With that Pallas Athena led the way, quickly, and he followed in the goddess's footsteps. When they had come down to the ship and the sea they found their long-haired companions on the shore, and the strong young Telemachus spoke to them.

"Come, friends, let us fetch the stores. They are all gathered together in the hall. My mother knows nothing about this, nor do the women; only one person heard what I said."

With that he led the way and they went with him, and they brought everything and stowed it in the well-benched ship, as the son of Odysseus told them. Then Telemachus stepped on board, with Athena going before him, and she sat down in the stern of the ship, and Telemachus sat beside her; and the men loosed the stern cables and came on board themselves and sat down at the benches.

And grey-eyed Athena sent them a good wind, a strong west wind singing over the wine-dark sea. Telemachus called to his men and told them to lay hold of the tackle, and they did as he called. They raised the fir mast and set it in the hollow socket and made it fast with the forestays, and hauled the white sail up with twisted ox-hide ropes. The wind filled the belly of the sail, and the dark wave sang loudly round the stem of the ship as she went, and she ran over the water on her way. Then, when they had made the tackle fast in the swift black ship, they set out bowls filled to the brim with wine and poured offerings to the immortal gods who live for ever, and most of all to the grey-eyed daughter of Zeus.

So all night long, and through the dawn, the ship cut her way forward.
