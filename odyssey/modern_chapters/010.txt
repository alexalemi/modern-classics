Book Ten

Then we came to the Aeolian island, where Aeolus lived, son of Hippotas, dear to the immortal gods, on a floating island, with a wall of unbreakable bronze all round it and the cliff running up sheer. Twelve children of his live in the halls, six daughters and six sturdy sons, and he gave his daughters to his sons as wives. So they feast continually beside their dear father and their good mother, with boundless good things laid out in front of them; and the house, full of the savour of the feasting, rings all round it even in the outer court by day, and by night they sleep again beside their chaste wives on blankets and on corded bedsteads.

So we came to their city and their fine palace, and for a whole month he made me welcome and questioned me about everything, about Ilios and the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans; and I told him the whole story in its order. But when I asked him in my turn if I might go, and told him to send me on my way, he refused me nothing, and helped my sending. He gave me a bag made of the hide of a nine-year-old ox, which he skinned, and in it he bound the ways of the blustering winds; because the son of Cronos had made him keeper of the winds, to still or to rouse whichever one he pleases. And he made it fast in my hollow ship with a bright silver cord, so that not the slightest breath could escape. But he sent the breath of the west wind out to blow for my sending, so that it should carry ships and men on their way. And this he was not to bring off, because we were lost through our own folly.

For nine days we sailed, night and day alike, and now on the tenth our own country came in sight, and we were so close that we could see men tending the beacon fires. Then sweet sleep came over me, in my weariness; because I had kept the ship's sheet in my own hand the whole time and given it to none of my companions, so that we might get home sooner. And meanwhile my companions started talking to one another, and saying that I was bringing gold and silver home for myself as gifts from Aeolus, the great-hearted son of Hippotas. And one of them would say, with a glance at his neighbour:

"How loved and honoured this man is, wherever he comes, in whatever city and country. He is carrying a great deal of fine treasure away with him from the land of Troy out of the plunder, while we, who have made the same journey he has, are going home with empty hands. And now Aeolus has given him these gifts, granting them freely out of affection. Come, let us have a quick look at what is here, and see how much gold and silver is in the bag."

So they spoke, and my companions' bad advice won the day. They loosed the bag, and all the winds leapt out, and the storm wind seized them at once and carried them out to sea, weeping, away from their own country. And I woke, and turned it over in my good heart whether to throw myself out of the ship and die in the sea, or to endure in silence and stay among the living. I endured and stayed, and covered my head and lay down in the ship. And the ships were carried by an evil blast of wind back to the Aeolian island, and my companions groaned.

There we went ashore and drew water, and my companions took their meal at once beside the fast ships. And when we had tasted food and drink, I took a herald and one companion with me and went to the splendid palace of Aeolus, and found him feasting beside his wife and his children. So we went into the house and sat down by the doorposts on the threshold, and their hearts were amazed, and they asked us:

"How have you come here, Odysseus? What cruel god attacked you? We sent you off with every care, so that you should reach your own country and your home, and any place you wanted."

So they spoke, and I answered them with a sorrowing heart: "My wicked companions did me this harm, and an accursed sleep along with them. But heal it, my friends; the power is with you."

So I spoke, addressing them with gentle words, and they said nothing. Then their father answered and said: "Get off our island as fast as you can, you vilest of all living things. There is no way I can help or send on his way a man who is hated by the blessed gods. Get out, since you come here hated by the immortals."

With that he sent me out of the house, groaning heavily.

From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts. And the men's spirits were worn down by the hard rowing, through our own folly, since there was no breeze any more to carry us on our way. So for six days we sailed, night and day alike, and on the seventh we came to the high citadel of Lamus, to Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, where one herdsman calls to another as he drives his flock in, and the other answers as he drives his out. There a man who never slept could have earned a double wage, one for herding cattle and one for pasturing white sheep; because the outgoings of the night and of the day are close together there.

When we had come in there to the fine harbour, which has a sheer cliff running unbroken along both sides of it, with jutting headlands opposite each other stretching out at the mouth and a narrow entrance, all the rest steered their curved ships in, and the ships were moored inside the hollow harbour close together; because no wave ever swelled in there, great or small, but there was a bright calm all round. But I alone moored my black ship outside, there at the edge of the land, making the cables fast to the rock. Then I climbed a rugged height, a point of outlook, and took my stand there. From there no works of oxen or of men could be seen; we saw only smoke going up from the land.

So I sent some of my companions to go and find out who the men were who ate bread here on the earth. I chose two, and sent a third with them as a herald. When they had gone ashore they went along a smooth road by which wagons used to bring wood down to the city from the high mountains. And in front of the city they met a girl drawing water, the fine daughter of Antiphates the Laestrygonian, who had come down to the fair-flowing spring Artacia, where they used to carry water to the town from. So they came up to her and spoke to her and asked her who was king of these people, and who they were that he was lord over. And she showed them at once the high-roofed house of her father.

But when they had gone into the splendid house, they found his wife there, huge as a mountain peak, and they were appalled at her. She called glorious Antiphates at once from the meeting place, her husband, and he contrived a miserable destruction for them. He seized one of my companions straight away and got his meal ready, and the other two sprang up and came running back to the ships. Then he raised a cry through the city, and as they heard it the mighty Laestrygonians came crowding from all sides, a host past counting, not like men but like the Giants. They pelted us from the cliffs with rocks as big as a man could lift, and at once a dreadful din went up through the ships, of men dying and of ships being crushed. And they speared them like fish and carried them home, a loathsome meal.

Now while they were killing the men inside the deep harbour, I drew my sharp sword from beside my thigh and cut the cables of my dark-prowed ship with it, and called quickly to my companions and told them to fall to their oars so that we could get out of trouble. And they all tossed the sea with their oar-blades in fear of death, and gladly my ship ran out to sea, away from the beetling cliffs; but all those other ships were lost together there.

From there we sailed on, grieving in our hearts, glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions; and we came to the island of Aeaea, where Circe of the lovely hair lived, a dread goddess with human speech, own sister of Aeetes of the deadly mind. Both of them are the children of Helios, who gives light to mortals, and of Perse their mother, whom Oceanus fathered.

Here we put in to shore with our ship in silence, into a harbour where ships may lie, and some god guided us. Then we disembarked and lay there two days and two nights, eating our hearts out with weariness and grief. But when Dawn of the lovely hair brought the third day to its birth, I took my spear and my sharp sword and went up quickly from the ship to a place with a wide view, hoping to see the works of men and hear their voices. So I climbed a rugged height, a place of outlook, and took my stand there, and I saw smoke rising from the broad-wayed earth in the halls of Circe, through the thick brush and the wood.

And I turned over in my mind and heart whether to go and look into it, since I had seen the flaming smoke. And as I turned it over, this seemed to me the better way: to go first to the fast ship and the shore of the sea and give my companions their meal, and then send them out to look. But when, as I went, I was near the curved ship, some god took pity on me in my loneliness, and sent a great high-antlered stag right into my path. He was coming down from his pasture in the wood to the river to drink, because the strength of the sun was oppressing him; and as he came out I struck him on the spine in the middle of the back, and the bronze spear went right through him, and down he fell in the dust with a moan, and his spirit flew from him.

Then I planted my foot on him and drew the bronze spear out of the wound and left it lying on the ground; and I pulled up twigs and osiers and wove a rope about a fathom long, well twisted from end to end, and bound the feet of the monstrous beast together and went off to the black ship carrying him across my back, leaning on my spear — because there was no way I could hold him on my shoulder with one hand; he was a very big beast. I flung him down in front of the ship and heartened my companions with gentle words, going up to each man in turn:

"Friends, we are not going down to the house of Hades yet, for all our sorrows, before the day of our fate comes. Come, while there is still food and drink in our fast ship, let us think about eating, so that we do not waste away with hunger."

So I spoke, and they quickly did as I said. They drew their cloaks off their faces and stared at the stag on the shore of the restless sea, because he was a very big beast. And when they had had their fill of looking they washed their hands and got a splendid feast ready. So all day long until sunset we sat feasting on plenty of meat and sweet wine. But when the sun set and the dark came on we lay down to rest on the shore of the sea.

And as soon as the early dawn appeared, rosy-fingered, I called my men together and spoke to them all:

"Listen to me, companions, for all the trouble you are in. My friends, we do not know where the dark is or where the dawn, nor where the sun that gives light to mortals goes down under the earth, nor where he rises. Let us think at once whether there is any plan left to us. I do not think there is. I climbed a rugged point of outlook and saw the island, with the boundless deep set round it like a crown. The island itself lies low, and in the middle of it my eyes saw smoke through the thick brush and the wood."

So I spoke, and their spirits broke inside them as they remembered the doings of Antiphates the Laestrygonian, and the violence of the great-hearted Cyclops, the man-eater. And they wailed aloud and shed big tears. But nothing came of their mourning.

Then I divided all my well-greaved companions into two bands and appointed a leader for each. I took command of one, and godlike Eurylochus of the other. Then we quickly shook lots in a bronze helmet, and out leapt the lot of great-hearted Eurylochus. So he set out, and twenty-two companions went with him, all weeping; and they left us behind, lamenting.

In the forest glades they found the house of Circe, built of polished stone in a place with a wide view; and round about it were mountain wolves and lions, whom Circe had bewitched herself, because she gave them evil drugs. These beasts did not rush at my men, but pranced round them fawning, wagging their long tails. And as hounds fawn round their master when he comes from a feast, because he always brings them scraps to soothe their temper, so the stout-clawed wolves and lions fawned round them. But the men were seized with fear when they saw the dreadful creatures.

So they stood in the gateway of the goddess with the lovely hair, and inside they heard Circe singing in a sweet voice as she went to and fro at a great imperishable web, the kind of work that goddesses do, finely woven and beautiful and splendid. Then Polites spoke among them, a leader of men, the dearest and most trustworthy of my companions:

"Friends, somebody inside is going to and fro at a great web, singing sweetly, so that the whole floor echoes with it. Some goddess it is, or some woman. Come, let us call to her quickly."

So he spoke, and they called aloud to her. And she came out at once and opened the shining doors and asked them in; and they all went with her in their folly. Only Eurylochus stayed behind, because he suspected there was a trap.

She brought them in and made them sit on chairs and seats, and made them a potion of cheese and barley meal and yellow honey with Pramnian wine; but she mixed deadly drugs into the food, so that they should forget their own country altogether. And when she had given them the potion and they had drunk it off, she struck them at once with her wand and penned them in the sties. And they had the heads and the voices and the bristles and the shape of pigs, but their minds stayed unchanged, just as before. So they were penned there weeping, and Circe threw down mast and acorns for them, and the fruit of the cornel tree, to eat, the kind of thing wallowing pigs feed on.

But Eurylochus came straight back to the fast black ship to bring news of his companions and their shameful fate. He could not utter a word, for all he wanted to, so stricken to the heart was he with great distress; his eyes were full of tears and his spirit was set on lamenting. But when we questioned him in astonishment, then he told us what had happened to the others, his companions:

"We went through the thickets, as you told us, noble Odysseus. We found a fine palace in the forest glades, built of polished stone, in a place with a wide view. Somebody was going to and fro there at a great web, singing in a clear voice, some goddess or some woman; and they called aloud to her. And she came out at once and opened the shining doors and asked them in, and they all went with her in their folly. But I stayed behind, because I suspected there was a trap. And then they all vanished together, and not one of them appeared again, though I sat a long while and watched."

So he spoke, and I slung my silver-studded sword over my shoulders, a great sword of bronze, and slung my bow round me, and told him to lead me back by the same road. But he took hold of me with both hands and begged me by my knees, and spoke to me with winged words, wailing:

"Do not take me there against my will, you whom Zeus brought up; leave me here. I know that you will not come back yourself, and will not bring any of your companions back either. Let us get away as fast as we can with the men we have here; we may still escape the evil day."

So he spoke, but I answered him: "Eurylochus, you stay here in this place, eating and drinking beside the hollow black ship; but I am going, because a hard necessity is laid on me."

With that I went up from the ship and the sea. But when, as I went through the sacred glades, I was about to come to the great house of the sorceress Circe, then Hermes of the golden wand met me as I went towards the house, in the likeness of a young man with the first down on his lip, at the age when the charm of youth is loveliest. He took my hand and said to me:

"Where are you going now, again, unlucky man, alone through the hills, knowing nothing of the country? Your companions over there in the house of Circe are penned like pigs in close-barred sties. And have you come to set them free? I tell you, you will not get back yourself; you will stay there with the others. But come, I will save you from harm and get you out. Take this potent herb and go to the house of Circe, and it will keep the evil day off your head. And I will tell you all the deadly tricks of Circe.

"She will mix you a potion and throw drugs into the food; but even so she will not be able to bewitch you, because the potent herb I am going to give you will not allow it. And I will tell you the rest. When Circe strikes you with her long wand, then draw your sharp sword from beside your thigh and rush at Circe as if you meant to kill her. And she will be seized with fear and will tell you to lie with her. Then do not refuse the goddess's bed after that, so that she sets your companions free and gives you hospitality. But make her swear a great oath by the blessed gods that she is not plotting some fresh mischief against you to your harm, in case, when she has you stripped, she makes you a weakling and unmanned."

With that the slayer of Argus gave me the herb, pulling it out of the ground, and showed me what it was like. It was black at the root, and its flower was like milk. The gods call it moly, and it is hard for mortal men to dig; but with the gods everything is possible.

Then Hermes went off to high Olympus through the wooded island, and I went on to the house of Circe, and my heart turned over a great deal darkly as I went. So I stood at the gates of the goddess with the lovely hair. I stood there and called, and the goddess heard my voice. She came out at once and opened the shining doors and asked me in; and I went with her, with a badly troubled heart.

She brought me in and made me sit on a silver-studded chair, a beautiful chair, finely made, with a footstool below for the feet. And she made me a potion in a golden cup for me to drink, and put a drug into it, with evil purpose in her heart. But when she had given it to me and I had drunk it off, and was not bewitched, she struck me with her wand and said to me:

"Off with you to the sty now, and lie down with the rest of your companions."

So she spoke; but I drew my sharp sword from beside my thigh and rushed at Circe as though I meant to kill her. And she gave a loud cry and ran in under the blow and took hold of my knees, and spoke to me with winged words, wailing:

"Who are you among men, and where from? Where is your city, and where are your parents? Amazement takes hold of me that you have drunk this charm and were not bewitched at all. No other man has ever withstood this charm once he has drunk it and it has passed the barrier of his teeth. But the mind in your breast is not one that can be beguiled. You must be Odysseus, the man of ready invention, whom the slayer of Argus with the golden wand always told me would come here on his way home from Troy with his fast black ship. Come, put your sword back in its sheath, and let the two of us go up into my bed, so that we may lie together in love and trust each other."

So she spoke; but I answered her: "Circe, how can you tell me to be gentle to you, when you have turned my companions into pigs in your halls, and are keeping me here now and telling me with a guileful purpose to go to your chamber and go up into your bed, so that when you have me stripped you can make me a weakling and unmanned? No, I will not be willing to go up into your bed unless you, goddess, will consent to swear a great oath that you are not plotting some fresh mischief against me to my harm."

So I spoke, and she swore the oath at once to do me no harm, as I told her. And when she had sworn and finished the oath, then I went up into Circe's beautiful bed.

Meanwhile her women were busy in the halls, four girls who are her serving-women in the house. They are the children of the springs and the groves and the sacred rivers that flow out to the sea. One of them threw fine purple rugs over the chairs and spread a linen cloth underneath them; another drew silver tables up in front of the chairs and set golden baskets on them; and the third mixed sweet honey-hearted wine in a silver bowl and served out golden cups; and the fourth brought water and kindled a great fire under a big cauldron, and the water grew warm. And when the water boiled in the bright bronze she set me in a bath and bathed me with water from the great cauldron, mixing it to my liking and pouring it over my head and shoulders, until she took the soul-consuming weariness out of my limbs.

And when she had bathed me and rubbed me richly with oil and put a fine cloak and a tunic on me, she brought me into the hall and made me sit on a silver-studded chair — a beautiful chair, finely made, with a footstool below for the feet. Then a maid brought water for my hands in a lovely golden jug and poured it over a silver basin for me to wash, and drew a polished table up beside me. And the housekeeper, a grave woman, brought bread and set it in front of me, and plenty of meat with it, giving freely from her stores. Then she told me to eat, but my heart was not inclined to it. I sat there with other thoughts, and my spirit foreboded trouble.

Now when Circe noticed that I sat like that and did not put out my hands to the food, but was weighed down by hard grief, she came close to me and spoke winged words:

"Why do you sit like this, Odysseus, like a man struck dumb, eating your heart out, and touch neither food nor drink? Do you suspect some other trick? You need not be afraid at all; I have sworn a great oath already to do you no harm."

So she spoke, but I answered her: "Circe, what right-minded man could bring himself to taste food or drink before he had won his companions' freedom and seen them in front of his own face? If you are telling me to eat and drink with a ready heart, set them free, so that my eyes may see my trusty companions."

So I spoke, and Circe went out through the hall holding her wand in her hand, and opened the doors of the sty and drove them out in the shape of nine-year-old pigs. So they stood there in front of her, and she went through the middle of them and rubbed each man with another charm. Then the bristles fell away from their limbs, which the deadly drug queenly Circe had given them had made grow before, and they became men again, younger than they had been, and far handsomer and taller to look at. They knew me, and every one of them clung to my hands, and a passionate sobbing came over them all, and the house rang marvellously round them; and the goddess herself was moved to pity.

Then the beautiful goddess came up to me and said: "Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of the many tricks, go now to your fast ship and the shore of the sea. First of all draw the ship up on the land, and store your goods and all the tackle in the caves; and then come back yourself, and bring your trusty companions."

So she spoke, and my proud heart agreed. I went off to the fast ship and the shore of the sea, and there I found my trusty companions beside the fast ship, wailing pitifully, shedding big tears. As calves in a farmyard frisk about the droves of cows coming back to the yard when they have had their fill of grazing — they all frisk together in front of them, and the pens do not hold them any longer, but they run round their mothers lowing continually — so those men, when their eyes took me in, crowded round me weeping; and it seemed to their hearts as though they had got home to their own country and the very city of rugged Ithaca, where they were bred and born. And they spoke to me with winged words, wailing:

"We are as glad at your coming back, you whom Zeus brought up, as though we had got home to Ithaca, our own country. But come, tell us what happened to the others, our companions."

So they spoke, and I answered them with gentle words: "First of all let us draw the ship up on the land, and store our goods and all the tackle in the caves. Then hurry, every one of you, and come with me, so that you can see your companions in the sacred halls of Circe, eating and drinking; they have unfailing supplies."

So I spoke, and they quickly did as I said. Only Eurylochus tried to hold all my companions back, and he spoke to them with winged words:

"Poor wretches, where are we going? Why are you so in love with trouble, that you would go down to the house of Circe, who will turn us all into pigs or wolves or lions, so that we have to guard her great house whether we like it or not? It was the same with the Cyclops, when our companions went to his fold, and this reckless Odysseus went with them. It was through this man's folly that they died too."

So he spoke, and I turned it over in my heart whether to draw the long sword from beside my strong thigh and strike his head off with it and bring it to the ground, close kinsman of mine by marriage though he was. But my companions, one after another, tried to check me with gentle words:

"You who are sprung from Zeus, as for this man, we will leave him here beside the ship, if you say so, to guard the ship; but lead us to the sacred house of Circe."

With that they went up from the ship and the sea. And Eurylochus was not left beside the hollow ship either, but came with us, because he was afraid of my terrible rebuke.

Meanwhile in her halls Circe bathed the rest of my companions with kindly care, and rubbed them richly with oil, and put fleecy cloaks and tunics on them; and we found them all feasting generously in the halls. And when they saw and recognised each other face to face, they wept and wailed, and the house rang round them.

Then the beautiful goddess came up to me and said: "Do not rouse this great lamenting any longer. I know myself both all the troubles you have suffered on the teeming water and all the wrong cruel men have done you on land. Come, eat food and drink wine, until you get the spirit back in your breasts that you had when you first left your own country of rugged Ithaca. As it is you are withered and spiritless, always thinking of your weary wanderings, and your hearts are never glad, because you have suffered a great deal."

So she spoke, and our proud hearts agreed. So we stayed there day after day for a whole year, feasting on plenty of meat and sweet wine. But when a year had gone and the seasons turned, as the months waned and the long days came round in their course, then my trusty companions called me out and said:

"Strange man, think now at last of your own country, if it is fated for you to be saved and to reach your high-roofed house and your own country."

So they spoke, and my proud heart agreed. So all day long until sunset we sat feasting on plenty of meat and sweet wine. But when the sun set and the dark came on they lay down to sleep through the shadowy halls, and I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe and begged her by her knees; and the goddess heard my voice, and I spoke to her with winged words:

"Circe, keep the promise you made me, to send me home. My spirit is eager to be gone now, and so is the spirit of my companions, who wear my heart away sitting round me mourning, whenever you happen not to be there."

So I spoke, and the beautiful goddess answered at once: "Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of the many tricks, do not stay in my house any longer against your will. But you must finish another journey first, and come to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the spirit of Theban Teiresias, the blind seer, whose mind stays steady. To him, even in death, Persephone has granted reason, so that he alone has understanding; the others flit about as shadows."

So she spoke, and my spirit broke inside me, and I wept as I sat on the bed, and my heart had no more wish to live and see the light of the sun. But when I had had my fill of weeping and writhing, I answered her and said:

"Circe, who will guide us on this journey? No man has ever yet gone to Hades in a black ship."

So I spoke, and the beautiful goddess answered at once: "Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of the many tricks, let there be no concern in your mind for a pilot to guide your ship. Set up your mast and spread the white sail and sit down; and the breath of the north wind will carry her on. But when you have crossed the stream of Oceanus in your ship, where there is a level shore and the groves of Persephone — tall poplars and willows that shed their fruit — beach your ship there by the deep eddying Oceanus, and go yourself to the dank house of Hades.

"There Periphlegethon and Cocytus flow into Acheron, and Cocytus is a branch of the water of the Styx; and there is a rock, and the meeting place of the two roaring rivers. Go up close to that, prince, as I tell you, and dig a pit a cubit's length this way and that, and pour an offering round it to all the dead, first with milk and honey, then with sweet wine, and in the third place with water, and sprinkle white barley meal over it. And make an earnest plea to the powerless heads of the dead, promising that when you come to Ithaca you will sacrifice in your halls a barren heifer, the best you have, and will fill the altar with rich gifts; and that to Teiresias alone you will sacrifice separately a ram, wholly black, the finest in your flock.

"But when you have made your plea in prayer to the glorious tribes of the dead, then sacrifice a ram and a black ewe, turning their heads towards Erebus but turning yourself away and setting your face towards the streams of the river. Then many ghosts of men who are dead will come out. Then call to your companions and tell them to flay and burn the sheep lying there, killed by the pitiless bronze, and to pray to the gods, to mighty Hades and to dread Persephone. And draw your own sharp sword from beside your thigh, and sit there, and do not let the powerless heads of the dead come near the blood until you have questioned Teiresias. Then the seer will come to you soon, leader of men, and will tell you your road and the measure of your journey, and about your homecoming, how you are to go over the teeming water."

So she spoke, and golden-throned Dawn came at once. She put a cloak and a tunic on me for clothes, and the nymph dressed herself in a long white robe, finely woven and beautiful, and put a fine golden girdle round her waist and a veil on her head.

But I went through the halls and roused my men with gentle words, going up to each man in turn: "Do not sleep any longer now, dozing in sweet slumber. Let us go; queenly Circe has told me everything."

So I spoke, and their proud hearts agreed. But not even from there could I bring my men away unhurt. There was one, Elpenor, the youngest of them all, not very brave in war and not sound in his understanding, who had lain down apart from his companions in the sacred house of Circe, looking for the cool air, because he was heavy with wine. He heard the noise and the bustle of his companions moving about and sprang up suddenly, and forgot to go to the long ladder to come down again, but fell headlong from the roof, and his neck was broken away from the spine, and his spirit went down to the house of Hades.

But as my men were going on their way I spoke among them and said: "You think, I suppose, that you are going to your own dear country. But Circe has marked out another journey for us, to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the spirit of Theban Teiresias."

So I spoke, and their spirits broke inside them, and they sat down right where they were and wept and tore their hair. But nothing came of their lamenting.

But as we were going on our way to the fast ship and the shore of the sea, sorrowing and shedding big tears, Circe had gone ahead meanwhile and made fast a ram and a black ewe beside the black ship; she had passed us easily. Who could see a god with his own eyes against the god's will, coming or going?
