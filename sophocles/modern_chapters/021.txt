Oedipus at Colonus
(Part 4 of 4)

Chorus.
Oedipus — for the sake of the man who sent him, say whatever is right
to say, and then send him back.

Oedipus.
Men who hold this land: if it had not been Theseus who sent him here
to me — Theseus, who thought it fair that he should hear my answer — he
would never have heard my voice at all. As it is, he has been granted
that much, and he will go away having heard from me things that will
never once make his life any happier.

You. Worst of men. When you held the sceptre and the throne that your
brother holds in Thebes now, you drove out your own father — this man
here — and made me a man with no city, and made me wear these rags,
the rags that bring tears to your eyes now you look at them, now that
you have walked into the same misery I am in. This is nothing to weep
over. It is something I have to carry, for as long as I live,
remembering you as my murderer. You are the one who put me here, to
live on hardship. You threw me out. It is because of you that I wander
and beg my daily bread from strangers. And if these girls had not been
born to me to look after me, I would be dead, for anything you did. As
it is they keep me alive. They are the ones who look after me. They
are men, not women, when it comes to sharing the work. You two are
some other man's sons, not mine.

So the god's eye is on you — not yet as it will be, very soon, if
those companies of yours are really marching on the city of Thebes.
There is no way you will ever tear that city down. Before that, you
will fall, fouled with blood, and your brother with you, the two of
you alike. Those are the curses I sent up against the pair of you
before, and I call on them now to come and fight on my side, so that
you two learn to respect the parents who made you, and not to hold your
father in contempt because the father who fathered such sons is blind.
These girls did not do that. So my curses have your seat of
supplication in their power, and your throne with it — if Justice,
spoken of from of old, still sits beside Zeus under the ancient laws.

Now get out. I spit you out. You have no father in me. Worst of all
bad men, take these curses with you that I call down on you: never to
conquer the land of your own blood with the spear, never to come home
to the valley of Argos, but to die by a kinsman's hand, and to kill
the man who drove you out. That is my prayer. And I call on the hateful
darkness of Tartarus, where my fathers are, to take you away and house
you there. I call on the goddesses of this place. I call on Ares, who
threw that terrible hatred into the two of you. You have heard it. Now
go. Go and tell all the people of Cadmus, and your loyal allies with
them, what kind of inheritance Oedipus has divided among his sons.

Chorus.
Polyneices, I take no pleasure in the road that brought you here. Now
go back, as fast as you can.

Polyneices.
Oh, my journey. Oh, the failure of it — and my companions. So that is
the end of the road we set out on from Argos. I am wretched. An end I
cannot even speak of to a single one of my companions, and cannot turn
them back from. I have to go and meet it without a word.

Sisters — his daughters, my own blood — you have heard the hard things
our father is praying for. If his curses are fulfilled, and you somehow
find a way home, then by the gods do not dishonour me. Put me in a
grave, with the rites the dead are owed. The praise you get from this
man now, for the work you do for him, will bring you another praise no
smaller, for what you do for me.

Antigone.
Polyneices, I beg you. Listen to me about one thing.

Polyneices.
What is it, dearest Antigone? Say it.

Antigone.
Turn your army back to Argos, as fast as you can. Do not destroy
yourself and the city both.

Polyneices.
It cannot be done. How could I ever lead the same army out again, once
I had shown fear?

Antigone.
But why do you need to be angry a second time, brother? What do you
gain by tearing down your own homeland?

Polyneices.
It is shameful to be in exile — I am the elder — and to be laughed at
like this by my brother.

Antigone.
Then do you see how you are carrying his prophecies straight to their
mark? He says the two of you will die at each other's hands.

Polyneices.
Because that is what he wants. I cannot give way to it.

Antigone.
Oh, I am wretched. But who will dare to follow you, once they hear what
this man has foretold?

Polyneices.
I will not report bad news. A good commander tells the better side and
keeps the worse to himself.

Antigone.
So that is what you have decided, brother?

Polyneices.
Yes. And do not hold me back. This road is mine to take now, doomed and
evil, thanks to my father here and his Furies. As for you two — may
Zeus be good to you, if you do this for me when I am dead. Alive, you
will not have me again. Let go of me now. Goodbye. You will never see
me living again.

Antigone.
Oh, I am wretched.

Polyneices.
Do not grieve for me.

Antigone.
Who could help grieving for you, brother, when you are hurrying towards
a death you can see in front of you?

Polyneices.
If I have to, I will die.

Antigone.
No. Listen to me instead.

Polyneices.
Do not urge what must not be.

Antigone.
Then I am wretched past all bearing, if I lose you.

Polyneices.
That lies with the god — this way, or the other. As for you two, I
pray to the gods that you never meet with harm. Everyone knows you do
not deserve to suffer.

(Polyneices goes.)

Chorus.
	New troubles — here they come, new,
	heavy with fate, from the blind stranger,
	unless what is allotted is finding its mark.
	I cannot say that any purpose of the gods is empty.
	Time sees these things. Time always sees them,
	throwing some of them down,
	and the next day raising others up again.
	The sky cracked. Zeus!

Oedipus.
	Children, children — is there anyone here
	who could bring Theseus, the best of men, to me?

Antigone.
	Father, what is it you want him for?

Oedipus.
	This winged thunder of Zeus is going to take me
	to Hades, now. Send for him. Quickly.

(Thunder.)

Chorus.
	Look — a great one. It comes crashing down,
	the unspeakable noise, thrown by Zeus.
	Terror has run up into the ends of my hair.
	My heart is cowering. The lightning is burning
	across the sky again.
	What end will it let loose? I am afraid.
	It never flies out for nothing, never
	without something coming after it.
	Great sky! Zeus!

Oedipus.
	Children, the end of my life that was foretold
	has come for me. There is no turning from it now.

Antigone.
	How do you know? What tells you so?

Oedipus.
	I know it well. Somebody go, as fast as he can,
	and bring the lord of this land to me.

(Thunder.)

Chorus.
	Ah — ah — there! Once again
	the piercing crash is all round us.
	Be kind, god, be kind, if you are bringing
	something dark for the earth, our mother.
	Let me find you gracious. Let me not,
	because I looked on a man under a curse,
	come in for a favour that brings no gain.
	Lord Zeus, I am calling to you.

Oedipus.
	Is the man near? Will he find me still breathing, children,
	and my mind still steady?

Antigone.
	What is the promise you want fixed in his mind?

Oedipus.
	To give them, for the good they did me,
	the favour I promised when I received it — in full.

Chorus.
	Come, my son, come! Even if you are out on the headland,
	in the hollow, making the altar holy
	with an ox for Poseidon, god of the sea — come.
	The stranger thinks you and your city and your people
	deserve a just return for what he was given.
	Hurry. Run, my lord.

(Theseus comes in.)

Theseus.
What is this noise again, all of you together — my own people plainly,
and the stranger as clear as they are? Was it a thunderbolt from Zeus,
or a burst of hail out of the rain? When a god sends a storm like this,
you can guess at anything.

Oedipus.
My lord, you have appeared just when I wanted you. Some god has made
this journey of yours a lucky one.

Theseus.
What is it now, son of Laius? What is new?

Oedipus.
My life is tipping on the scale. I want to die without cheating you and
this city of what I agreed to.

Theseus.
What proof of your death are you relying on?

Oedipus.
The gods themselves are the heralds. They are announcing it to me, and
not one of the signs that were laid down is false.

Theseus.
How do you mean, old man? What makes it plain?

Oedipus.
The thunder, crash after crash, and the many bolts of lightning
flashing from the hand that nobody has ever beaten.

Theseus.
You convince me. I have seen you prophesy a good deal, and none of it
false. Tell me what has to be done.

Oedipus.
I will teach you, son of Aegeus, what will be laid up for this city and
will never grow old or bring you pain. I myself, now, with no hand to
guide me, will lead the way to the place where I must die. Never tell
any human being where it is — neither where it is hidden nor what part
of the country it lies in — so that it may stand for you always as a
defence, better than many shields, better than allied spears brought in
from your neighbours. The things too holy to be stirred by speech you
will learn for yourself when you come to that place alone. I cannot
speak them to any of these citizens, nor to my own children, though I
love them. Keep them yourself, always; and when you come to the end of
your life, tell them to your eldest son alone, and let him show them in
turn to the one who comes after him, for ever. That way you will keep
this city safe from the men who grew from the dragon's teeth. Countless
cities, however well they are governed, slip easily into outrage. The
gods see well, but they see late, when a man throws away what is theirs
and turns to madness. Do not want that to happen to you, son of Aegeus.
But I am teaching a man what he knows already.

Now, the place — the god's presence is pressing me — let us go, and not
hold back any longer. Children, follow me, this way. It is my turn now.
I have come out as a new guide for the two of you, as you were for your
father. Come. Do not touch me. Let me find the holy tomb by myself,
where it is allotted that this man be hidden in this earth. This way.
Here — this way. Hermes the guide is leading me this way, and the
goddess of the dead. Light — no light to me — once, somewhere, you were
mine; now my body feels you for the last time. I am going now to hide
the end of my life in the house of Hades. Dearest of hosts — you, and
this land, and your people — may you be happy; and in your good fortune
remember me when I am dead, and be fortunate for ever.

(He goes into the grove, leading. His daughters, Theseus and his men
follow him.)

Chorus.
	If it is right for me to honour with my prayers
	the goddess no one sees,
	and you, lord of the things of the night,
	Aidoneus, Aidoneus — I beg you:
	let the stranger come to the end
	without pain, without a death
	of heavy groaning,
	to the plain of the dead below that hides everything,
	and the house of the Styx.
	Many were the sufferings that came to him
	for nothing. Let a just god
	raise him up again.

Chorus.
	Goddesses under the earth,
	and you, the body of the beast nobody has beaten,
	who lie at the gates where so many come,
	and snarl out of the cave —
	the untamed guard beside Hades,
	so the story has always gone:
	I pray to you, Death, child of Earth and Tartarus,
	that the beast leave the way clear
	for the stranger as he sets out
	for the plain of the dead below.
	You are the one I call on,
	the sleep that does not end.

(A Messenger comes out of the grove.)

Messenger.
Citizens — the shortest way to tell it is to say that Oedipus is dead.
But what happened cannot be told in a few words, and what was done
there was not small either.

Chorus.
He is dead, then — the poor man?

Messenger.
You can be sure that he has left this everyday life behind him.

Chorus.
How? By some god's doing, and without pain, poor man?

Messenger.
That is exactly the part worth wondering at. How he set out from here
you know yourself; you were here. No one of his friends leading him —
he himself leading all of us. When he had come to the steep path down,
the one rooted into the earth with steps of bronze, he stopped on one
of the many branching tracks, near the hollow basin in the rock where
the pact of Theseus and Peirithous is kept, faithful for ever. He stood
midway between that basin and the Thorician rock, between the hollow
wild pear and the tomb of stone, and there he sat down, and undid his
filthy clothes. Then he called his daughters and told them to bring
water from somewhere running, for washing and for an offering. The two
of them went to the hill in sight of us, the hill of Demeter of the
green shoots, and did what their father asked in a short time; they
washed him and dressed him the way it is done.

When he had had everything he wanted done, and nothing he had asked for
was left undone, Zeus under the earth thundered. The girls shivered
when they heard it. They fell at their father's knees and wept, and did
not stop beating their breasts and wailing at full length. And when he
heard that sudden bitter sound he folded his arms round them and said:
"Children, on this day you have no father any more. All that was mine
is gone now, and you will not have the hard work of looking after me
any longer. It was hard, I know, children. But one word alone undoes
all that labour. Love — no one ever gave you more of that than this man
did; and now you will be without him for the rest of your lives."

Messenger.
Like that, clinging to one another, all of them sobbed and wept. But
when they came to the end of their weeping and the sound had died away,
there was silence. And suddenly a voice called out to him, so that
every one of us stood with our hair on end in a sudden terror. The god
called him, again and again, from every side: "You — you there,
Oedipus. Why are we waiting to go? You have kept us too long already."
When he understood it was a god calling him, he asked for the lord of
the land to come to him, Theseus himself. And when Theseus came, he
said: "Dear friend, give me the pledge of your right hand, the one an
oath is sworn on, for my children — and you, children, give yours to
him. Promise never to betray these girls of your own free will, and
always to do whatever, in your good judgement, is for their good." And
he, like the fine man he is, without any show of pity, swore to the
stranger that he would do it.

When that was done, Oedipus at once felt for his children with his
blind hands, and said: "Children, you must bear it bravely in your
hearts and go away from this place. Do not think it right to look at
what must not be seen, or to hear people saying what must not be
heard. Go, as fast as you can. Only Theseus, who has the right, must
stay and learn what is done here." That much we all heard him say, and
we went with the girls, groaning, our tears running unchecked. When we
had gone a little way, in a short time we turned and looked back — and
the man was nowhere. Not there at all any more. Only the king, alone,
holding his hand up in front of his face to shade his eyes, as if
something terrible had appeared, something not to be looked at. And
then, in a very short while, we saw him bowing down to the earth and
to Olympus of the gods in one and the same prayer.

By what death that man died, no mortal could tell you, except Theseus
alone. No fiery thunderbolt of the god took him off; no whirlwind rose
from the sea in that hour. Either some escort came from the gods, or
the dark floor of the underworld opened kindly for him, without pain.
The man was sent on his way with no grieving, no sickness, no
suffering — and if any mortal ever was, he was a wonder. If I seem to
anyone to be talking nonsense, I would not try to win over a man who
thinks I am a fool.

Chorus.
Where are his daughters, and the friends who went with them?

Messenger.
Not far. The sound of their grief is plain enough. It tells you they
are coming this way.

(Antigone and Ismene come in.)

Antigone.
	Aiai. Oh — now, now it is ours,
	the two of us, and not one thing only but all of it:
	to weep for the cursed blood in our father,
	planted in us, doomed as we are —
	the father for whom we carried the long labour,
	steady, all that time,
	and now at the last we bring back
	what cannot be reckoned:
	what we saw and what we suffered.

Chorus.
	What is it?

Antigone.
	You can guess it, friends.

Chorus.
	He is gone?

Antigone.
	Just as you would most wish, if you were wishing.
	How not? No war god met him,
	and no sea;
	the plains that no eye can see took him,
	carried off in some death nobody witnessed.
	Wretched — and on our eyes
	a night of destruction has come down.
	How are we to find the hard bread of life,
	wandering some far land,
	or the swell of the sea?

Ismene.
	I do not know. Let Hades, the killer,
	take me down to die with my old father.
	I am wretched. The life ahead of me
	is not a life to be lived.

Chorus.
	Best pair of daughters —
	what a god brings, you must bear.
	Do not burn too hot with grief.
	You have come through nothing to be blamed for.

Antigone.
	So there was a longing even for troubles.
	What was in no way dear was dear,
	all the while I held him in my arms.
	Father — dear one —
	you who wear the dark under the earth for ever,
	even down there you will never be
	unloved by me and by her.

Chorus.
	He did it?

Antigone.
	He did what he wanted.

Chorus.
	What was that?

Antigone.
	He died on the foreign ground he wanted.
	He has his bed under the earth,
	well shaded, for ever,
	and he did not leave a grief behind him unwept.
	This eye of mine, father, weeps for you
	and goes on weeping. I do not know
	how I am to make a pain this size
	disappear. Oh — you wanted
	to die on foreign ground,
	but you died like this, with no one by you. Not me.

Ismene.
	Wretched — what lot is waiting
	for you and me now, dear one,
	left like this without a father?

Chorus.
	But since he untied the end of his life
	in blessedness, friends,
	stop this grieving. Nobody
	is out of the reach of harm.

Antigone.
	Let us hurry back, dear one.

Ismene.
	To do what?

Antigone.
	A longing has hold of me.

Ismene.
	For what?

Antigone.
	To see the hearth under the earth —

Ismene.
	Whose?

Antigone.
	Our father's. Oh, I am wretched.

Ismene.
	How can that be allowed? Surely you see —

Antigone.
	Why do you scold me?

Ismene.
	And this too —

Antigone.
	What now? What is it this time?

Ismene.
	He fell without a tomb, apart from everyone.

Antigone.
	Take me there, and then kill me too.

Ismene.
	Aiai. Wretched past bearing — where, now,
	alone like this, with no way out,
	am I to live my hard life?

Chorus.
	Girls — do not be afraid.

Antigone.
	But where am I to run?

Chorus.
	Before this, something escaped —

Antigone.
	What?

Chorus.
	Your affairs. They escaped falling out badly.

Antigone.
	I know.

Chorus.
	Then what is it you are thinking?

Antigone.
	How we are to get home — I do not know.

Chorus.
	Do not even look for it.

Antigone.
	Hardship has hold of us.

Chorus.
	It had before, too.

Antigone.
	Then it was past bearing. Now it is worse than that.

Chorus.
	A great sea of it is what you two were allotted.

Antigone.
	Yes. Yes.

Chorus.
	I say so too.

Antigone.
	Aiai — where are we to go, Zeus?
	To what hope, of all that are left,
	is the god driving me now?

(Theseus comes in.)

Theseus.
Stop your wailing, children. Where the favour of the night below is
stored up as a treasure shared by all, grieving is not right. It brings
retribution.

Antigone.
Son of Aegeus, we fall at your feet.

Theseus.
To have what done, children?

Antigone.
We want to see our father's tomb ourselves, with our own eyes.

Theseus.
That is not allowed.

Antigone.
What do you mean, my lord — ruler of Athens?

Theseus.
	Children, he forbade me
	to let anyone come near that place,
	or any mortal call out over
	the holy tomb that holds him.
	If I did that well, he said,
	I would keep this country free of pain for ever.
	The god heard me say it,
	and the Oath of Zeus, that hears all things.

Antigone.
	If that is what he had in mind,
	then it must be enough for us.
	But send us to Thebes, old Thebes,
	in case we can somehow stop
	the killing that is coming
	for our brothers.

Theseus.
	I will do that, and everything else
	I can do that is of use to you
	and pleasing to the man under the earth
	who has just gone from us.
	I must not tire of it.

Chorus.
	Stop now. Do not raise
	the lament any higher.
	All this stands settled. It holds.
