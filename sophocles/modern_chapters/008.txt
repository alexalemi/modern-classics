Oedipus the King
(Part 3 of 3)

Oedipus.
And how can the man who fathered me count for nothing?

Messenger.
Because he did not father you. No more than I did.

Oedipus.
Then why did he call me his son?

Messenger.
You should know this: he had you as a gift. Out of my hands.

Oedipus.
And he loved me that much — a child from another man's hands?

Messenger.
He had no children before. That is what won him over.

Oedipus.
And you — had you bought me, or found me, when you gave me to him?

Messenger.
Found you. In the wooded folds of Cithaeron.

Oedipus.
What were you doing, travelling in that country?

Messenger.
I was in charge of the mountain flocks there.

Oedipus.
A shepherd. A hired hand, wandering about for wages?

Messenger.
And the man who saved your life, child, at the time.

Oedipus.
Saved me from what? What was wrong with me when you took me in your
arms?

Messenger.
The joints of your ankles could tell you that.

Oedipus.
Oh — why do you bring that up? That old injury?

Messenger.
I set you free. Your feet had been pinned through at the ankles.

Oedipus.
A terrible mark of shame to carry out of the cradle.

Messenger.
So terrible that it gave you your name — Oedipus, the swollen foot.
That piece of luck is what you are called after.

Oedipus.
In the gods' name — who did it? My mother or my father? Tell me.

Messenger.
I do not know. The man who gave you to me knows more about that than
I do.

Oedipus.
You got me from somebody else? You did not find me yourself?

Messenger.
No. Another shepherd handed you over to me.

Oedipus.
Who? Can you say who he was?

Messenger.
He was called one of Laius's men, I think.

Oedipus.
Laius — the king who ruled this country, years ago?

Messenger.
That is the one. The man was a herdsman of his.

Oedipus.
Is he still alive? Could I see him?

Messenger.
You people who live here would know that best.

Oedipus.
Is there anyone here — any of you standing near me — who knows the
herdsman he is talking about? Who has seen him, out in the fields or
here in the town? Speak up. The moment has come for this to be found
out.

Chorus.
I think it is nobody but the man from the fields, the one you were
already asking to see. But Jocasta here could tell you that as well as
anyone.

Oedipus.
Wife — you know the man we sent for just now? Is that who he means?

Jocasta.
What does it matter who he means? Pay no attention. What has been said
— do not waste a thought on it. It is nothing.

Oedipus.
Impossible. I have clues like these in my hands, and I am not going to
bring my birth into the open?

Jocasta.
In the gods' name, if you care at all for your own life, stop looking.
I am sick enough already.

Oedipus.
Take heart. Even if I turn out to be a slave, born of a slave, and her
mother a slave before her — three generations of it — you are not shown
to be low-born.

Jocasta.
Even so, listen to me. I am begging you. Do not do this.

Oedipus.
I will not listen. Not until I know the whole thing for certain.

Jocasta.
I know what I am saying. I am telling you what is best for you.

Oedipus.
Then this "best" of yours has been torturing me for a long time.

Jocasta.
Oh, you unlucky man. I hope you never find out who you are.

Oedipus.
Somebody go and bring the herdsman here to me. And let her go on
enjoying her rich family.

Jocasta.
Oh — oh, you poor wretched man. That is all I can call you. That, and
nothing else, ever again.

(She goes into the palace.)

Chorus.
Why has your wife gone, Oedipus, rushing off like that, in that wild
grief? I am afraid something terrible is going to break out of that
silence.

Oedipus.
Let it break out. Whatever it likes. My own seed — I want to see what
it is, however small it turns out to be. She, perhaps — she has a
woman's big pride — is ashamed of my low birth. But I count myself the
child of Fortune, the Fortune who gives good things, and I will not be
dishonoured. She is the mother I was born from. And the months, my
brothers, have marked me out small and then great. That is what I am,
and I could never come out of it a different man. So I will find out
where I come from.

Chorus.
	If I am any prophet, if I know anything at all,
	then, Cithaeron — by Olympus —
	you will not get through tomorrow's full moon
	without our praising you as Oedipus's countryman,
	his nurse and his mother,
	and dancing for you,
	because you did our king a kindness.
	Apollo, god we cry to — let this please you.

Chorus.
	Who was it, child, who was it bore you?
	One of the long-lived nymphs, lying with Pan,
	the mountain-walker, your father?
	Or some bride of Apollo? —
	every upland pasture is dear to him.
	Or was it the lord of Cyllene, Hermes,
	or the god of the Bacchants who lives on the high peaks,
	who took you as a gift, a find,
	from one of the nymphs of Helicon
	he plays with most?

Oedipus.
Elders — if I may make a guess about a man I have never dealt with, I
think I see the herdsman we have been looking for all this time. His
long old age is a match for this stranger's years; and besides, I
recognise the men bringing him as servants of my own. But you are
probably ahead of me in knowledge, if you have seen the herdsman
before.

Chorus.
I know him, be sure of it. He was Laius's man — as trusty a shepherd as
any.

(The old herdsman is brought in.)

Oedipus.
I ask you first, Corinthian stranger — is this the man you mean?

Messenger.
That is him. The one you are looking at.

Oedipus.
You. Old man. Look at me and answer what I ask you. Were you Laius's
man, once?

Servant.
I was. A slave — not bought, brought up in the house.

Oedipus.
Doing what work? What kind of life?

Servant.
Most of my life I went with the flocks.

Oedipus.
Where, mostly? What country did you keep them in?

Servant.
Cithaeron, some of the time. And the ground next to it.

Oedipus.
This man — do you know him? Did you ever meet him up there?

Servant.
Doing what? Which man do you mean?

Oedipus.
This one, here. Have you ever had anything to do with him?

Servant.
Not that I could say straight off, from memory.

Messenger.
No wonder, master. But I will remind him clearly of what he has
forgotten. I know very well he remembers when we were up in the
Cithaeron country, he with two flocks and I with one, and I was
alongside this man three whole seasons, six months each, from spring
until Arcturus rose. Then, for the winter, I used to drive mine down to
my own folds, and he took his to Laius's pens. Is any of this true, or
is it not? Did it happen as I say?

Servant.
It is true. Though it was a long time ago.

Messenger.
Come on, then. Tell me. Do you remember giving me a child, back then, to
bring up as my own?

Servant.
What is this? Why are you asking that?

Messenger.
This is him, my friend. The one who was a baby then.

Servant.
Damn you. Will you shut your mouth?

Oedipus.
Do not scold him, old man. Your words need a scolding a great deal
more than his.

Servant.
And how have I done wrong, best of masters?

Oedipus.
By not telling about the child he is asking after.

Servant.
He is talking without knowing anything. He is wasting his effort.

Oedipus.
You will not talk to oblige me. You will talk in pain.

Servant.
No — in the gods' name, do not hurt an old man.

Oedipus.
Somebody twist his arms behind him. Quickly.

Servant.
Why? What have I done? What do you want to know?

Oedipus.
The child he is asking about. Did you give it to him?

Servant.
I did. I wish I had died that day.

Oedipus.
You will come to that, if you do not tell me the truth.

Servant.
If I tell it, I am destroyed far worse.

Oedipus.
This man, it seems, is going to play for time.

Servant.
No, I am not. I said just now that I gave it to him.

Oedipus.
Where did you get it? Your own house, or somebody else's?

Servant.
Not mine. I took it from somebody.

Oedipus.
Which of these citizens? From what house?

Servant.
In the gods' name, master, no — do not ask any more.

Oedipus.
You are dead if I have to ask you again.

Servant.
Well then. It was one of the children of Laius's house.

Oedipus.
A slave's? Or one of his own blood?

Servant.
Oh — I am right at the terrible thing now. The saying of it.

Oedipus.
And I at the hearing of it. But it has to be heard.

Servant.
It was called his own child. But your wife inside could tell you best
how it stands.

Oedipus.
She gave it to you?

Servant.
Yes, my king.

Oedipus.
For what?

Servant.
So that I would get rid of it.

Oedipus.
Her own child? The wretched woman.

Servant.
She was afraid of the prophecies. Bad ones.

Oedipus.
What prophecies?

Servant.
That it would kill its parents. That was the story.

Oedipus.
Then why did you let it go to this old man?

Servant.
Out of pity, master. I thought he would carry it away to another
country, the one he came from. And he saved it — for the worst of all.
Because if you are the man he says you are, know that you were born to
a terrible fate.

Oedipus.
Oh. Oh. It has all come out. All of it, clear. Light — let this be the
last time I look at you. I have been shown for what I am: born from
those I should never have been born from, living with those I should
never have lived with, and the killer of those I should never have
killed.

(He goes into the palace.)

Chorus.
	Oh, generations of men,
	I count your lives as equal to nothing.
	Where is the man, where,
	who carries off more of happiness
	than just enough to seem to have it,
	and having seemed, to fall away?
	With your example before me,
	your fate, yours — Oedipus, you poor wretch —
	I call no mortal blessed.

Chorus.
	He drew his bow past everyone
	and won a fortune blessed in every way —
	O Zeus — he brought down
	the girl with the crooked claws,
	the singer of riddles, and rose up
	for my country as a tower against death.
	From that day you were called my king,
	and honoured above all men,
	ruling in great Thebes.

Chorus.
	And now — who is more wretched to hear of?
	Who lives closer to savage ruin, to pain,
	in the turning-over of a life?
	Oh, famous Oedipus,
	one harbour was enough —
	the same harbour, for you
	to put in at as son and as husband.
	How, how could the furrows your father ploughed
	bear you in silence, poor man, for so long?

Chorus.
	Time, who sees everything, has found you out
	against your will. He passes sentence
	on the marriage that was no marriage,
	where the one who begot was the one begotten.
	Oh, son of Laius,
	I wish, I wish I had never seen you.
	I mourn like a man pouring out a funeral song
	from his lips. To tell the plain truth:
	you gave me breath again,
	and you have closed my eyes in the dark.

(A second messenger comes out of the palace.)

Messenger.
You who have always been the most honoured men in this land — what
things you are going to hear, what things you will see, what grief you
will carry, if you still care, as kin should, for the house of
Labdacus. I do not think the Ister or the Phasis, the great rivers,
could wash this house clean, so much is hidden in it — and more is
about to come into the light, evils done on purpose, not by accident.
And of all hurts, the ones that hurt most are the ones we are shown to
have chosen ourselves.

Chorus.
What we knew before was already more than heavy enough to groan under.
What do you have to add to it?

Messenger.
The quickest thing to say, and to take in: Jocasta is dead. Our queen.

Chorus.
Poor, poor woman. What caused it?

Messenger.
Her own hand. The worst of what happened you are spared, because you
did not see it. Still, as far as my memory holds it, you will hear what
that wretched woman suffered.

She came in through the porch in a frenzy and went straight for her
marriage bed, tearing at her hair with the fingers of both hands. Once
inside, she slammed the doors shut behind her and called on Laius, dead
so long ago — remembering the child got years back, by whose hand he
died himself, leaving the mother to breed with his own son a cursed
brood. And she wept over the bed where, twice wretched, she had borne a
husband from a husband and children from her child.

How she died after that I do not know. Oedipus burst in, shouting, and
because of him we could not watch her misery through to the end. Our
eyes were on him as he ran round the room. He went from one of us to
the next demanding a sword, and asking where he could find the wife who
was no wife — the mother's field, sown twice over, where he and his
children had both been planted. And as he raved, some god pointed the way
for him. It was none of us men who were standing near. With a terrible
cry, as though somebody were leading him, he threw himself at the
double doors, bent the bolts out of their sockets, and fell into the
room.

And there we saw the woman hanging, her neck in a twisted noose of
swinging cords. When he saw her he gave a terrible deep roar, poor man,
and loosed the rope she hung from. And when she was lying on the
ground, poor wretch, what came next was terrible to see. He tore the
golden brooches from her dress — the pins she had fastened her clothes
with — lifted them, and struck them into his own eyes, into the
sockets, saying something like this: that they would not see him any
more, not what he was suffering and not what he was doing; that from
now on, in the dark, they would look at the people they should never
have seen, and fail to know the people he had wanted to know.

Chanting that, over and over, not once, he raised his hand and struck
his eyes again and again. And with every blow the bleeding eyeballs wet
his beard — not oozing slow drops of gore, but all at once a black rain
of blood, with hail in it, came pouring down.

This has broken out from two people, not one: evils mingled together
for husband and wife. Their old happiness, before, was a happiness
they had a right to. Now, on this one day: groaning, ruin, death,
shame — every name there is for evil, and not one of them missing.

Chorus.
And now? Is there any rest from the pain for the wretched man?

Messenger.
He is shouting for someone to open the doors and show all Thebes the
father-killer, the mother's — I cannot say the word; it is unholy — so
that he can throw himself out of the land and not stay in the house
under the curse he called down himself. But he needs strength, and
someone to guide him. The sickness is more than a man can carry. He
will show you himself. Look — the bolts of the doors are being drawn.
You are about to see a sight that even a man who hated him would pity.

(Oedipus comes out, blind. The blood is still on his face.)

Chorus.
	Oh, terrible thing for people to see,
	most terrible of everything
	I have ever come upon. Poor man,
	what madness came over you? What god
	took a leap longer than the longest
	and landed on your unlucky life?
	Oh, oh, you wretched man —
	I cannot even look at you. There is so much
	I want to ask, so much to learn,
	so much to look at,
	and you make me shudder so.

Oedipus.
	Aiai, aiai, I am wretched.
	Where am I being carried, poor man? Where
	is my voice flying off to, on the wind?
	Oh, my fate — how far you leapt.

Chorus.
	Into something terrible. Not to be heard. Not to be looked at.

Oedipus.
	Oh, cloud of darkness,
	my own, come over me, unspeakable, driven on
	by a wind that cannot be turned aside —
	oh, no —
	oh, no, again — how they go into me together,
	the stab of these pins and the memory of what I did.

Chorus.
	No wonder. In pain like this
	you mourn twice and carry the evil twice.

Oedipus.
	Oh, my friend —
	you are still here, still my attendant, still standing by,
	still putting up with the blind man, and caring for him.
	Oh, oh.
	You cannot hide from me. I know you plainly,
	dark as I am — I know your voice.

Chorus.
	You have done a terrible thing. How could you bear
	to put out your own eyes? Which of the gods drove you?

Oedipus.
	It was Apollo, friends, Apollo
	who brought it to pass, this evil, evil suffering of mine.
	But no hand struck them except my own — mine, wretched as I am.
	Why should I have eyes,
	when there was nothing sweet left for them to see?

Chorus.
	It was just as you say.

Oedipus.
	Then what is there for me to see,
	or to love, or what greeting
	could still touch my ear with any pleasure, friends?
	Take me away, out of this country, as fast as you can,
	take me away, friends — the great destruction,
	the man most cursed, and more than that
	the man the gods hate most.

Chorus.
	Wretched in your mind and in your fortune, both alike —
	I wish I had never known you.

Oedipus.
	Death to him, whoever he was,
	who took the cruel pin out of my feet on the mountain
	and rescued me from death and saved me. He did me no kindness.
	If I had died then
	I would not be this grief to those who love me, or to myself.

Chorus.
	I would have wanted that too.

Oedipus.
	I would not have come here my father's killer,
	or been called by men the bridegroom
	of the woman I was born from.
	Now I am godless, the child of unholy parents,
	sharing the bed of the man who begot me, poor wretch.
	If there is an evil older than evil,
	Oedipus has drawn it.

Chorus.
I cannot say you planned this well. You would have been better off dead
than alive and blind.

Oedipus.
Do not lecture me that this was not the best way to do it. Do not give
me any more advice. If I had eyes, I do not know how I could have looked
at my father with them when I went down to Hades, or at my poor mother
— I have done things to those two that no hanging could pay for. Or the
sight of my children — was that something I would have wanted to look
at, born the way they were born? Never. Not with these eyes. Nor the
city, nor its walls, nor the holy statues of the gods — all of which I,
the most wretched of men, I who was brought up in Thebes the finest man
in it, took from myself when I gave the order that everyone must drive
out the impious man, the one the gods have shown to be unclean, and of
the family of Laius. Having exposed a stain like that on myself, was I
to look at these people with steady eyes? No. And if there were any
way to dam up the spring of hearing through my ears, I would not have
held back from shutting this wretched body up altogether, so that it
was blind and heard nothing. It is sweet for the mind to live outside
its troubles.

Cithaeron, why did you take me in? Why did you not kill me the moment
you had me, so that I would never have shown men where I came from?
Polybus, Corinth, and the old house I called my father's — what a
beautiful thing you brought up in me, with evil festering under the
skin. Now I am found evil, and born of evil. You three roads, and the
hidden glen, the oak wood and the narrow place where the three ways
met, that drank my father's blood from my own hands — do you still
remember me? What I did in front of you, and what I went on to do
after, when I came here? Marriage — marriage, you gave me life, and
having given it you sent the same seed up again, and showed the world
fathers, brothers, sons, one blood; brides, wives, mothers; every
foulest thing that is done among human beings. But it is not right to
speak of what it was not right to do. Quickly, in the gods' name, hide
me somewhere outside the land, or kill me, or throw me into the sea,
where you will never have to look at me again. Come — do not think it
beneath you to touch a wretched man. Do as I ask, do not be afraid. No
man can carry my evils except me.

Chorus.
Here is Creon, at the right moment for what you ask — to act, and to
decide. He is left the only guardian of the country in your place.

Oedipus.
Oh — what am I going to say to him? What claim have I on his trust that
would be just? Everything I did to him before has been shown to be
wrong.

(Creon comes in.)

Creon.
I have not come to laugh at you, Oedipus, or to throw any of the old
wrongs in your face. But you men — if you have no shame left before the
children of men, at least respect the flame of the lord Sun, who feeds
everything, and do not show this pollution uncovered like this, a thing
that neither the earth nor the holy rain nor the light will accept.
Take him into the house as fast as you can. It is right that only
family should see and hear a family's troubles.

Oedipus.
In the gods' name — since you have torn away what I expected, and
come, the best of men, to me, the worst — do one thing for me. I say
it for your sake, not mine.

Creon.
What is it you want from me so badly?

Oedipus.
Throw me out of this land, as fast as you can, to a place where no
human being will ever speak to me.

Creon.
I would have done it, be sure of that, if I had not wanted first of
all to learn from the god what should be done.

Oedipus.
But his word came out in full: destroy the father-killer, the unholy
one — me.

Creon.
That is what was said. All the same, where we now stand, it is better
to find out clearly what to do.

Oedipus.
You will ask the god? For a wretch like me?

Creon.
Yes. Even you will put your trust in the god now.

Oedipus.
Yes. And I lay this charge on you. I beg it of you. The woman inside —
bury her as you think right. You will do it properly; she is your own.
But me — never let this city, my father's, be condemned to have me
living in it. Let me live in the mountains, on the Cithaeron that is
called mine, that my mother and father while they lived set aside as my
proper tomb, so that I die by the will of the two who tried to kill me.
And yet I know this much: no sickness will destroy me, and nothing
else will either. I would never have been saved from dying, except for
some terrible evil. Well, let my fate go where it will.

My children, Creon. Do not trouble yourself over the boys. They are
men; wherever they are, they will never lack a living. But my two
girls, poor pitiable things — my table was never set apart from them,
never without me; whatever I touched, they always had a share in it —
take care of those two for me. And above all let me touch them with my
hands and weep over what has happened. Do it, my lord. Do it, you
noble-born man. If I touched them with my hands I would think I had
them, as I did when I could see.

What is this? In the gods' name — is that not my two dear ones I hear,
crying? Has Creon pitied me and sent my children, the dearest of all my
children? Am I right?

Creon.
You are. I arranged it. I knew the joy you had in them — the joy you
have had all along.

Oedipus.
Then may good fortune come to you, and for this road may some god
guard you better than he guarded me. Children, where are you? Come
here. Come to these hands, your brother's hands — hands that did this
to the eyes of the father who begot you, eyes that were bright once. He
saw nothing, girls, and knew nothing, and became your father in the
woman he was sown in himself. I weep for you both as well — I have no
strength to look at you — when I think of the rest of the bitter life
you will have to live among men. What gatherings of the citizens will
you go to, what festivals, that you will not come home from in tears,
instead of having watched the show? And when you reach the age for
marriage — who will he be, girls, who will take the risk, taking on the
disgrace that will wound my children and yours alike? What evil is
missing? Your father killed his father. He ploughed the mother he was
sown in, and got you from the same place he was born from. That is what
they will throw at you. And then who will marry you? Nobody. There is
nobody, children. You are going to wither, barren and unmarried. That
is plain.

Son of Menoeceus — you are the only father left to them; the two of us
who made them are gone, both of us. Do not look on while they go
wandering, beggars, unmarried, without a home — they are your own blood
— and do not put them on a level with my evils. Pity them. Look at
them, so young, stripped of everything except what you can give. Nod to
me, noble man. Give me your hand on it.

And you, children — if you were old enough to understand, I would have
a great deal to tell you. As it is, pray this for me: to live wherever
the moment allows, and to have a better life than the father who begot
you.

Creon.
Enough. You have wept enough. Go inside the house.

Oedipus.
I must obey. Though nothing about it is sweet.

Creon.
Everything is good in its season.

Oedipus.
Do you know on what terms I go?

Creon.
You will tell me, and then I will know.

Oedipus.
That you send me out of this land, to live away from it.

Creon.
What you ask is the god's to give.

Oedipus.
But I have become the thing the gods hate most.

Creon.
Then you will get it soon enough.

Oedipus.
You agree?

Creon.
I do not like to say things I do not mean. It is empty talk.

Oedipus.
Then take me away from here. Now.

Creon.
Come on, then. Let go of the children.

Oedipus.
No — do not take these from me.

Creon.
Do not try to be master of everything. What you did master has not
stayed with you through your life.

(Oedipus is led inside.)

Chorus.
People of Thebes, our own country, look: this is Oedipus, who knew the
famous riddle and was the most powerful of men. Which citizen did not
look at his fortune with envy? See what a sea of terrible trouble he
has come into. So, while a mortal is still waiting to see that final
day, count no one happy until he has crossed the boundary of his life
without suffering pain.
