Philoctetes
(Part 1 of 3)

(A rocky shore on Lemnos. Odysseus and Neoptolemus have just landed,
with one sailor. Odysseus keeps back from the rocks.)

Odysseus.
This is the shore of Lemnos. The sea goes all the way round it, and no
one walks here and no one lives here. It was here, Neoptolemus — son of
Achilles, raised by the strongest father any Greek ever had — that I
once put ashore the man from Malis, the son of Poeas, and left him. I
was ordered to do it by the men in command. His foot was dripping with
a disease that was eating it away, and we could not so much as touch a
drink-offering or a sacrifice in peace: he kept the whole camp in an
uproar with his wild, unlucky cries, howling and groaning. But why go
over that? This is no moment for long speeches. If he finds out I am
here, I throw away the whole trick I mean to catch him with, and I mean
to catch him fast.

Now the work is yours: help me with what is left. Look for a rock
somewhere here with two mouths — a cave shaped so that in cold weather
there are two places to sit in the sun, and in summer a breeze blows
through the tunnel from end to end and brings sleep with it. A little
below it, on the left, you may see water running from a spring, if it
is still there. Go up quietly and signal to me whether he is still in
the same place or somewhere else, so that you can hear the rest of the
plan, and I can lay it out, and the two of us can carry it together.

Neoptolemus.
King Odysseus, the job you describe is not far off. I think I can see a
cave like the one you mean.

Odysseus.
Above you or below? I cannot make it out.

Neoptolemus.
Here, up above. And there is no sound of footsteps.

Odysseus.
Make sure he is not lying in there asleep.

Neoptolemus.
I see an empty dwelling. There is nobody inside.

Odysseus.
And nothing in there to make it a home?

Neoptolemus.
A bed of leaves, trodden down, as if someone sleeps here.

Odysseus.
The rest bare? Nothing else under the roof?

Neoptolemus.
A cup made out of a single piece of wood — some bad craftsman's work —
and things for lighting a fire beside it.

Odysseus.
That is his treasure you are describing.

Neoptolemus.
Ugh. And here are more of them: rags drying in the sun, full of some
heavy sickness.

Odysseus.
The man lives in this place, that is clear, and he is not far off. How
could a sick man walk far, with that old ruin in his leg? Either he has
gone out to find food, or he knows of some leaf somewhere that dulls
the pain. Send the man you have with you to keep watch, so that he does
not come on me unawares. He would rather have me than all the Greeks
together.

Neoptolemus.
He is going, and the path will be watched. Now, if you need anything
more, tell me the rest.

(The sailor goes.)

Odysseus.
Son of Achilles, you must be true to your birth for the job you came on
— and not just with your body. If you hear something new, something
you have not been told before, you must serve it. You are here to
serve.

Neoptolemus.
What are you ordering me to do, then?

Odysseus.
The mind of Philoctetes. You have to steal it out of him with a story,
by cunning, as you talk to him. When he asks who you are and where you
come from, say: the son of Achilles. There is nothing to hide in that.
But say you are sailing home. You have left the Greek fleet and the
army; you hate them with a great hatred. They sent for you, begging you
to come from home, because you were their only hope of taking Troy —
and when you came and claimed the arms of Achilles, which were yours by
right, they judged you not worth them and handed them to Odysseus. Say
what you like about me: the worst of the worst. You will not hurt me
with any of it. But if you do not do this, you will bring grief on
every Greek there is. If this man's bow is not taken, you cannot sack
the land of Dardanus.

And let me explain why you can go up to him safely and be trusted, and
I cannot. You sailed to Troy under oath to nobody, under no compulsion,
and not with the first expedition. I can deny none of those things. So
if he sees me while he is still master of his bow, I am dead, and I
will take you down with me for being at my side. No — this is the very
thing we have to be clever about: how you are to become the thief of
those arms that cannot be beaten.

I know, boy. You were not born to say things like this or to scheme
harm. But it is a sweet thing to take the prize of victory. Bring
yourself to it. We will show ourselves honest men another time. For
now, give yourself to me for one small, shameless part of one day, and
then, for the rest of time, be called the most righteous man alive.

Neoptolemus.
Son of Laertes, the things I hate hearing said, I hate doing. It is not
my nature to get anything by a dirty trick — not mine, and not, they
tell me, my father's, who begot me. But I am ready to take the man by
force, not by cunning. He has one foot to stand on; he is not going to
overpower this many of us by force. Still, I was sent here to work with
you, and I do not like the thought of being called a traitor. But I
would rather, my lord, do the right thing and fail than win by doing
wrong.

Odysseus.
Son of a good father — I was young once too, and I had a lazy tongue
and a busy hand. Now I have come out and been tested, and I see that
with men it is the tongue, not the deed, that leads everything.

Neoptolemus.
What are you ordering me to do, then, other than lie?

Odysseus.
I am telling you to take Philoctetes by cunning.

Neoptolemus.
Why cunning? Why not persuade him and bring him?

Odysseus.
He will never be persuaded. And you cannot take him by force.

Neoptolemus.
Is he really so terrifyingly strong that he dares anything?

Odysseus.
Arrows you cannot escape, and death goes ahead of them.

Neoptolemus.
So a man cannot even go near him?

Odysseus.
No. Not unless you take him by cunning, as I say.

Neoptolemus.
You do not think it is shameful, then, to tell lies?

Odysseus.
Not if the lie brings us through safe.

Neoptolemus.
How does a man look him in the face and dare to say it?

Odysseus.
When what you do is for gain, there is no place for hanging back.

Neoptolemus.
And what gain is it to me that he should come to Troy?

Odysseus.
That bow, and nothing else, takes Troy.

Neoptolemus.
Then I am not the one who will sack it, as you told me?

Odysseus.
Not you without the bow, and not the bow without you.

Neoptolemus.
Then it has to be hunted down, if that is how things stand.

Odysseus.
Do this, and you carry off two prizes.

Neoptolemus.
What are they? Tell me, and I will not refuse to do it.

Odysseus.
You will be called clever and a good man, both at once.

Neoptolemus.
All right. I will do it, and throw off all shame.

Odysseus.
You remember what I told you to say?

Neoptolemus.
Be sure of it. I agreed, once and for all.

Odysseus.
You stay here, then, and wait for him. I am going, so that I am not
seen here with you, and I will send the lookout back to the ship. And
if the two of you seem to me to be wasting time, I will send that same
man back here again, disguised as the captain of a merchant ship, so
that nobody knows him. He will talk in a roundabout way; take from what
he says whatever is useful at the moment, my boy. I am going to the
ship, and I leave all this to you. May Hermes the Deceiver, who sends
us on this errand, be our guide — and Victory, Athena of the City, who
keeps me safe, always.

(He goes. The Chorus comes in: sailors from Scyros, Neoptolemus's own
men.)

Chorus.
	What must I, what must I do, master —
	a stranger in a strange land —
	what must I hide, what say
	to a man who will suspect us?
	Tell me. One man's skill
	outreaches another's skill,
	and so does his judgment, when he holds
	the sceptre of Zeus that comes from the god.
	And to you, my boy, all that power
	has come down from the beginning.
	So tell me
	what I must do to serve you.

Neoptolemus.
	For now — since you may want to see
	the place at the island's edge where he lies —
	look, and do not be afraid.
	But when the terrible traveller
	comes back to this house of his,
	keep close to my hand, watch it,
	and try to do what the moment needs.

Chorus.
	That care has been my care a long time, my lord:
	to keep my eye, before everything, on your good.
	Now tell me. What sort of house
	does he live in, sitting there,
	and what ground does he hold?
	It is not out of season for me to know,
	or he may fall on me from somewhere unseen.
	What is the place? Where does he sit?
	Where does he walk — inside, or out of doors?

Neoptolemus.
	You see his house, with its two doors —
	a bed in the rock.

Chorus.
	And the poor wretch himself — where has he gone?

Neoptolemus.
	It is plain to me that he is dragging
	a track along somewhere near here, after food.
	That, they say, is the way he lives:
	shooting animals with feathered arrows,
	a wretch, and wretchedly,
	and nobody comes near him
	with a cure for his troubles.

Chorus.
	I pity him, for my part.
	No one to care for him,
	no companion's face to look at,
	miserable, always alone,
	he is sick with a savage sickness
	and at a loss at every need
	as it comes. How, how does the unlucky man hold out?
	Oh, the contrivances of the gods.
	Oh, the unhappy race of men,
	whose life is never measured right.

Chorus.
	This man — second, it may be,
	to no one of the oldest houses —
	lies with no share in anything life gives,
	alone, apart from other men,
	among dappled beasts and shaggy ones,
	pitiful in his pain and in his hunger both,
	with troubles nothing can cure. And Echo
	of the mountains, her mouth never shut,
	heard from far off, answers back
	his bitter cries.

Neoptolemus.
	None of this is a wonder to me.
	It was gods' work, if I understand anything —
	the suffering that came at him
	from cruel-hearted Chryse,
	and what he suffers now, with no one to tend him.
	It cannot be but some god is seeing to it
	that he should not aim at Troy
	the god's arrows nobody can fight
	before the time is out
	when, they say, Troy has to fall to them.

Chorus.
	Quiet, boy.

Neoptolemus.
	What is it?

Chorus.
	A sound came up —
	the kind that lives with a man being worn down —
	from over there, or there.
	It strikes me, strikes me true,
	the voice of someone crawling
	along his track because he must.
	I cannot miss it, far off as it is,
	that heavy voice of a man worn out.
	He cries out, and it is clear.

Chorus.
	But listen, my boy —

Neoptolemus.
	Say it.

Chorus.
	— to a new thought.
	The man is not away from home. He is here,
	and not with the music of a pipe,
	like a shepherd with his flocks in the fields —
	no: stumbling, it may be, because he must,
	he cries a cry that carries far,
	or he is looking out at the harbour
	no ship comes into. He is calling out something terrible.

(Philoctetes comes in, from the cave side, dragging his foot.)

Philoctetes.
You — strangers. Who are you? What country have you put in from, to
this land with no harbour and no one living in it? What homeland, what
people, should I guess you belong to? The cut of your clothes is Greek,
and nothing is dearer to my eyes. But I want to hear your voices. Do
not shrink back from me in fear, do not be frightened because I have
gone wild to look at. Pity a man who is miserable and alone, cast away
like this, with no one of his own, ill-used. Speak to me — if you have
come here as friends. Answer me. It is not right that I should fail to
get that much from you, or you from me.

Neoptolemus.
Then know this first, stranger: we are Greeks. That is what you want to
learn.

Philoctetes.
Oh, the dearest sound there is. To be greeted by such a man — after so
long! What need brought you in to shore, my boy, what carried you here?
What errand? Which wind, the kindest of them all? Tell me all of it, so
that I know who you are.

Neoptolemus.
My people are from Scyros, the island the sea runs round. I am sailing
home. I am called the son of Achilles — Neoptolemus. Now you know all
of it.

Philoctetes.
Son of the father I loved best, son of a country I loved — the old man
Lycomedes' fosterling. On what voyage did you touch this land? Where
have you sailed from?

Neoptolemus.
From Troy, as it happens. That is where I am sailing from now.

Philoctetes.
What? You were never on board with us at the start, when the fleet went
out to Troy.

Neoptolemus.
Were you part of that labour too?

Philoctetes.
My boy — do you not know who you are looking at?

Neoptolemus.
How could I know a man I have never once seen?

Philoctetes.
Not even my name? You never heard a word of what happened to me, the
troubles I have been dying of?

Neoptolemus.
Take it that I know nothing of what you are asking.

Philoctetes.
Oh, I am wretched many times over; the gods have turned bitter against
me. Not a rumour of the state I am in has gone home, or reached any
corner of Greece. The men who threw me out — an unholy act — keep quiet
and laugh, and my sickness flourishes and keeps on growing.

My boy, son of Achilles your father: I am the man — you may have heard
of him — who is master of the bow of Heracles. Philoctetes, the son of
Poeas. The two commanders and the king of the Cephallenians, Odysseus,
threw me out here in disgrace, alone as you see me, wasting away with a
savage disease, bitten with the savage bite of the snake that kills
men. With that for company, boy, they set me down here and went away
and left me alone, when they had put in here with the fleet from Chryse
out in the sea. They were glad, then, when they saw me asleep on the
shore under the roof of the rock, after all the tossing at sea, and
they left me and went — setting out a few rags first, as you would for
a beggar who has come down in the world, and a little something to eat
as well. A small charity. I hope they get the same.

Boy, can you picture what it was to get up out of sleep that day, when
they were gone? What tears I cried, what evils I howled over — seeing
the ships I had sailed with all gone, and not a man in the place, not
one who could help me, not one who would take a hand with me in my
sickness. I looked everywhere and found nothing there but pain — and
plenty of that, my boy.

(He stops for a moment, then goes on.)

Philoctetes.
So time went by for me, season after season, and in this narrow house I
had to be my own servant, alone. For the belly, this bow found what was
needed; it brought down the doves on the wing. And whatever the arrow
struck, off the bowstring, I would crawl to it myself, in misery,
dragging my wretched foot behind me. If I had to get a drink, or break
a bit of wood when the frost was spread on the ground, as it is in
winter, I would creep out in pain and manage it somehow. And then there
would be no fire; but by grinding stone on stone I would at last bring
out the hidden spark, and that is what keeps me alive, always. A roof
to live under, and fire — that supplies everything except an end to
being sick.

Now, my boy, let me tell you about the island. No sailor comes near it
by choice. There is no anchorage, nowhere he can sail to and sell a
cargo at a profit or be put up by a host. Men with any sense do not
make their voyages here. But suppose someone is driven in against his
will — in the long course of a man's life such things happen often
enough. When they come, my boy, they have pitying words for me, and
perhaps in their pity they have given me a share of food, or some
clothing. But the one thing nobody will do, when I bring it up, is take
me safely home. So I am dying, wretched, in this the tenth year now, of
hunger and of hardship, feeding this sickness that never stops eating.
That is what the sons of Atreus and the brute strength of Odysseus have
done to me, boy. May the gods on Olympus give them the same, some day,
in return for what I have suffered.

Chorus.
I think I pity you too, son of Poeas, as much as the strangers who came
before us.

Neoptolemus.
And I can be a witness to what you say myself. I know it is true. I
have met the wickedness of the sons of Atreus for myself, and the brute
strength of Odysseus.

Philoctetes.
What — do you have a complaint against those damned sons of Atreus as
well? Some wrong they did you, to be angry about?

Neoptolemus.
If only my hand could fill up my anger one day, so that Mycenae would
learn, and Sparta, that Scyros too is a mother of fighting men.

Philoctetes.
Well said, boy. What is it? Why have you come here with this great
anger, laying a charge against them?

Neoptolemus.
Son of Poeas, I will tell you — though it is hard to tell — how they
insulted me when I came. When fate decreed that Achilles should die —

Philoctetes.
Oh — say no more. Tell me this first: the son of Peleus is dead?

Neoptolemus.
Dead. Not by any man's hand, but a god's. Shot, they say — brought down
by Apollo.

Philoctetes.
Well. The one who killed and the one who died were both of good birth.
I do not know, boy, whether to ask about your wrong first or mourn for
him.

Neoptolemus.
I think your own pains are enough for you, poor man, without grieving
over your neighbour's.

Philoctetes.
You are right. So tell me your business again from the start — how
they outraged you.

Neoptolemus.
They came for me in a ship dressed out in colours, the great Odysseus
and the man who raised my father, and they said — whether it was true
or an empty story I do not know — that since my father was dead, it was
not lawful for anyone but me to take the towers of Troy. That was what
they told me, stranger, and it did not keep me long from sailing, and
fast. Mostly it was longing for the dead man: to see him before he was
buried, because I had never seen him. And then there was the fine story
on top of it, that if I went I would take the towers of Troy.

It was the second day of my voyage when a fair wind and the oars
brought me in to Sigeum — bitter Sigeum. As soon as I landed the whole
army came round me in a ring to welcome me, swearing they were looking
at Achilles alive again, the man who was gone. But he lay there dead.
And I, unlucky, when I had wept for him, went not long afterwards to
the sons of Atreus — my friends, as I had every reason to think — and
asked for my father's arms, and everything else that had been his. And
they said — oh, the most shameless thing anyone ever said: "Seed of
Achilles, everything else your father had, you may take. But another
man is master of those arms now: the son of Laertes." The tears came at
once and I jumped up in a heavy rage and said, in pain, "You wretch,
did you dare give my arms to somebody else instead of me, without
asking me?" And Odysseus — he happened to be standing near — said,
"Yes, boy. They gave them, and rightly. I was there and I saved them,
and saved him too." And I was furious, and at once I started lashing at
him with every insult there is, leaving nothing out, if he was going to
rob me of my arms. He had come to that point, and he is not a man who
angers easily, but stung by what he heard he answered like this: "You
were not where we were. You were away, when you were needed. And since
you have such a bold mouth, you will never sail back to Scyros with
those arms." That is what I heard, that is how I was abused, and I am
sailing home, robbed of what is mine by Odysseus himself, the worst man
of a bad line.

And I do not blame him as much as the men in charge. A city belongs
wholly to its leaders, and so does an army; and when men break the
rules, it is their teachers' words that made them bad. There. The whole
story is told. Whoever hates the sons of Atreus — may he be as dear to
the gods as he is to me.

Chorus.
	Earth of the mountains, feeder of all things,
	mother of Zeus himself,
	you who hold the great Pactolus and its gold —
	there too, mother, lady, I called on you
	when all the outrage of the sons of Atreus
	came down on this man,
	when they handed over his father's arms,
	the thing most to be honoured,
	to the son of Laertes.
	Oh blessed one, who rides
	on lions that kill bulls.

Philoctetes.
You have come to me, strangers, carrying a clear token of grief, it
seems — a grief that matches mine. You sing the same tune I do, and I
recognise the work: this is the sons of Atreus and Odysseus. I know
that man would set his tongue to any dirty story and any villainy that
would let him get to something wicked in the end. None of that
surprises me. What does is that the greater Ajax, if he was there,
could stand and watch it.

Neoptolemus.
He was no longer alive, friend. I would never have been robbed like
that while he lived.

Philoctetes.
What are you saying? He is dead and gone too?

Neoptolemus.
Think of him as one who no longer sees the light.

Philoctetes.
Oh, no. But the son of Tydeus, Diomedes, and the son of Sisyphus that
Laertes bought — Odysseus — they will never die. They are the ones who
should not be alive.

Neoptolemus.
No, they will not. Be sure of that. They are flourishing now, and
greatly, in the Greek army.

Philoctetes.
And what of my good old friend Nestor of Pylos — is he not alive? He
used to hold back their wickedness, with his wise advice.

Neoptolemus.
He is doing badly now. Antilochus, the son he had at his side, is dead
and gone from him.

Philoctetes.
Oh — two more men you have named that I would least have wanted to hear
were dead. Oh, no. What is a man to look for, when these are dead and
Odysseus is alive — here, again — when he should have been the one
reported dead in their place?

Neoptolemus.
He is a clever wrestler, that man. But even clever schemes,
Philoctetes, get tripped up often enough.

Philoctetes.
Tell me, in the gods' name — where was Patroclus when you needed him?
Your father loved him more than anyone.

Neoptolemus.
He was dead too. And in one short lesson I will teach you this: war
never takes a bad man if it can help it. It always takes the good ones.

Philoctetes.
I will be your witness on that. And on that exact point I will ask you
about a man of no worth — but terrible with his tongue, and clever. How
is he doing now?

Neoptolemus.
Who could you mean, but Odysseus?

Philoctetes.
I did not mean him. There was a man called Thersites, who would never
choose to speak once when he could speak again, even where nobody let
him speak at all. Do you know if he is alive?

Neoptolemus.
I never saw him. But I heard he is still alive.

Philoctetes.
He would be. Nothing bad has ever died yet. The gods wrap those up
carefully; somehow they take pleasure in turning the villains and the
twisted ones back from Hades, and the just and the good they are for
ever sending down. How am I to reckon this? How praise it, when I
praise the gods' ways and find the gods are bad?

Neoptolemus.
For my part, son of a father from Oeta, from now on I will keep a long
way off from Troy and from the sons of Atreus, and look at them from
there. Where the worse man is stronger than the good, where what is
decent goes to ruin and the coward wins — men like that I will never
love. Rocky Scyros will be enough for me from now on; I will enjoy my
home. Now I go to the ship. And you, son of Poeas — goodbye, all the
good there is, goodbye. May the gods take the sickness away from you,
as you wish. We must go, so that whenever the god lets us sail, we can
be on our way.

Philoctetes.
You are leaving already, boy?

Neoptolemus.
The right moment calls us to watch for the wind near the ship, not far
from it.

Philoctetes.
By your father, then, and by your mother, boy, and by everything at
home you love, I beg you as a suppliant: do not leave me alone like
this, deserted in these troubles — you see what they are like; you have
heard how many there are. Make me a side-job. There is a lot of
disgust, I know, in a cargo like me. Put up with it all the same. To a
man of good birth what is shameful is hateful and what is decent brings
a good name. Leave this undone and you carry a blot on your name that
is not pretty; do it, boy, and you win the greatest prize of a good
name there is, if I get home alive to the land of Oeta.

Come. It is not one whole day's trouble. Bring yourself to it. Take me
and throw me wherever you like — in the bilge, in the bow, in the
stern, wherever I will trouble the men on board least. Nod yes, by Zeus
himself, who watches over suppliants, boy; be persuaded. I fall at your
knees, for all that I am a helpless wretch, and lame. Do not leave me
deserted like this, far from any path men walk. Bring me safe to your
own home, or to Euboea, where Chalcodon rules. From there it is no long
voyage for me to Oeta, and the ridge of Trachis, and the fine stream of
Spercheius, so that you can show me to my father, whom I love — though
for a long time now I have been afraid he may be gone. I sent him many
messages by the men who came here, prayers and begging, that he would
send a ship himself and bring me safely home. But either he is dead, or
— as I think likely — the messengers made little of my business and
hurried on their voyage home.

Now, though, in you I have come to a man who can be my escort and my
messenger both. You save me. You pity me. See how everything for men is
full of fear and danger: to do well, and then to have the opposite
happen. When a man is out of trouble he should watch for the terrible
things, and when he is living well, that is the time to look hardest at
his life, in case it is destroyed without his noticing.
