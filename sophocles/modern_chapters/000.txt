Ajax
(Part 1 of 3)

(Before Ajax's hut, at the far end of the Greek camp at Troy. Night is
just over. Odysseus is studying the ground. Athena is present, but only
he can hear her, and he cannot see her.)

Athena.
Son of Laertes, I have always found you out on the prowl, looking for
some opening against your enemies. And here you are again, at Ajax's
hut down by the ships, where his post is at the edge of the camp. I
have been watching you a while now, hunting, measuring his tracks —
they are fresh — to work out whether he is inside or not. Your nose has
brought you straight to the door, like a good Spartan hound. The man is
in there. He went in just now, his face running with sweat, and his
hands too, from the killing. You can stop peering through the doorway.
Tell me instead what you are so set on, and hear it from someone who
knows.

Odysseus.
Athena — your voice, of all the gods the one I love best. I cannot see
you, and still I know you at once. I catch what you say and hold it,
the way you catch the note of a bronze-mouthed trumpet. You are right:
I am circling the track of a man who hates me, Ajax with the great
shield. It is him I have been following, and nobody else. Something
happened last night that we cannot make sense of — if in fact he did
it. We know nothing for certain. We are guessing. So I put myself
forward for the job.

We found the cattle, all of them, dead. Our own plunder, butchered by
somebody's hand, and the herdsmen with them. Everyone is laying it at
his door. And I have a scout who says he saw him, alone, running across
the plain with a sword still wet, and told me so. I went straight onto
the trail. Some of the signs I can read. Some of them baffle me, and I
cannot tell whose they are. You have come at the right moment. In
everything, before and after, it is your hand that steers me.

Athena.
I know, Odysseus. I have been out on the road a while, keeping watch,
on your side of the hunt.

Odysseus.
Then, dear mistress, am I working to any purpose?

Athena.
The man did it. These are his deeds.

Odysseus.
And what possessed him to raise his hand like that?

Athena.
He was weighed down with rage over the arms of Achilles.

Odysseus.
Then why go at the flocks?

Athena.
He thought the blood he was getting on his hands was yours.

Odysseus.
So this was a plot? Against the Greeks?

Athena.
And he would have carried it off, if I had not been paying attention.

Odysseus.
What made him so bold? What put that in his head?

Athena.
He came at you in the night, on his own, in secret.

Odysseus.
How close did he get? Did he reach us?

Athena.
He was at the doors of the two generals.

Odysseus.
Then how was his hand held back, with murder in it?

Athena.
I held him back. I threw a weight across his eyes — ideas about a joy
that would have finished him — and turned him aside onto the sheep, and
the mixed droves under the herdsmen's guard, the spoil you had not yet
divided up. He fell on them and went round in a circle cutting a
slaughter through the horned animals, breaking their spines. One moment
he thought he had the two sons of Atreus in his hands and was killing
them himself; the next it was some other commander he was falling on.
And the man ran about in a sickness of madness, and I drove him on. I
kept throwing him further into the net.

Then, when he had worn himself out at it, he tied up the oxen that were
still alive, and all the sheep, and brought them home — as though he
had men, not a catch of cattle. And now he has them roped together
indoors, and he is torturing them.

I will show you the madness too, with your own eyes, so you can go and
tell every Greek what you saw. Stand your ground and do not be afraid.
He will not be your ruin. I will turn the beams of his eyes aside and
keep them off your face.

(She calls towards the hut.)

You there — you with your prisoners' arms strapped behind them — I am
calling you. Come here. Ajax, I am calling you. Come out in front of
the house.

Odysseus.
Athena, what are you doing? Do not call him out.

Athena.
Be quiet. Do you want a name for cowardice?

Odysseus.
For the gods' sake, no. Let him stay inside. That is enough.

Athena.
Afraid of what? Was he not a man before?

Odysseus.
He was. My enemy then, and still is.

Athena.
And is there any laughter sweeter than laughing at an enemy?

Odysseus.
It is enough for me that he stays in his hut.

Athena.
You are afraid to look at a madman in plain sight?

Odysseus.
If he were sane I would not step out of his way.

Athena.
He will not see you now, even standing beside him.

Odysseus.
How, if he is looking out of the same eyes?

Athena.
I will darken them. He can be staring straight at you.

Odysseus.
True enough. Anything is possible when a god arranges it.

Athena.
Then stand still and say nothing. Stay exactly where you are.

Odysseus.
I will stay. I would rather be somewhere else.

Athena.
Ajax. You. I am calling you a second time. Why so little respect for
the ally at your side?

(Ajax comes out. There is blood on him, and a whip in his hand.)

Ajax.
Athena — welcome. Child of Zeus, welcome. You stood by me
magnificently. I will crown you with solid gold out of this catch, to
thank you for it.

Athena.
Well said. But tell me one thing. Did you dip your sword properly in
the Greek army?

Ajax.
I can boast about it. I am not going to deny it.

Athena.
And did you get your armed hand as far as the sons of Atreus?

Ajax.
So far that they will never dishonour Ajax again.

Athena.
The men are dead — that is what you are telling me.

Ajax.
Dead. Now let them try taking my arms off me.

Athena.
Good. And the son of Laertes — where does he stand with you? Or has he
got away?

Ajax.
That damned fox. You are asking me where he is?

Athena.
I am. Odysseus. The one who stands against you.

Ajax.
He is inside, mistress, sitting down. My favourite prisoner. I do not
want him dead yet.

Athena.
Not until you do what? What more do you get out of it?

Ajax.
Not until he is tied to the pillar under my roof —

Athena.
And what harm will you do the poor wretch then?

Ajax.
— with his back whipped red. Then he can die.

Athena.
Do not torture the poor wretch like that.

Ajax.
Athena, in everything else, take what pleasure you like. But he pays
that price and no other.

Athena.
Well, since doing it is what gives you pleasure — use your hand. Hold
back from nothing you have in mind.

Ajax.
I am going to the work. And I want this from you: stand by me always as
you have stood by me today.

(He goes back inside.)

Athena.
Do you see, Odysseus, how great the strength of the gods is? Who could
you have found more level-headed than that man, or better at doing what
the moment needed?

Odysseus.
Nobody that I know of. And I pity him for all that, wretched as he is,
enemy of mine though he is, because he is yoked under a ruin that is
not to be undone. I am not thinking of his case any more than my own. I
see that all of us who are alive are nothing but phantoms, or a
weightless shadow.

Athena.
Then, since you can see that much, never say an arrogant word yourself
against the gods, and take on no swagger, if you outweigh another man
in the strength of your hand or in the depth of your money. A single day
tips all human things down, and lifts them up again. The gods love the
man who keeps himself in hand, and they detest the bad.

(Athena is gone. The Chorus comes in: sailors from Salamis, Ajax's own
men.)

Chorus.
	Son of Telamon, you who hold
	the seat of sea-washed Salamis
	where the water comes right up —
	when things go well for you I am glad.
	But when the stroke of Zeus falls,
	or a rumour comes raging out of the Greek camp
	with all their bad tongues in it,
	I am afraid. A great dread takes me,
	and I flinch like the eye of a dove.
	So it was with the night now over:
	a loud noise has settled on us,
	and it is our disgrace —
	that you went into the meadow
	where the horses run mad
	and destroyed the Greeks' animals,
	the plunder that was left,
	the spear-won share not yet divided,
	killing them with the flashing iron.
	These are the whispers Odysseus shapes
	and breathes into everybody's ear,
	and they believe him. What he says
	about you now is easy to believe,
	and every man who hears it
	is more delighted than the man who told it,
	and tramples on your suffering.
	Aim at a great spirit and you cannot miss.
	Say the same things about me
	and nobody would credit it.
	It is the man who has something that envy crawls towards.
	And yet the small without the great
	are a shaky tower to shelter behind.
	The lowly stand up best beside the great,
	and the great are held upright by lesser men.
	But there is no teaching this to fools
	before they need to know it.
	Those are the men making the noise against you,
	and we have no strength to fight it off
	without you, my lord.
	Once they have got out from under your eye
	they chatter like flocks of birds.
	But if you appeared — with the great vulture over them —
	they would drop down suddenly,
	silent, and not a sound out of them.

Chorus.
	Was it Artemis, the bull-driver, daughter of Zeus —
	terrible rumour,
	mother of my shame —
	who drove you against the herds that belong to us all?
	Some victory she got no thanks for, perhaps,
	or cheated of glorious spoils,
	or a stag brought down and no gift given?
	Or has the Lord of War in his bronze breastplate
	held some grudge over a spear shared,
	and paid back the insult
	with contrivances in the night?

Chorus.
	Never out of your own mind, son of Telamon,
	would you have gone so far wrong
	as to fall upon the flocks.
	When madness comes from the gods it arrives.
	But let Zeus and Apollo turn aside
	this evil talk of the Greeks.
	And if it is the great kings
	putting the story about behind their hands,
	or the man out of the worthless line of Sisyphus —
	then do not, my lord, do not go on sitting
	in the hut by the sea with your face hidden,
	and let them make an evil name for you.

Chorus.
	Get up from where you sit. Wherever you have settled
	into this long standstill, out of the fighting,
	you are letting the ruin flare up to heaven.
	Your enemies' outrage runs about unafraid
	in the glens where the wind blows,
	and every tongue in the army is cackling,
	and it is heavy to bear.
	The pain in me has come to a stop and will not move.

(Tecmessa comes out of the hut.)

Tecmessa.
	Crew of Ajax's ship,
	men of the line that sprang from the earth of Athens,
	we have cause to cry out, we who care
	for the house of Telamon far away.
	The terrible one, the great one, the man of raw force,
	Ajax, is lying sick
	under a storm that has muddied him.

Chorus.
	What change has this night brought,
	and what weight, after the day before?
	Daughter of Teleutas the Phrygian, speak.
	Ajax took you in war and holds to you and loves you,
	so you can tell us something,
	you are not without knowledge.

Tecmessa.
	How am I to say a thing that cannot be said?
	What you are going to hear weighs as much as a death.
	Madness took him in the night —
	our famous Ajax — and has left him shamed.
	Go inside the hut and you can see it:
	butchered animals soaking in blood,
	sacrifices, and his hand made every one.

Chorus.
	What news you have shown us of the man of fire,
	not to be borne, and not to be escaped either,
	spread abroad by the great Greek lords,
	and their loud telling of it keeps growing.
	I am afraid of what is coming. He will die
	in front of everybody, that man,
	with his hand knocked off its aim,
	having killed with the dark sword the beasts
	and the herdsmen who ride with them.

Tecmessa.
	So that was it. That was where he came from,
	bringing the flock in ropes behind him.
	Some of them he cut the throats of, there on the floor;
	some he struck at the ribs and tore open.
	And he picked up two white-footed rams,
	and sheared off the head of one, and the tip of its tongue,
	and threw them away; and the other
	he tied upright to a pillar,
	and took a heavy harness strap
	and beat it with the doubled lash, whistling,
	cursing it in language
	that a god taught him and no man.

Chorus.
	It is time for each of us to cover his head
	and steal away on foot,
	or take his seat at the fast bench of the oars
	and let the ship go out to sea.
	Such are the threats the two kings, the sons of Atreus,
	are driving at us. I am afraid of being stoned,
	beaten down beside this man,
	whom a fate you cannot go near has hold of.

Tecmessa.
	Not any more. Like a south wind
	that comes up hard and then drops, with no lightning in it,
	he is quiet. And now he is in his right mind
	he has a new pain. Because to look at your own suffering,
	when no other hand was in it,
	lays a sharp ache under you.

Chorus.
If he has stopped, I think we may do well enough. Once the trouble is
gone it counts for less.

Tecmessa.
And if you were given the choice, which would you take: to hurt your
friends and be happy yourself, or to share the grief and be one of the
grieving?

Chorus.
The double sorrow is the greater evil, lady.

Tecmessa.
Then we are ruined now, and the sickness is over.

Chorus.
What do you mean? I do not follow you.

Tecmessa.
That man, while the sickness was on him, took pleasure in the horrors
he was held by, though being near him was misery for us, who were sane.
Now that it has let go of him and he can breathe, he is driven from end
to end by a wretched grief, and we are no better off than we were
before. Is that not two sorrows out of one?

Chorus.
I agree, and I am afraid a stroke has come from a god. How else, if he
is no happier now it has released him than he was when he was sick?

Tecmessa.
That is how it stands. You had better know it.

Chorus.
How did the trouble first swoop down on him? Tell us. We share the pain
of it.

Tecmessa.
You are in it with us, so you will hear all of it.

In the dead of night, when the evening lamps had burned out, he took up
a two-edged sword and started feeling his way to the door on some
errand that led nowhere. I went at him and said, "What are you doing,
Ajax? Nobody has summoned you, no messenger came for you, you have
heard no trumpet — why are you setting out? The whole army is asleep."
And he said what he always says, the same few words he has worn out:
"Woman, silence is what makes a woman lovely." I understood him, and I
stopped, and he went out alone.

What happened out there I cannot tell you. He came back in with them
roped together — bulls, herd dogs, the woolly catch. Some he took by
the neck, some he turned face up and cut their throats and broke their
backs; and some, still tied, he tortured as though they were men, going
at cattle. At the end he darted out through the door and hauled up
words at some shadow — some of it against the sons of Atreus, some
about Odysseus — laughing a great deal at how much outrage he had gone
and paid them back for.

Then he rushed back into the house, and slowly, somehow, over time, he
came to himself. He looked round the room, and it was full of his own
ruin, and he struck his head and howled. He went down in the wreckage,
among the wreck of the slaughtered sheep, and sat there with his nails
dug into his hair and his fist closed on it.

For a long while he sat and said nothing. Then he began threatening me
with terrible things if I did not tell him everything that had
happened, and he demanded to know what business it was that he had
found himself in. And I was frightened, friends, of what he had done,
and I told him all of it, as much as I knew for certain.

He cried out at once, and the sound was wretched. I had never heard him
make it before. He had always taught that wailing like that belonged to
cowards and low-hearted men; he used to grieve quietly, no loud
weeping, and groan low like a bull. Now he is down in this miserable
luck of his, no food, no drink, sitting still where he fell among the
cattle the iron killed. And it is plain that he means to do something
terrible. His words say so, and so do his laments.

Friends — this is the errand I came on — go in and help him, if there
is anything you can do. Men like him can be won over by what their
friends say.

Chorus.
Tecmessa, daughter of Teleutas, this is terrible news you bring us: our
man driven out of his mind by his own suffering.

Ajax.
(inside) Oh — oh no. Oh no.

Tecmessa.
And it will get worse, by the sound of it. Did you not hear Ajax? That
cry he just gave?

Ajax.
(inside) Oh — oh no. Oh no.

Chorus.
Either the man is sick again, or he is in pain at the sight of what the
sickness left behind.

Ajax.
(inside) My son. My son.

Tecmessa.
Oh, this is misery. Eurysaces — it is you he is calling. What is he
thinking of? Where are you? Oh, this is misery.

Ajax.
(inside) Teucer. Where is Teucer? Is he going to be off raiding for
ever, while I am destroyed?

Chorus.
He sounds sane. Open the doors. Perhaps if he looks at me he will pull
himself together.

Tecmessa.
There. It is open. Now you can look at what he has done, and at the
state he is in himself.

(The doors are opened. Ajax is sitting among the slaughtered animals.)

Ajax.
	Ah — my sailors, my friends,
	the only friends I have left,
	the only ones still keeping faith,
	look at me. Look what a wave has just gone round me
	and broken, out of a storm of blood.

Chorus.
	It seems your evidence was all too good, lady.
	What he does shows there is no mind in him.

Ajax.
	Ah — you men who know the sea,
	who came aboard and pulled the blade through the salt water,
	you and nobody else, you and nobody else,
	I see as a defence against what is happening to me.
	Come and kill me on top of these.

Chorus.
	Say better words than that. Do not cure a bad thing
	with a bad thing, and make the hurt of the ruin worse.

Ajax.
	You see the bold one, the man with the great heart,
	the one who never shook in a battle with the enemy —
	you see me, terrible-handed, against animals that could not fight back.
	The laughter. The outrage they have done me.

Tecmessa.
	Ajax, my lord, I beg you, do not say these things.

Ajax.
	Get out. Take yourself back out of here.
	Ah. Ah.

Chorus.
	In the gods' name, give way. Get some sense.

Ajax.
	I am cursed. I let them go out of my hand,
	the men who were asking to be punished,
	and I fell on cattle with curled horns and fine goats
	and made their dark blood run.

Chorus.
	Why grieve over what is done? It cannot be
	other than it is.

Ajax.
	You who see everything, you tool
	that every crime is done with, son of Laertes,
	the filthiest thing in the whole army —
	how you must be laughing, and enjoying it.

Chorus.
	It is as a god allows that any man laughs or mourns.

Ajax.
	Let me see him even so, shattered as I am.
	Oh. Oh no.

Chorus.
	Say nothing big. Can you not see what trouble you are in?

Ajax.
	Zeus, father of my fathers,
	let me destroy that smooth-tongued creature, that hated thing,
	and the two kings who share the command,
	and then die myself.

Tecmessa.
	When you pray for that, pray that I die with you.
	What reason have I to live, once you are dead?

Ajax.
	Ah —
	Darkness, you are my light.
	Blackness under the earth, the brightest thing my eyes can find,
	take me. Take me to live with you.
	Take me. I am not fit any longer to look for help
	from the family of the gods, or for anything good
	from men, who live a day at a time.
	The daughter of Zeus, the strong goddess,
	is grinding me down to nothing.
	Where is a man to run?
	Where can I go and stay?
	If what I have done is ruined, friends,
	and these things lying beside me are what I have,
	and I have set myself to a fool's hunting —
	then the whole army would come at me with both hands
	and kill me.

Tecmessa.
	This is misery, that a man who was worth so much
	should say the kind of thing he could never once have borne to say.

Ajax.
	Ah —
	you channels of the sounding sea,
	you caves along the shore, you pasture at the water's edge,
	long, long, far too long
	you have held me here at Troy. But not any more.
	Not while I still have breath in me.
	Let anyone with sense understand that.
	Scamander, you streams nearby,
	you were good to the Greeks;
	you will not look at this man again.
	I will say a large thing:
	Troy never saw his equal come out of the land of Greece
	in all the army. And now
	I lie here, stripped of honour.

Chorus.
	I do not know how to hold you back,
	nor how to let you go on speaking,
	fallen into troubles like these.

Ajax.
Aiai. Who would ever have thought my own name would fit my sorrows so
exactly? Aias — aiai. I can cry it twice now, and three times over.
These are the troubles I have run into.

My father took the first prize for courage out of this same country of
Ida, the fairest in the army, and carried every glory home. I am his
son. I came to the same ground at Troy with no less strength in me, and
what my hand did was no smaller — and I am destroyed here, dishonoured
by the Greeks.

Though I know one thing for certain: if Achilles were alive and were
awarding his own arms to the best man, nobody would have taken them
before me. As it is, the sons of Atreus have got them into the hands of
a man who will do anything, and shoved the victories of Ajax aside. And
if these eyes and this mind had not swerved off what I intended, they
would never have voted a verdict like that against another man again.

Instead the daughter of Zeus, the grim-eyed goddess nobody masters,
tripped me at the very moment I was fitting my hand to them, and threw a
raving sickness into me, so that I could bloody my hands on livestock.
And they have got away and are laughing about it — no thanks to me. But
when a god does the harm, even a worthless man can slip past a better
one.

So what now? What am I to do? The gods obviously hate me, the Greek
army loathes me, all Troy hates me and so does this whole plain. Do I
go home across the Aegean, and leave my station at the ships and the
sons of Atreus to themselves? And then what face do I show my father
Telamon when I get there? How is he going to bear the sight of me,
standing in front of him with nothing, no prize for courage, when he
himself won the great crown of it? No. I could not do it.

Well then — go at the Trojan wall, take them on alone, do something
worth doing, and die at the end of it? But that, I think, would only
make the sons of Atreus happy. No.

Some enterprise has to be found that will show my old father he did not
father a son with nothing inside him. It is a disgrace for a man to
want a long life when nothing changes in his troubles. What pleasure is
there in one day after another, moving you up to the edge of death and
back again? I would not give anything for the man who warms his hands
at empty hopes. A man of good birth has two choices and no more: live
well, or die well. That is all of it. You have heard me out.

Chorus.
Nobody will ever say that was a borrowed speech, Ajax. That came out of
your own mind. All the same — stop. Let your friends have charge of
your thinking, and put these ideas down.

Tecmessa.
Ajax, my lord — there is no greater evil for a human being than the
luck they are forced to live with. My father was a free man, as strong
in wealth as any in Phrygia. Now I am a slave. Somehow that is what the
gods decided, and above all it was your hand that did it. And since I
came into your bed, I want what is good for you, and I am begging you,
by Zeus of our hearth and by the bed you and I came together in: do not
leave me to the bitter talk of your enemies, do not leave me to some
stranger's hand.

The day you die and widow me by dying, you can be sure that on that
same day the Greeks will take me by force, and your son with me, and I
will be fed as a slave is fed. And one of my masters will say something
cutting, shooting words at me: "Look at the woman who shared Ajax's
bed, the greatest man in the army. Look at the work she does now, after
a life other women envied." That is what they will say. Fate will drive
me, and the shame of the words will stain you and your family.

Have some respect for your father, whom you are leaving in a miserable
old age; have some respect for your mother, with her long share of
years, who prays to the gods so often that you will come home alive.
And pity your son, my king. If he is robbed of the care he should be
growing up in, and has to get through his days without you, in the
hands of guardians who are neither family nor friends — think what you
will be leaving to him and to me when you die.

There is nothing left for me to look at except you. You are the reason.
Your spear made nothing of my country. Another fate took my mother and
the man who fathered me and gave them a house in the world of the dead.
So what homeland could I have except you? What money? Everything that
keeps me safe is in you.

Remember me too. A man ought to hold on to what he remembers, if he has
ever taken any pleasure anywhere. It is kindness that gives birth to
kindness, always. The man who lets the memory of a good turn run out of
him was never a man of any quality.
