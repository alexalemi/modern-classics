Ajax
(Part 3 of 3)

Teucer.
This is misery. Misery.

Chorus.
You have cause to mourn.

Teucer.
It happened so fast.

Chorus.
Far too fast, Teucer.

Teucer.
This is misery. And the man's child — where in all Troy am I to find
him?

Chorus.
Alone, by the hut.

Teucer.
Then bring him here at once, before one of our enemies snatches him
away the way a hunter takes a cub from a lioness and leaves her with
nothing. Go. Hurry. Help me. Men everywhere like to stand over the dead
and laugh at them, once they are only bodies on the ground.

Chorus.
While he was still alive, Teucer, he gave you orders to look after the
child, and you are doing it.

Teucer.
Of everything my eyes have ever looked at, this is the most painful.
And of all the roads I have gone down, this one I have just come along,
tracking you, hunting out your footsteps once I heard what had happened
to you, dearest Ajax — this is the one that has hurt me most.

A rumour about you went through the whole Greek army as fast as if a
god had sent it: that you were dead and gone. I was still far off when
I heard it, and I groaned quietly. Now I am looking at it, and it
destroys me.

Uncover him. Let me see the worst of it.

(The body is uncovered.)

That face — it is hard to look at, and there is a bitter courage in it.
What a crop of griefs you have sown for me by dying. Where can I go?
What people will take me in, when I was no use to you in your trouble?

I suppose Telamon — your father, and mine — will greet me with a smile
and a kind word when I come home without you. Naturally he will. He is
a man who cannot manage a pleasant look even when things go well for
him. What will he leave unsaid? What insult will he spare the bastard,
got on a woman his spear won — the coward, the man too soft to be a
man, who betrayed you, dear Ajax; or better still, who betrayed you on
purpose, so as to have your power and your house once you were dead.
That is what he will say. He is a man with a short temper and old age
has made him heavy, and his anger picks quarrels out of nothing.

And in the end I shall be pushed out of the country and thrown away,
called a slave instead of a free man by his own mouth.

That is what waits for me at home. Here at Troy I have many enemies and
very little that helps me. All of it I have got out of your death.

What am I to do? How am I to draw you off the sharp tooth of this
shining sword, the murderer you breathed your last against? Do you see
how, in time, Hector was going to destroy you after all, even dead?

In the gods' name, look at the luck of these two men. Hector was lashed
to the chariot rail with the very belt Ajax gave him, and shredded
along the ground until his life was gone out of him. And Ajax had this
present from Hector, and it is on this that he has died, in a fall that
killed him.

Was it not a Fury who forged this blade? Was that belt not made by
Hades, the grim workman? For my part I would say that these things, and
all things ever, are contrived for men by the gods. If anyone does not
care for that idea, let him keep his own, and I will keep mine.

Chorus.
Do not go on at length. Think how you will bury him, and what you are
going to say — and soon. I can see an enemy coming, and a man who means
harm is likely to come laughing at our troubles.

Teucer.
Which of the army do you see?

Chorus.
Menelaus. The man this whole voyage was fitted out for.

Teucer.
I see him. He is not hard to make out at this distance.

(Menelaus comes in.)

Menelaus.
You there. I am telling you not to lift that body for burial. Leave it
where it is.

Teucer.
Why did you spend so many words on that?

Menelaus.
Because it is my decision, and the decision of the man who commands the
army.

Teucer.
And would you tell us what reason you are putting forward?

Menelaus.
This. We hoped when we brought him from home that we had an ally for
the Greeks and a friend, and on closer inspection we found him worse
than a Trojan. He planned the murder of the entire army and marched
against us in the night to take us with the spear. And if some god had
not put that attempt out, we would have drawn the lot he has drawn, and
we would be lying dead by the most shameful death there is, and he
would be alive. As it is, a god turned his outrage aside so that it
fell on sheep and cattle.

For which reason there is no man strong enough to put his body in a
tomb. He will be thrown out somewhere on the pale sand and become food
for the birds along the shore.

So do not raise any terrible force of feeling about it. If we could not
master him alive, at any rate we shall rule him dead, however much you
object, and keep him in order with our hands. While he lived there was
never a moment when he would listen to a word from me.

It is the mark of a low nature when a man in the ranks does not think
it right to obey the men set over him. The laws will never run well in
a city where there is nothing to be afraid of, and no camp can be
governed sensibly if it has no fear and no shame in front of it as a
defence. A man may grow a great body, but he should expect to fall,
even from a small hurt. Where a man has fear in him and shame with it,
you may be sure he has found his safety; but where there is licence to
be outrageous and do as one likes, be certain that such a state will in
time run before a fair wind and then go straight to the bottom.

Let there be fear, I say, standing where fear belongs. And let us not
imagine we can do what we enjoy and pay nothing back in pain later.
These things come round in turn. He used to be the hot one, doing the
outrage; now it is my turn to think well of myself.

So I am warning you not to bury him. Bury him and you may fall into a
grave yourself.

Chorus.
Menelaus — do not lay down wise principles and then commit an outrage
on the dead yourself.

Teucer.
I shall never again be surprised, men, when somebody born a nobody does
wrong, if men who are supposed to be well born make errors like that in
what they argue.

Come — say it again from the beginning. Do you really claim you brought
this man here as an ally, and that you recruited him? Did he not sail
of his own accord, as his own master? Where are you his commander?
Where do you get the right to be king over the men he brought from
home? You came here as king of Sparta, not as our master. Nowhere in
what you are lawfully allowed to do was it laid down that you should
give him orders any more than he should give them to you.

You sailed here under other men's command, not as the supreme general
with authority over Ajax at any time. Command the troops you command,
and use your solemn language to discipline them. This man I will lay in
the grave as justice requires, whether you forbid it or the other
general does, and I am not afraid of your mouth.

He did not go on this expedition for the sake of your wife, like those
worn-out drudges. He went for the sake of an oath he had sworn, and not
for you at all — he never thought much of nobodies.

So come back with more heralds, and bring the commander-in-chief with
you. I would not turn round for your noise as long as you are what you
are.

Chorus.
Again — I do not like that tone in trouble like this. Hard words sting,
however right they are.

Menelaus.
The archer seems to think rather well of himself.

Teucer.
Yes. It is no menial skill I have.

Menelaus.
How you would boast, if you had a shield.

Teucer.
Even without one I would be a match for you in full armour.

Menelaus.
That tongue of yours feeds a fearful temper.

Teucer.
When a man has right on his side he may think in a large way.

Menelaus.
Is it right that my murderer should prosper?

Teucer.
Your murderer? That is a strange thing to say, if you are alive after
being killed.

Menelaus.
A god saved me. As far as he is concerned, I am dead.

Teucer.
Then do not dishonour the gods, when it is the gods who saved you.

Menelaus.
Would I find fault with the law of heaven?

Teucer.
You would, if you stand here and stop the dead being buried.

Menelaus.
I stop my own enemies being buried. That would not be right.

Teucer.
Did Ajax ever stand out in the open and make war on you?

Menelaus.
He hated me and I hated him. You knew that too.

Teucer.
He hated you because you were caught rigging the votes to rob him.

Menelaus.
He lost that at the hands of the judges, not mine.

Teucer.
You could make a great many stealthy crimes look handsome.

Menelaus.
That remark is going to bring somebody pain.

Teucer.
No more, I think, than the pain we shall be giving.

Menelaus.
I will tell you once. There is no burial for him.

Teucer.
And you will hear the answer. He will be buried at once.

Menelaus.
I saw a bold-tongued man once who urged sailors to put to sea in
winter. And when the worst of the storm was on him you could not have
found a voice in him at all. He got under his cloak and let the crew
walk over him as they liked. So with you and that raging mouth: perhaps
some great storm out of a small cloud will blow up and put your
shouting out.

Teucer.
And I have seen a man stuffed with foolishness who mocked at his
neighbours' misfortunes. And a man rather like me, of the same temper,
looked at him and said: "You. Do not do wrong to the dead. Do it and be
sure you will suffer for it." That is how he warned the poor fool to
his face. And I can see him now, I think, and he is nobody but you.
Have I been speaking in riddles?

Menelaus.
I am going. It would be a disgrace if it got out that I argue when I
have the power to use force.

Teucer.
Then crawl off. It is the worst disgrace to me to stand listening to an
empty man talking nonsense.

(Menelaus goes.)

Chorus.
	There is going to be a trial of this,
	and a great quarrel. Teucer, be quick,
	as quick as you can manage, and find
	some hollow grave for him, where he will keep
	his damp tomb, and men will remember it always.

(Tecmessa comes back, with Eurysaces.)

Teucer.
Here they are, and in good time — the man's son and his wife, come to
arrange the burial of this poor body.

Come here, child. Stand close, and take hold of your father as a
suppliant, the man who fathered you. Kneel here and beg, and hold in
your hands locks of hair — mine, and hers, and yours as the third. That
is all a suppliant has.

And if any soldier of the army drags you off this body by force, then
for his wickedness let him be thrown out of his country wickedly and
get no burial, and let his whole family be cut off at the root, just as
I cut this lock.

Take it, child. Keep it safe. Let nobody move you. Kneel down and hold
on to him.

And you — do not stand about beside him like women instead of men.
Defend us until I get back, when I have seen to a grave for him, though
the whole world forbids it.

(He goes.)

Chorus.
	Which will be the last year? When will the count
	of all these wandering years come to an end,
	and stop bringing on me the endless ruin
	of the work of the spear,
	over the wide fields of Troy,
	the sorrow and the shame of Greece?

Chorus.
	He should have gone up into the great sky first,
	or down to Hades, where everyone goes,
	that man who showed the Greeks
	how to make common war with hateful weapons.
	Those labours of his, the parents of all our labours.
	He is the man who ruined the human race.

Chorus.
	He gave me no share in garlands,
	none in the deep wine-cups,
	no sweet noise of the pipes,
	nothing, the miserable man,
	no lying down at night for pleasure.
	And from love — from love — he has shut me out entirely.
	Here I lie with nobody caring,
	and the heavy dew keeps soaking my hair,
	to remind me of Troy, where there is no joy.

Chorus.
	Before this, bold Ajax was always
	my defence against the terror in the night
	and against the flying spears.
	Now he has been handed over
	to a hateful power. What is left for me?
	What pleasure is there now?
	Let me be where the wooded headland
	fences off the sea, under the cliff
	of Sunium, so that we could speak
	to holy Athens as we passed.

(Teucer comes back.)

Teucer.
Here I am — I hurried when I saw the commander-in-chief, Agamemnon,
coming this way at speed. It is obvious he means to let that clumsy
mouth of his go.

(Agamemnon comes in.)

Agamemnon.
So it is you, they tell me, who dared to open your mouth wide and make
these terrible threats against us — and nothing has happened to you
yet. You. I mean you, the son of the captive woman. No doubt if you had
been born of a well-bred mother your talk would have gone up to the sky
and you would have walked about on tiptoe. As it is, you are a nobody
and you have stood up for another nobody lying here, and sworn that we
came out with no authority, either as admirals or as generals over the
Greeks or over you: Ajax sailed as his own master, you say.

Is it not a great injury to have to hear this from the mouths of
slaves? What sort of man is it you shout about so arrogantly? Where did
he go, or where did he stand, that I did not? Have the Greeks no men
except him?

It looks as though we did ourselves harm when we announced the contest
for the arms of Achilles, if we are to be called corrupt on every side
because of Teucer — if it will never be enough for you people, even
beaten, to accept the verdict that satisfied most of the judges, and
you will always be shooting slander at us, or knifing us behind our
backs when you finish behind us in the race.

Where those habits get in, no law could ever be settled at all, if we
are going to push the rightful winners aside and bring the men at the
back up to the front. That has to be stopped. It is not the thick,
broad-shouldered men who are the steadiest. It is the men who think
well who win everywhere. A broad-backed ox is kept straight on the road
by a small whip.

And I can see that medicine coming your way before long, unless you
acquire some sense. The man no longer exists — he is a shadow already —
and still you insult us boldly and give your tongue too much freedom.

Will you not restrain yourself? Remember your birth, and what you are.
Bring somebody else here, a free man, who can plead your case in front
of me instead of you. When you speak I no longer follow it. I do not
know your foreign language.

Chorus.
I wish you both had the sense to hold yourselves in. I have no better
advice to give the two of you.

Teucer.
How quickly gratitude to the dead drains out of men and is found to
have gone over to the other side. This man no longer holds even the
smallest word of remembrance for you, Ajax — for you, who so often
worked yourself to the bone on his account and put your own life in
front of the spear for him. It is all gone. Thrown away.

You there, with all those words and no sense in them: do you not
remember anything of the time you were shut inside your own defences,
already down to nothing, when the battle turned, and he came alone and
saved you? The fire was already burning at the stern decks of the ships
and Hector was leaping high over the ditch at the hulls. Who stopped
that? Was it not this man — the man who, you say, never set foot
anywhere you had not been first? Do you grant that he did his duty by
you then?

And the other time, when he went out alone against Hector, alone
against alone, by the fall of the lot, and nobody ordered him to? The
lot he put in was not the kind that runs away. It was no lump of wet
earth. It was the one that would come first and lightly out of the
crested helmet. He is the man who did those things, and I was beside
him — the slave, the son of the foreign mother.

Teucer.
You poor creature. Where are you looking when you talk like that? Do
you not know that your father's father, Pelops, was a foreigner, a
Phrygian, long ago? That Atreus, who fathered you, set before his own
brother a meal made out of his brother's children — the most unholy
thing that has been done? And that you yourself were born of a Cretan
mother, and her own father caught a stranger on top of her and handed
her over to the voiceless fish to be destroyed?

Being what you are, do you reproach a man like me with his breeding? I
am the son of Telamon, who won my mother as his bedfellow for being the
bravest in the army; and she was a king's daughter by birth, the child
of Laomedon, and Heracles gave her to him as the choice gift out of the
spoil.

So, born the best from two of the best — could I disgrace my own
blood-relation? Him, lying here under such a weight of trouble, whom
you would push out unburied, and say so without any shame at all?

Understand this clearly. If you throw him out anywhere, you throw the
three of us out with him, lying together. It is a fine thing for me to
die in the open, working for him, rather than for your wife — or should
I say your brother's? So do not think about my safety. Think about your
own. If you cause me any trouble at all, you will soon wish you had
been a coward with me rather than bold.

(Odysseus comes in.)

Chorus.
Lord Odysseus, you have come at the right moment, if you are here to
untie this and not to pull it tighter.

Odysseus.
What is the trouble, friends? I heard the sons of Atreus shouting some
way off, over this brave man's body.

Agamemnon.
And is that not because we have just been hearing the most shameful
language from this man, Lord Odysseus?

Odysseus.
What language? I can forgive a man for throwing abuse back when he has
had abuse thrown at him.

Agamemnon.
He heard shameful things because he was doing shameful things to me.

Odysseus.
What did he do to you that harmed you?

Agamemnon.
He says he will not leave this body without its burial. He will bury
it whether I like it or not.

Odysseus.
Then may a friend tell you the truth, and go on pulling the oar with
you exactly as before?

Agamemnon.
Speak. I would be out of my mind otherwise. I count you my greatest
friend among the Greeks.

Odysseus.
Then listen. In the gods' name, do not bring yourself to throw this man
out unburied so hard-heartedly. Do not let the violence in you win so
completely that you trample justice underfoot.

He was once the most hostile man in the army to me, too — from the day
I won the arms of Achilles against him. But hostile as he was, I would
not dishonour him in return, or refuse to say that of all of us Greeks
who came to Troy he was the best and bravest, except for Achilles.

So it would not be just for you to dishonour him. It is not him you
would be damaging but the laws the gods laid down. There is no justice
in doing harm to a good man once he is dead, not even if you hate him.

Agamemnon.
You, Odysseus — you are fighting for him against me?

Odysseus.
I am. I hated him when it was honourable to hate him.

Agamemnon.
And should you not put your heel on him now he is dead?

Odysseus.
Do not take pleasure, son of Atreus, in an advantage that does you no
credit.

Agamemnon.
It is not easy for a man with absolute power to be scrupulous.

Odysseus.
But it is easy to allow something to friends who advise you well.

Agamemnon.
A good man ought to listen to those in office.

Odysseus.
Stop. You win when you give way to your friends.

Agamemnon.
Remember what sort of man you are doing this favour for.

Odysseus.
He was my enemy. But he was a man of quality.

Agamemnon.
What are you doing? Have you so much respect for an enemy's corpse?

Odysseus.
His excellence weighs far more with me than his hostility did.

Agamemnon.
Men who behave like this are the unsteady kind.

Odysseus.
A great many men are friendly at one time and bitter at another.

Agamemnon.
And these are the friends you recommend acquiring?

Odysseus.
It is not my habit to recommend a rigid spirit.

Agamemnon.
You will make us look like cowards today.

Odysseus.
On the contrary. We shall look like just men to every Greek.

Agamemnon.
So you really are telling me to let the body be buried?

Odysseus.
I am. I shall come to the same necessity myself.

Agamemnon.
How true it is that every man works for himself in everything.

Odysseus.
And who should I work for more than myself?

Agamemnon.
Then it will be called your doing, and not mine.

Odysseus.
However you do it, you will come out of it well.

Agamemnon.
Then be quite certain of this: to you I would grant a larger favour
than this one. But that man, here or below, will be my worst enemy
just the same. You may do what you like.

(He goes.)

Chorus.
Odysseus, anyone who denies you were born with a wise judgement, after
this, is a fool.

Odysseus.
And now I say to Teucer that from this moment I am as much his friend
as I was once his enemy. I would like to join in burying your dead and
share the work, and leave out no service that men should perform for
the best and bravest of us.

Teucer.
Odysseus, you are the best of men, and I have nothing but praise for
what you have said. You have proved my fears completely wrong. Of all
the Greeks you were his deadliest enemy, and you alone stood by him
with your hands ready. You did not come here, alive, and outrage the
dead — unlike the general who came here out of his senses, who wanted,
he and his brother, to throw the man out mutilated and unburied.

So may the Father who is first on Olympus, and the Fury who forgets
nothing, and Justice who completes what she begins, destroy those bad
men badly, exactly as they wanted to throw this man out, insulted and
undeserving.

But you, child of old Laertes — I hesitate to let you touch the body in
the burial, in case I offend the dead by it. In everything else be our
partner. And if you want to bring any soldier of the army with you, he
is welcome. I will get everything else ready. And you should know that
to us you have been a good man.

Odysseus.
It was my wish to help. But if it does not please you that I should, I
accept what you say, and I will go.

(He goes.)

Teucer.
	Enough. This has gone on a long time already.
	Some of you be quick, dig the hollow grave.
	Others set the high stand with the cauldron
	round with fire, ready for the washing.
	Let another party bring from the hut
	the gear he wore under his shield.
	Child, you too — with what strength you have,
	put a loving hand on your father here
	and help me lift him. The channels
	are still warm, and still driving up
	the dark force in him.
	Come on, everyone who says he is a friend.
	Move. Get going. Do the work
	for this man, who was good in every way —
	and no better man has ever had it done for him.
	I mean Ajax, when he was alive.

Chorus.
	There is a great deal a man can know once he has seen it.
	Before he sees it, nobody can prophesy
	what is coming, or how he will come out of it.
