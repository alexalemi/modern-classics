Oedipus at Colonus
(Part 3 of 4)

Oedipus.
Do you hear what he is saying?

Chorus.
He will not do it. Great Zeus be my witness.

Creon.
Zeus would know that. You do not.

Chorus.
Is this not outrage?

Creon.
Outrage. And you will have to put up with it.

Chorus.
All of you, everyone, leaders of this land, come here, come quickly!
These men are over the line already.

(Theseus comes in, with his men behind him.)

Theseus.
What is that shouting? What has happened? What were you so afraid of,
that you stopped me in the middle of a sacrifice at the altar of the sea
god, the god who watches over this Colonus? Tell me all of it. I ran
here faster than my legs enjoyed, and I want to know why.

Oedipus.
Dearest of friends. I know your voice. This man here has just done me a
terrible wrong.

Theseus.
What wrong? Who has hurt you? Speak.

Oedipus.
Creon. The man you are looking at. He has torn my children away from me,
the only pair I had, and he is taking them off.

Theseus.
What did you say?

Oedipus.
You have heard what has been done to me.

Theseus.
Then one of you men, quick, to the altars over there. Order the whole
people to leave the sacrifice and race, on foot and on horseback, with
the reins loose, to the place where the two roads the traders use come
together, so that the girls do not get past, and I am not made a
laughing-stock to this stranger, beaten by force. Go. Do as I have
ordered, and fast.

As for this man: if I let my anger go as far as he has earned, he would
not get out of my hands unhurt. As it is, he will be judged by the very
laws he brought in here with him, and by no others. You will never leave
this country until you have brought those girls back and set them here
in front of me, where I can see them. What you have done is a disgrace
to me, and to your own ancestors, and to your own land. You came into a
city that practises justice and settles nothing without law; you brushed
aside the authority of this country, burst in, took what you wanted and
made it yours by force. You must have decided that my city was empty of
men, or a city of slaves, and that I counted for exactly nothing.

And yet it was not Thebes that trained you to be bad. Thebes does not
like to breed lawless men, and she would not praise you if she heard you
were robbing me, and robbing the gods, dragging off by force the unhappy
people who came to them for protection. If I had set foot in your
country, even with the justest claim in the world, I would not have
dragged anybody off or led anybody away without the consent of whoever
ruled there. I would have known how a stranger ought to behave among
citizens. But you are disgracing a city that does not deserve it, your
own city; and the years piling up on you have made you old and empty of
sense at the same time.

I said it before and I say it again now: somebody bring those girls
here, fast, unless you want to become a resident of this country
yourself, by force and not by choice. That is what I am telling you, and
what is in my mind is the same as what is on my tongue.

Chorus.
Do you see where you have landed, stranger? By where you come from, you
look like a just man. By what you do, you turn out to be evil.

Creon.
Son of Aegeus, I did not do this because I took this city for a place
empty of men, or empty of counsel, as you say. I did it because I judged
that nobody here would ever feel such a passion for my relatives that
they would keep them against my will. And I knew this people would not
take in a man who killed his father, a polluted man, a man found living
in a marriage that was unholy, with children in it. That much I knew:
this country has a council on the hill of Ares that judges well, and it
does not allow wanderers like that to settle in this city. I trusted in
that, and I laid hands on my prize.

And I would not have done it, if he had not called down bitter curses on
me and on my family. That was the wrong done to me, and I thought I had
the right to pay it back. Anger has no old age except death; only the
dead feel no pain.

So do what you like. Even if I am in the right, having nobody on my side
makes me small. Still, old as I am, I will try to answer what you do
with what I do.

Oedipus.
You shameless thing. Whose old age do you think you are insulting with
this? Mine, or your own? Killings, marriages, disasters: you have let
all of that out of your mouth at me, and I bore all of it, poor wretch,
against my will. That was what the gods wanted; perhaps they had some
old anger against my family. Take me by myself and you could not find
one fault in me to throw in my face, no crime to explain why I did these
wrongs to myself and to my own people.

Tell me this. If an oracle came to my father from the gods, saying he
would die at the hands of his children, how can you justly hold that
against me, when I had not been born yet, when no father had begotten me
and no mother conceived me? I did not exist. And if, having been born
unlucky, as I was, I came to blows with my father and killed him,
understanding nothing of what I was doing or whom I was doing it to, how
can you reasonably blame an act nobody meant?

And my mother. You wretch. Have you no shame, forcing me to speak of her
marriage, when she was your own sister, and when it was the kind of
marriage I am now going to describe? Because I will not keep quiet, when
you have gone that far in unholy talk. She bore me. Yes, she bore me,
oh, the misery of it, not knowing, and I not knowing; and having borne
me, she gave me children, to her own shame. But one thing I know for
certain: you foul-mouth her and me on purpose. I married her without
meaning to, and I speak of it now without wanting to. No. I will not be
called evil for that marriage, nor for my father's killing, which you
keep flinging at me over and over, bitterly, as a taunt.

Answer me one question, only one. If someone came up to you, here and
now, and tried to kill you, you, the just man, would you stop to ask
whether the killer was your father, or would you pay him back on the
spot? I think, if you are fond of living, you would pay back the man who
did it, and not look round for a justification. Well, those are the
evils I walked into, with the gods leading me. And I do not think my
father's soul, if it came back to life, would say a word against me on
that.

But you are not just. You think it is fine to say everything, what may
be said and what may not, and so you taunt me like this in front of
these men. And you find it fine to flatter Theseus's name, and Athens,
and how well she is governed; and in the middle of all that praise you
forget one thing: that if any land knows how to worship the gods with
honour, this one comes first at it. And it is from this land that you
tried to steal me, an old man and a suppliant. You laid hands on me, and
you have taken my girls and gone. For that, I call on the goddesses of
this place, I beg them, I lay it on them with my prayers: come as my
helpers and my allies, so that you find out what kind of men stand guard
over this city.

Chorus.
The stranger is a good man, my lord. What has happened to him is utter
ruin, and it deserves our help.

Theseus.
Enough talk. The men who did this are running, and we, who were wronged,
are standing still.

Creon.
Then what are your orders for a man with no strength left?

Theseus.
Lead the way on the road out there, and I will be your escort. If you
are keeping the girls somewhere in these parts, you can show me where
yourself. If your men are running off with them in their grip, there is
no need for us to trouble: others are after them already, and they will
never get out of this country to thank their gods. Go on. Lead. And
understand this: the man who held has been held. Luck caught you in the
middle of your hunt. What is got by dishonest trickery does not stay
got.

And you will have nobody else to help you in this. I know very well you
did not come to this pitch of outrage, this daring you have shown just
now, unarmed and unprepared. There is somebody you trusted when you did
it. That I must look into; I must not let this city be weaker than one
man. Do you take any of that in? Or does it seem as empty to you now as
it did when you were planning this?

Creon.
Say what you like while I am here; I will not object. At home, we too
shall know what has to be done.

Theseus.
Threaten as you go. Oedipus, you stay here quietly, and trust me for
this: unless I die first, I will not stop until I have put your children
back in your hands.

Oedipus.
Bless you, Theseus, for your nobility, and for the just care you have
taken of us.

(Theseus goes out with his men, taking Creon with him.)

Chorus.
	Oh, to be there, where the enemy
	turn and stand, and soon
	the bronze cry of battle breaks:
	on Apollo's shore, perhaps,
	or the shore of the torches,
	where the great goddesses keep
	their holy rites for mortals,
	and their priests, the Eumolpids,
	carry a golden key upon the tongue.
	There, I think, Theseus,
	the man who wakes the fight,
	and the two unwed sisters
	will soon meet, in a shout that settles it,
	somewhere in these fields.

Chorus.
	Or maybe they are nearing
	the pastures west of Oea's snowy rock,
	on colts, or in chariots
	racing away at full stretch.
	He will be caught. Terrible
	is the war god of these parts,
	terrible the strength of Theseus's men.
	Every bridle flashes,
	every rider lets the reins go loose
	and comes on: all of them
	who honour Athena of the horses
	and the sea god who holds the earth,
	Rhea's own son.

Chorus.
	Are they at it, or still to start?
	Something tells me,
	my mind is a prophet,
	that soon I shall meet the girls
	who suffered terribly,
	who found terrible things at a kinsman's hands.
	Zeus will finish something, finish it today.
	I foretell a good fight.
	Oh, to be a dove, storm-fast, storm-strong,
	and reach a cloud high in the air,
	and hang my eye above the battle.

Chorus.
	Zeus, all-ruler of the gods,
	all-seeing Zeus, grant
	the guardians of this land
	the strength that wins,
	and bring the ambush to a good catch;
	and you, holy Pallas Athena, his child.
	And Apollo the hunter,
	and his sister, who follows
	the dappled deer that run so fast:
	I want them to come, two helpers,
	for this land and for its people.
	Wandering stranger, you will not say
	your watchman was a false prophet.
	I see the girls, there,
	with their escort, coming close again.

Oedipus.
Where? Where? What are you saying? What did you say?

(Antigone and Ismene come in, with Theseus and his men.)

Antigone.
Father. Father. If only some god would let you see this best of men, who
has brought us back here to you.

Oedipus.
Child, are you both really here?

Antigone.
Yes. These arms saved us: Theseus's, and his dearest men's.

Oedipus.
Come here to your father, children. Let me hold you. I never hoped you
would come back.

Antigone.
You will get what you ask for. We want the gift as much as you do.

Oedipus.
Where are you, then? Where?

Antigone.
Here. We are coming to you together.

Oedipus.
My dearest branches.

Antigone.
Everything is dear to the one who made it.

Oedipus.
The props a man leans on.

Antigone.
Unlucky props of an unlucky man.

Oedipus.
I have what I love most. Now if I died I would not be wholly wretched,
with the two of you standing beside me. Press against me, children, one
on each side, grown to the father who grew you, and give me rest from
that wandering, so lonely and so wretched, before this. And tell me what
happened, as briefly as you can. For girls of your age a short account
is enough.

Antigone.
Here is the man who saved us. Hear it from him, father; the deed was
his. That way my part will be short.

Oedipus.
Stranger, do not wonder if I go on at length, and stubbornly, with
children who have come back when I had given up hope. I know very well
that this joy in them has come to me from you and from nobody else. You
saved them; no other man alive did. And may the gods give you what I
wish for you, you yourself and this land; because it is among you alone,
of all people, that I have found reverence, and decency, and mouths that
do not lie. I know it, and these words are how I pay it back. What I
have, I have through you and through no other man.

Give me your right hand, my lord, so I can touch it, and kiss your face,
if that is allowed. But what am I saying? How could a man like me, born
to misery, want you to touch a man in whom every stain of evil has come
to live? I will not do it. I would not let you, if you offered. Only
people who have been through this can share the weight of it. Take my
greeting from where you stand, and go on caring for me justly, as you
have up to this hour.

Theseus.
I am not surprised that you spun your words out at some length in your
delight at these children, or that you put their words ahead of mine.
There is nothing in that to weigh on me. It is not with words that I try
to make my life shine, but with things done. And here is the proof: I
have broken none of what I swore to you, old man. I am here, bringing
the girls alive, untouched by all those threats. As for how the fight
was won, why should I boast about it for nothing, when you will hear it
yourself from these two once you are together?

But there is something that came my way just now, as I was walking here.
Give me your opinion on it. It is a small thing to tell, but it is worth
wondering about; and a man should treat nothing as beneath his notice.

Oedipus.
What is it, son of Aegeus? Tell me. I know nothing of what you are
asking about.

Theseus.
They say a man, not from your city, but of your blood, has thrown
himself down somehow at the altar of Poseidon and is sitting there as a
suppliant. The altar where I was sacrificing when I set out to come
here.

Oedipus.
Where is he from? What does he want by sitting there?

Theseus.
I know only one thing. They tell me he asks for a short word with you.
Nothing heavy.

Oedipus.
What kind of word? A seat at an altar is not the seat for a small
matter.

Theseus.
They say he asks only to come and speak with you, and to go back the way
he came in safety.

Oedipus.
Who could it be, sitting at that altar?

Theseus.
Think. Is there anybody of your family in Argos who might want that
favour from you?

Oedipus.
Dearest friend, stop there.

Theseus.
What is wrong?

Oedipus.
Do not ask me to —

Theseus.
To what? Say it.

Oedipus.
From what I have heard, I know who this suppliant is.

Theseus.
And who is he, that I should have anything against him?

Oedipus.
My son, my lord. The hated one. Of all men alive, his are the words it
would hurt me most to hear.

Theseus.
Why? Can you not listen, and still not do what you do not want to do?
What is painful to you in hearing him?

Oedipus.
That voice, my lord, has become the most hateful thing there is to his
father. Do not force me to give way in this.

Theseus.
But consider whether his sitting there compels you. Whether you have a
duty to the god that you must keep.

Antigone.
Father, listen to me, even if I am young to be giving advice. Let this
man do what his own heart and the god want; let him give that favour.
And give way to the two of us, and let our brother come. He will not
drag you off your resolve by force, do not be afraid of that, with words
that are not to your good. What harm is there in hearing words? Things
badly planned show themselves in the telling. You fathered him. So even
if he is doing you the wickedest of the wickedest wrongs, father, it is
not right for you to wrong him back. Let him come. Other men have bad
children and a sharp temper too, and still they are talked round; their
friends' words work on their nature like a spell.

Look back at those things, not at now: at what you suffered through your
father and your mother. If you look at that, I know you will see how bad
an end comes of a bad temper. You have no small reason to think about
it, robbed as you are of the eyes that cannot see. Give way to us. It is
not right that people asking for what is just should have to keep on
asking; nor that a man should be treated well and then not know how to
pay it back.

Oedipus.
Child, you win, and the pleasure you win from me weighs heavily. Let it
be as you two want. Only, stranger, if that man is to come here, let
nobody ever get power over my life.

Theseus.
I need to hear that once, old man, not twice. I do not want to boast;
but know that you are safe, so long as any god keeps me safe.

(Theseus goes out.)

Chorus.
	Whoever wants the larger share of life,
	and lets the fitting measure go,
	is keeping company with folly:
	that is plain to me.
	Because the long days lay down
	a great deal that is nearer to pain,
	and where the pleasures are you will not find,
	once a man falls past
	what he needs. And the helper
	comes to all alike in the end,
	when the doom of Hades shows itself,
	no wedding song, no lyre, no dance:
	death at the last.

Chorus.
	Not to be born beats every argument.
	And once a man has appeared,
	to go back where he came from
	as fast as he can is next best by far.
	For when youth goes by,
	with its light-headed follies in its hands,
	what heavy blow stays away? What labour is not in it?
	Envy, faction, strife, battles,
	and killings. And last of all, allotted,
	comes despised old age,
	weak, unsociable, friendless,
	where every evil of evils lives together.

Chorus.
	That is where he is, this poor man,
	and not I alone.
	Like some headland facing north,
	beaten by the waves on every side in winter,
	so the terrible ruins break on him too,
	wave after wave, from crown to base,
	and never leave him:
	some from the setting of the sun,
	some from its rising,
	some from the beam of noon,
	some from the Rhipaean hills of night.

Antigone.
And look, here comes the stranger, it seems, and alone, father, nobody
with him. He is walking this way with the tears pouring from his eyes.

Oedipus.
Who is it?

Antigone.
The man we have had in mind all this while. Polyneices has come to us.

(Polyneices comes in.)

Polyneices.
Oh, what am I to do? Do I weep first for my own troubles, sisters, or
for what I see of his, my father's, an old man's? I have found him here,
in a strange country, cast out, with the two of you, in clothes like
these, whose filth, old and loathsome, has settled on the old man's body
and is wasting his sides; and on his eyeless head the hair blows
uncombed in the wind. And the food he carries for his poor belly, it
seems, is a match for all the rest.

I am wretched, utterly, and I learn it all far too late. And I say it
against myself: I have come here the worst of men, in the matter of
feeding you. Do not learn what I am from anybody else. But Shame sits
beside Zeus on his throne, over everything men do; let her stand beside
you too, father. There are cures for the wrongs I have done; there is no
adding to them any more.

Why are you silent? Say something, father. Do not turn away from me.
Will you not answer me at all? Will you send me off in dishonour,
without a word, without even telling me what you are angry about?

Seed of this man, my sisters, you at least try to move our father's
mouth, which will not be approached and will not speak, so that he does
not send me off like this, dishonoured, when I am the god's suppliant,
without one word in answer.

Antigone.
Say it yourself, poor man: what you have come needing. A flood of words,
by pleasing somehow, or angering, or stirring pity, has given a voice
before now to people who had none.

Polyneices.
Then I will say it. You guide me well. First I make the god himself my
helper, the god from whose altar the ruler of this land raised me up to
come here, granting me the right to speak and to hear and to leave in
safety. And I will want the same from you, strangers, and from these two
sisters, and from my father.

Now what I came for, father. I want to tell you. I have been driven out
of my own country as an exile, because I claimed, as the elder-born, the
right to sit on your throne, the throne of sole power. For that
Eteocles, my younger brother, thrust me out of the land, not by beating
me in argument, not by coming to any test of hand or deed, but by
persuading the city. And the chief cause of that, I say, is your Fury;
and I hear the same from the prophets.

When I came to Dorian Argos, and took Adrastus as my father-in-law, I
bound to myself by oath every man in the land of Apia who is called
first and honoured with the spear, so that with them I could gather the
army of seven spears against Thebes, and either die in a wholly just
cause or throw the men who did this out of the land.

Well. Why have I come now? To you, father, with a suppliant's prayers,
on my own account and on my allies', who now with seven companies and
seven spears stand round the whole plain of Thebes. There is Amphiaraus,
the spear-shaker, first with the spear and first in the paths of birds;
second, the Aetolian, Tydeus, son of Oeneus; third, Eteoclus, Argive
born; fourth, Hippomedon, sent by his father Talaus; the fifth,
Capaneus, swears he will raze the town of Thebes and burn it to the
ground; sixth, Parthenopaeus the Arcadian comes hurrying, named for the
mother who was long a virgin before she bore him, the true son of
Atalanta; and I, your son, or if not yours, then the child of an evil
fate, but called yours at least, lead the fearless army of Argos
against Thebes.

And all of us, father, beg you by these children and by your own life,
and we ask you to let go of your heavy anger against this man, as he
sets out to punish his brother, who thrust him out and robbed him of his
country. Because if there is anything to trust in oracles, they said the
power would be with whichever side you joined.

So now, by our springs and by the gods of our blood, I ask you to listen
and give way. We are beggars and strangers, and you are a stranger; you
and I live by flattering other men, both of us, with the same fate
allotted to us. And he, the tyrant at home, oh, the misery of it, laughs
at us both and grows soft on it. If you stand with my purpose, I will
scatter him with little trouble and in little time. And then I will
bring you home and set you in your own house, and set myself there too,
once I have thrown him out by force. That is a boast I can make, with
you on my side. Without you, I have not the strength even to survive.
