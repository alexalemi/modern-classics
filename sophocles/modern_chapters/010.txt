The Women of Trachis
(Part 2 of 3)

Lichas.
	I am going. I was a fool to stand here listening to you this long.

Messenger.
	Not until you have answered one short question.

Lichas.
	Ask, if you want something. You are not the silent kind.

Messenger.
	The prisoner you sent into the house. You know the one I mean?

Lichas.
	I do. Why do you ask?

Messenger.
	This girl, then, whom you look at as if you had never seen her —
	did you not say she was Iole, Eurytus's daughter,
	and that you were bringing her here?

Lichas.
	Said it in front of whom? Who is going to come forward,
	and from where, to swear he heard that from me?

Messenger.
	Plenty of the townspeople. In the middle of the market square
	of Trachis a whole crowd heard exactly that from you.

Lichas.
	I said that was what I had heard. Repeating what people guess
	is not the same thing as giving an exact account.

Messenger.
	Guess? Did you not say, and swear to it,
	that the girl you were bringing was to be Heracles' wife?

Lichas.
	I said wife? For the gods' sake, dear mistress, tell me —
	who is this stranger?

Messenger.
	A man who stood there and heard you say
	that a whole city was brought down out of desire for this girl,
	that it was not the Lydian woman who destroyed it
	but the love for her that came into the open.

Lichas.
	Lady, send this person away. Trading nonsense with a sick man
	is not what a man in his right mind does.

Deianeira.
	No. By Zeus, whose lightning strikes down
	the high glens of Oeta, do not cheat me of the truth.
	You will not be telling it to a bad woman,
	or to one who does not know how people are:
	that what we take pleasure in does not stay the same for ever.
	Anyone who squares up to Love like a boxer,
	fists raised, has lost his senses.
	Love rules the gods themselves however he likes,
	and he rules me. So why not another woman, made as I am?
	If I blamed my husband for catching this sickness
	I would be out of my mind —
	or blamed this girl, who shares with him in a thing
	that is no disgrace to her and no harm to me.
	No. But if it was from him that you learned to lie,
	you have learned a lesson that does you no credit;
	and if you are teaching yourself, then when you set out to be kind
	you will turn out to be cruel.
	Tell me the whole truth. For a free man
	to be called a liar is an ugly mark to carry.
	And you will not get away with it, either:
	you told too many people, and they will tell me.
	If you are afraid, you are afraid of the wrong thing.
	Not being told: that is what would hurt me.
	What is so terrible about knowing? Has Heracles, one man,
	not been with more women than anyone could count?
	And not one of them ever had a hard word
	or a reproach from me. Nor will this girl,
	even if she is melting with love for him —
	because when I looked at her I pitied her more than any of them.
	Her beauty has destroyed her life,
	and, poor thing, without meaning to, she has wrecked
	her own country and made it a slave.
	Well, let all that run with the wind.
	But I am telling you: cheat other people if you like.
	With me, always tell the truth.

Chorus.
	Do what she says. It is good advice. You will not regret it
	with this lady later, and you will have my thanks as well.

Lichas.
	Dear mistress — now that I see you are a mortal
	who thinks as a mortal should, and not unreasonable,
	I will tell you the whole truth and hide nothing.
	It is as this man says.
	A terrible longing for this girl went through Heracles
	once; and it was for her sake that Oechalia,
	her father's city, was ruined, brought down by his spear.
	And — I must say what is on his side too —
	he never told me to hide it, and never denied it.
	It was I, lady, afraid of hurting you
	with news like this, who went wrong —
	if you count it as going wrong at all.
	But now you know the whole story:
	for his sake and for yours alike,
	put up with the woman, and let the words
	you spoke about her just now stand for good.
	Because he, who wins every other fight with his hands,
	has been beaten by his love for this girl, completely.

Deianeira.
	That is how I think too, and that is what I will do.
	I will not take on a second sickness of my own
	by fighting the gods, which nobody wins.
	Let us go inside, so that you can carry my messages,
	and the gifts that have to go back in return for gifts —
	take those with you too. It would not be right
	for you to go back empty-handed,
	after arriving with such a train behind you.

(They go into the house.)

Chorus.
	Great is the strength Aphrodite carries off in victory, always.
	What she did to the gods I pass over —
	how she fooled the son of Cronus, Zeus himself, I will not say,
	nor Hades of the dark, nor Poseidon who shakes the earth.
	But for this bride —
	who were the two great rivals who came down for her before the wedding?
	Who went out to the contest of blows and dust?

Chorus.
	One was the strength of a river, the shape of a bull,
	high-horned, four-legged: Achelous, from Oeniadae.
	The other came from Thebes, the city of Bacchus,
	shaking his springing bow, his spears, and his club —
	the son of Zeus. The two of them came together then
	into the middle, hungry for her bed;
	and Aphrodite alone, who makes the marriage, sat between them with the umpire's rod.

Chorus.
	Then there was the thud of fists and the twang of the bow
	and the bull's horns all mixed in together;
	there were legs locked and twisted,
	there were deadly blows from the foreheads,
	and groaning from both.
	And the girl, soft, lovely-eyed,
	sat on a hillside that could be seen far off,
	waiting for whichever would be her husband.
	The fight rages on, as I tell it;
	but the bride's face, the prize they fought for,
	waits, pitiable, for the end.
	And suddenly she has gone from her mother,
	like a calf left on its own.

(Deianeira comes out of the house alone.)

Deianeira.
Friends — while our guest is inside saying goodbye to the captive girls,
on the point of leaving, I have slipped out of the house to you without
his knowing. Partly to tell you what my hands have been contriving.
Partly to have you grieve with me over what I am going through.

I have taken in a girl — no, I think, not a girl any longer, but a
woman who has been yoked to a man — the way a sailor takes on cargo: a
piece of merchandise that will wreck my peace of mind. And now we are
two women waiting under one blanket for one man's embrace. That is what
Heracles, the man I called faithful and good, has sent home to me as
payment for keeping his house all this long time.

I do not know how to be angry with him. He has caught this sickness
badly, and it has happened many times. But to live in the same house
with her, sharing the same marriage — what woman could do it? I can see
her youth coming into flower while mine is fading; and a man's eye loves
to snatch the flower, and turns its step away from what is faded. So I
am afraid of this: that Heracles will be called my husband and be the
younger woman's man.

But as I said, for a woman with any sense, anger is not becoming. So I
will tell you, friends, the way I have found of getting release and
relief.

I had an old gift, from a monster of the old days, hidden in a bronze
jar. I was still a girl when I took it from the shaggy-chested centaur
Nessus, as he lay dying, out of his own wound. He was the one who used
to carry people in his arms, for a fee, across the deep-running river
Evenus — no oars to row them over, no ship's sail. He carried me too, on
his shoulders, the first time I went with Heracles as his wife, when my
father sent me on my way; and when I was in midstream he put his hands
on me where he had no business. I screamed, and Zeus's son turned round
at once and let fly a feathered arrow from his hands. It went whistling
through his chest into the lungs. And as he died, the monster said this
much to me:

"Child of old Oeneus, you shall get this much good out of my ferrying,
if you do what I say, since you are the last passenger I ever carried.
Take the blood clotted round my wound in your hands, at the place where
the arrow was dipped in the black gall of the Hydra, the creature of
Lerna, and you will have a charm for Heracles' heart, so that he will
never look at any woman and love her more than you."

I thought of this, friends — since his death it had been kept locked
away safely in the house — and I dipped this robe in it, doing
everything he told me while he was alive. And it is done. I hope I never
know anything of wicked daring, never learn it; I hate the women who go
in for it. But if there is any way I can get the better of this girl,
with love-charms and spells laid on Heracles — that is the plan, and it
is ready. Unless you think I am doing something foolish. If so, I will
stop at once.

Chorus.
If there is anything to trust in what has been done, we think you have
not planned badly.

Deianeira.
The trust goes this far: it looks as though it will work. But I have
never put it to the test.

Chorus.
You will have to act to find out. However sure you feel, you cannot know
a thing until you have tried it.

Deianeira.
Well, we shall know soon. There he is, already at the door; he will be
gone in a moment. Only — keep this well covered for me. Do a shameful
thing in the dark, and you will never fall into shame.

(Lichas comes out.)

Lichas.
What are your orders? Tell me, daughter of Oeneus. I have stayed so long
that I am late already.

Deianeira.
That is exactly what I have been seeing to for you, Lichas, while you
were talking to the foreign women inside: that you should carry this for
me. A long-woven robe, a gift to my husband from my own hands. When you
give it to him, tell him this. No one is to put it on his body before he
does. It is not to be seen by the light of the sun, or inside a holy
enclosure, or by the fire on a hearth, until he stands out in the open,
in full view, and shows it to the gods on the day the bulls are
sacrificed. That was my vow: if I ever saw him come home safe, or heard
for certain that he had, I would dress him in this robe and show him to
the gods as a new sacrificer in a new garment. And you will carry a
token of it, which he will easily recognise: the mark inside this seal.
Now go. And first, keep to the rule that a messenger does not take it
upon himself to do more than he was sent to do; and second, see to it
that his thanks and mine come together, so that you earn double instead
of single.

Lichas.
If I practise Hermes' craft of carrying messages at all soundly, I will
never once slip in your service. I will carry this box and show it
exactly as it is, and add the assurance of the words you have told me to
go with it.

Deianeira.
You can go now. You know very well how things stand here in the house.

Lichas.
I know, and I will tell him that all is well.

Deianeira.
And you know, from your own eyes, how I received the foreign girl — how
warmly I welcomed her.

Lichas.
So warmly it struck my heart with pleasure.

Deianeira.
Then what else is there for you to tell him? I am afraid it would be
too early to speak of my longing for him, before I know whether I am
longed for over there.

(Lichas goes out along the road. Deianeira goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	You who live beside the harbour and the rocks
	and the hot springs, and the crags of Oeta;
	you who live along the inner shore of the Malian gulf
	and the beach of the girl with the golden shuttle, Artemis,
	where the Greeks hold their famous assemblies
	at the Gates, at Thermopylae:

Chorus.
	soon the flute with its lovely voice
	will rise up for you again, not with a harsh
	and grating clang, but with music like the lyre, fit for the gods.
	Because the son that Alcmena bore to Zeus
	is hurrying home,
	carrying the spoils of all his excellence.

Chorus.
	We had him out of the city altogether, somewhere,
	while we waited twelve months,
	out on the sea, knowing nothing.
	And his loving wife, poor woman,
	wretched, with a wretched heart,
	was wasting away, always in tears.
	But now Ares, stung to fury,
	has set her free from the days of toil.

Chorus.
	Let him come, let him come. Let the many-oared ship
	that carries him not stop
	before he reaches this town,
	leaving the island altar
	where they say he is sacrificing.
	Let him come from there all desire,
	steeped through with the charm of Persuasion,
	with the robe for its excuse.

(Deianeira comes out of the house.)

Deianeira.
Women, I am afraid I may have gone too far in everything I was doing
just now.

Chorus.
What is it, Deianeira, daughter of Oeneus?

Deianeira.
I do not know. But I am losing heart. I think I will soon turn out to
have done a great wrong, out of a good hope.

Chorus.
Surely not something to do with your gift to Heracles?

Deianeira.
Exactly that. So much so that I would never advise anyone to be eager
to act when they cannot see what the act will do.

Chorus.
Tell us, if it can be told, what you are afraid of.

Deianeira.
Something has happened, women, that if I tell it, you will hear as a
wonder nobody could have expected. The thing I was using just now to
anoint the festal robe — a white tuft of wool from a fleecy sheep — has
vanished. Nothing in the house ate it. It ate itself, wasted away on its
own, and crumbled to nothing on the top of a stone slab. So that you can
know the whole of it, how this came about, I will tell it at more
length.

Of the instructions the centaur gave me, the monster, while he was in
agony from the bitter arrowhead in his side, I left out nothing. I kept
them as if they were writing cut in a bronze tablet that no hand could
wash away. This was what he told me, and this is what I did: keep this
drug in the innermost part of the house, away from fire and never
touched by a warm ray of sun, until I should spread it fresh on
something and put it to use. And so I had done. And just now, when the
time came to act, I anointed the robe secretly, indoors, with a tuft of
wool pulled from one of our own sheep; then I folded my gift up and laid
it, out of the sunlight, in its hollow chest, as you saw.

But as I am going back inside, I see a thing there are no words for,
that no human mind could work out. Somehow I had happened to throw the
tuft of wool I was anointing with into the full blaze of the sun's
rays. As it warmed, it ran all together into nothing and crumbled away
on the ground, looking most of all like the crumbs you would see left
where a saw has cut through wood. That is how it lies where it fell. And
out of the earth where it lay, clotted foam is seething up, like the
thick juice of the blue fruit from Bacchus's vine when it is poured on
the ground.

Deianeira.
So I do not know, in my misery, which way to turn my mind. I only see
that I have done a terrible thing. Why, in return for what, would the
monster have wished me well as he died, when I was the reason he was
dying? It is impossible. No — he wanted to destroy the man who shot
him, and he was casting his spell on me to do it. And I am learning
that now, too late, when it is no use any more. If my judgement is not
wrong, I alone, wretched as I am, am going to destroy him. Because I
know that the arrow that struck the centaur hurt even a god, Cheiron,
and that it kills every kind of animal it touches. And this black
poison of the blood, which came out through Nessus's wound from that
same arrow — how can it fail to kill Heracles too? In my judgement, at
least, it cannot. And yet I have decided: if he is to fall, I will die
with him in the same fall, at the same moment. No woman who cares
more than anything about not being evil by nature could bear to live
and be called evil.

Chorus.
When terrible things are done, fear is unavoidable. But you should not
judge your hope before the outcome.

Deianeira.
Where the plan is bad there is no hope — not the kind of hope that gives
you any confidence.

Chorus.
But with people whose mistake was not of their own choosing, anger
softens. That is what should happen with you.

Deianeira.
That is what someone would say who had no share in the harm — not
someone with trouble of her own at home.

Chorus.
It would be best to say nothing more, unless you mean to say it to your
own son. He is here — the one who went off before to look for his
father.

(Hyllus comes in.)

Hyllus.
Mother. I wish I could pick one of three things for you: that you were
no longer alive; or that if you were spared, you were somebody else's
mother; or that from somewhere you could exchange the heart you have
for a better one.

Deianeira.
What is it, my son? What have I done that you hate?

Hyllus.
Your husband — I mean my father — know that you have killed him. Today.

Deianeira.
Oh. What is this you have brought out, child?

Hyllus.
Something that cannot fail to be fulfilled. What has come into the
light — who could make it not have happened?

Deianeira.
What are you saying, my son? From whom did you learn that I have done a
thing so far from anyone's envy, that you charge me with it?

Hyllus.
From nobody. I saw my father's crushing misfortune with my own eyes. I
did not hear it from anyone's tongue.

Deianeira.
Where did you find him? Where were you at his side?

Hyllus.
If you have to know, then I must tell all of it.

After he had sacked the famous city of Eurytus and was on his way with
the trophies of victory and the first pick of the spoils — there is a
headland of Euboea washed by the sea on both sides, Cape Cenaeum, where
he was marking out altars and a leafy sacred precinct for Zeus, his
father's god. That is where I first saw him, and I was glad; I had been
longing for it. He was about to make a great sacrifice, with many
victims, when his own herald, Lichas, arrived from home carrying your
gift — the deadly robe. He put it on, exactly as you had instructed, and
began by killing twelve bulls without a blemish, the first fruits of the
plunder; but altogether he brought a hundred beasts of every kind to the
altar.

And at first, poor wretch, he prayed with a calm mind, glad of the
finery and the dress. But when the bloody flame began to blaze up from
the solemn offerings and the resinous pine, sweat came up on his skin,
and the robe clung to his sides at every joint, close-glued, as if a
craftsman had fitted it. Then a biting pain came into his bones, and
wrenched him; and then the poison began to eat him, like the venom of
some deadly, hateful viper.

At that he shouted for Lichas, poor devil, who had no part in your
crime, and demanded to know by what trick he had brought this robe. And
Lichas, knowing nothing, poor wretch, said it was your gift and yours
alone, exactly as it had been sent. When my father heard that, and a
spasm of agony took hold of his lungs, he grabbed him by the foot, where
the ankle turns in its socket, and hurled him at a rock washed by the
sea. The white brain came spurting out through his hair, with the middle
of his skull smashed open and the blood along with it.

Hyllus.
And all the people cried out and wailed at the sight of them — the one
in his sickness, the other destroyed — and nobody dared to go near the
man. He was dragged to the ground and hauled into the air, shouting,
screaming; and the rocks all round rang with it, the mountain headlands
of Locris and the capes of Euboea.

When he had worn himself out with throwing himself on the ground, over
and over, and howling, over and over, cursing the bed he had made with
you, wretched woman, and the marriage into the house of Oeneus, which he
said he had got himself as the ruin of his life — then, out of the smoke
that hung round the altar, he lifted his rolling eyes and saw me,
weeping, in the middle of the great crowd of soldiers. He looked straight
at me and called me.

"My son, come here. Do not run away from my suffering, not even if you
have to die with me as I die. Lift me and carry me out of here; best of
all, put me somewhere no human being will see me. But if you have any
pity, at least ferry me out of this country as fast as you can. Do not
let me die here."

That was his order, and we laid him in the middle of a boat and brought
him to this shore, only just, bellowing in his convulsions. And you will
see him soon, either alive or just dead.

That, Mother, is what you have been caught planning against my father,
and doing. May Justice that punishes, and the Fury, pay you back for it.
If it is lawful to pray so, I pray it. And it is lawful, because you
threw the law away first, when you killed the best man of all the men on
earth. You will never look at his like again.

(Deianeira turns and goes towards the house without a word.)

Chorus.
Why are you going off in silence? Do you not know that by saying
nothing you are speaking on the side of the man who accuses you?

Hyllus.
Let her go. Let a fair wind carry her far out of my sight. Why should
she keep up the dignity of the name "mother" for nothing, when nothing
she does is what a mother does? Let her go, and much good may it do her.
And the pleasure she is giving my father — may she get the same herself.

(Deianeira goes into the house. Hyllus follows.)

Chorus.
	Look, girls, how suddenly it has come upon us,
	the god's word spoken long ago,
	the old prophecy which foresaw it all:
	that when the twelfth year of ploughing
	had run its full count of months, it would bring
	the end of the labours for Zeus's own son.
	And this is coming true, on a steady wind, exactly.
	For how could a man who no longer sees the light
	ever again have any painful labour to serve, once he is dead?

Chorus.
	If the deadly cloud of the centaur
	is smeared on his sides by a trap that had to close,
	with the poison melted into him
	that Death gave birth to and the glittering snake fed —
	how can he see any other sun than this one,
	fused to the Hydra's shape,
	the most terrible of all? And along with that,
	the murderous goads of black-haired Nessus,
	set on him by treacherous words, boil in him and torture him.

Chorus.
	And this poor woman, seeing the great harm
	rushing at her house from the new marriage,
	did not hold back: part of it she brought on herself,
	and part came from a stranger's mind,
	in a fatal exchange.
	Surely she groans in ruin now;
	surely she wets a pale dew
	of thick tears.
	And the fate that is coming shows ahead of it
	a treacherous, enormous ruin.

Chorus.
	The spring of tears has burst. The sickness has poured out — oh,
	such suffering as no enemy ever brought
	on glorious Heracles, to be pitied.
	Oh, dark point of the spear that led the fight,
	which brought that bride so swiftly then
	down from the heights of Oechalia at the spear's edge —
	and Aphrodite, silent, in attendance,
	stands revealed as the one who did all this.
