Philoctetes
(Part 3 of 3)

Neoptolemus.
What do we do, men?

(Odysseus comes out from where he has been hiding, with two of his men
behind him.)

Odysseus.
You worst of men, what are you doing? Get back here and hand that bow to
me.

Philoctetes.
Oh. Who is that? Is that Odysseus I hear?

Odysseus.
Odysseus. Be sure of it. Me, the man you are looking at.

Philoctetes.
Oh. I am sold, and I am finished. So this was the man who trapped me and
stripped me of my weapons.

Odysseus.
I did. Be sure of that too. Nobody else. I admit it freely.

Philoctetes.
Give it back. Boy, let me have the bow.

Odysseus.
That he will never do, not even if he wanted to. And what is more, you
are coming with it. Or these men will take you by force.

Philoctetes.
Me? You worst of the bad, you most shameless of them all, these men will
take me by force?

Odysseus.
If you will not crawl there on your own.

Philoctetes.
Land of Lemnos. Fire that Hephaestus made, that masters everything. Are
you going to put up with this, this man dragging me off your ground by
force?

Odysseus.
It is Zeus, understand that. Zeus, who rules this land. Zeus, who has
decided it. I am only his servant.

Philoctetes.
You hateful thing. What things you find to say. You hold the gods up in
front of you, and you make liars of the gods.

Odysseus.
No. Truth-tellers. And the road has to be walked.

Philoctetes.
I say no.

Odysseus.
And I say yes. You will do as you are told.

Philoctetes.
Oh, I am wretched. So my father bred me a slave, plainly, and not a free
man.

Odysseus.
No. The equal of the best men there are, the men you are going to take
Troy with, and pull it down by force.

Philoctetes.
Never. Not if I have to suffer every evil there is. Not while this
island's steep rock is under my feet.

Odysseus.
What are you going to do?

Philoctetes.
Throw myself off. This head of mine, I will bloody it on the rocks
below, right now.

Odysseus.
Hold him, both of you. Do not let him have that.

Philoctetes.
My hands. Look what is done to you, for want of the bowstring you love,
hunted down, both of you, and roped by this man.

And you, you who have never had one healthy thought or one free one, how
you crept up on me again. How you hunted me. You took this boy for your
shield, a boy I did not know, who was too good for you and good enough
for me; who knew nothing except to do what he was ordered, and who is
plainly in pain now, even now, over what he did wrong himself and over
what I have suffered. But that bad soul of yours, always peering out
from some corner, taught him well. He had no gift for it and no wish for
it, and still you taught him to be clever at evil.

And now, you wretch, you mean to tie me up and take me from this shore,
the same shore where you threw me out: no friend, no company, no city, a
corpse among the living. Ah. Damn you. I have prayed that for you often
enough. But the gods deal me out nothing sweet, so you are alive and
glad of it, and I have the pain, this exact pain, that I am alive at
all, with all these evils on me, and laughed at by you and by the two
sons of Atreus, the pair of generals you do these errands for. And yet
you sailed with them only when they yoked you by a trick and forced you.
I sailed of my own free will, with seven ships, to my utter ruin, and
they threw me out with no honour. So you say. They say it was you.

And now what are you taking me for? Why haul me off? What for? I am
nothing. To you I have been dead for years. How is it, you thing the
gods hate most, that I am not lame to you any more, and not stinking?
How will you burn your offerings to the gods if I sail with you? How
will you pour them a drink? That was your excuse for throwing me out in
the first place.

A bad death to you. And you will die badly, for the wrong you have done
this man, if the gods care anything for justice. And I know they care.
You would never have sailed on this errand for one miserable man if some
goad from the gods had not driven you here after me.

Land of my fathers. Gods who look down and see. Pay them back. Pay them
back, all of them, in time, at last, if there is any pity in you for me.
My life is a pitiful thing. But if I saw those men destroyed, I would
think I had got clear of my sickness.

Chorus.
That is a hard man, Odysseus, and hard words he has said. His troubles
have not bent him.

Odysseus.
I could say a good deal in answer to that, if I had the time. As it is,
I have one thing to say. Where a certain kind of man is needed, I am
that kind of man. And where the contest is one of just and good men, you
would not find anyone more god-fearing than me. It is my nature to want
to win, everywhere, except against you. To you, this once, I will gladly
give way.

Let him go. Take your hands off him. Let him stay. We have no further
need of you, not now we have these weapons. Teucer is with us, and he
has the skill; and so have I. I think I could handle this bow no worse
than you, and point it no worse. So what do we need you for? Goodbye.
Enjoy walking about on Lemnos. We are leaving. And perhaps this prize of
yours will bring me the honour that should have been yours.

Philoctetes.
Oh, what am I to do, in my bad luck? Are you going to show yourself to
the Greeks decked out in my weapons?

Odysseus.
Not another word to me. I am leaving.

Philoctetes.
Seed of Achilles, will you not even speak to me? Are you going to walk
off like that?

Odysseus.
Move. Do not look at him. You are a decent man, and you will wreck our
luck.

Philoctetes.
And you, strangers, will you leave me here too, alone like this? Have
you no pity for me?

Chorus.
The boy here commands our ship. Whatever he says to you, we say too.

Neoptolemus.
I am going to be told by this man that I am too full of pity. All the
same, stay, if he agrees to it, for as long as it takes the sailors to
get the ship ready and for us to make our prayers to the gods. In that
time perhaps he will come to think better of us. We two are going. And
you, when we call, come quickly.

(Odysseus and Neoptolemus go out towards the ships. Philoctetes is left
with the Chorus.)

Philoctetes.
	Hollow cave in the rock,
	hot, and then freezing. So I was never
	going to leave you after all. Poor fool.
	You will be with me while I die.
	Oh. Oh, oh.
	Wretched shelter, filled to the brim
	with the pain that came out of me,
	what will there be for me now, day by day?
	Where will I find any hope of food?
	Who will feed me?
	Up there the timid doves
	will go by on the shrill wind.
	I cannot stop them any more.

Chorus.
	You did this. You, I say. You chose it,
	you and your heavy luck.
	This did not come from anywhere else,
	from some power stronger than you.
	You could have seen sense,
	and you chose to praise the worse fate
	over the better.

Philoctetes.
	Wretched, then. Wretched,
	and broken by suffering. From now on
	I live here with no man after this,
	and I die here.
	Aiai. Aiai.
	No more bringing home food,
	no more winged arrows in my strong hands
	to take it with.
	Words I could not see through, words with a lie inside,
	from a crooked mind, crept under me.
	Let me see him,
	the man who planned it,
	allotted my pain for exactly as long as I have had it.

Chorus.
	Fate. Fate from the gods did this to you.
	No trick from my hand caught you.
	Turn that hateful, ill-fated curse of yours
	onto other men.
	Because I care about this too:
	do not push away my friendship.

Philoctetes.
	Oh, oh. Somewhere on the grey shore
	he is sitting, laughing at me,
	tossing in his hand the thing that fed me,
	my poor life's food, the bow nobody else ever carried.
	Dear bow, forced out of the hands that loved you,
	if you have any feeling
	you must look with pity
	at the friend of Heracles, who will never use you again.
	Now you are handled in another man's turn,
	a man of many devices;
	you are looking at shameful tricks,
	at a hateful face, my enemy's,
	and at the countless evils
	that rose out of shameful things,
	the evils he planned against me. Oh, Zeus.

Chorus.
	A man should always say what is right.
	But once he has said it,
	let him not spit out spiteful, stinging words.
	That man was one man, picked from many,
	and at their command he did
	what was a service to all his friends.

Philoctetes.
	Winged things I hunted,
	and tribes of bright-eyed beasts
	that this ground feeds on the hills,
	you need not leap from your dens
	and run from me any more. My hands
	do not hold the strength of the arrows they held before.
	I am wretched now.
	Come freely. The place is not fearful
	to you any more. Crawl in.
	Now it is a fine time to fill your mouths
	with blood for blood,
	on my discoloured flesh, all you want.
	I will be leaving life soon.
	Where would food come from?
	Who feeds on the winds like this,
	with no power any more over anything
	the life-giving earth sends up?

Chorus.
	By the gods, if you have any respect for a stranger,
	come near to one who comes near you with nothing but good will.
	But know this. Know it well:
	it is in your own hands to escape this doom.
	It is a pitiful thing to feed it,
	and to have no way of learning to carry
	the endless weight that lives with it.

Philoctetes.
	Again. Again you have brought the old pain back to me,
	you, the best of all who ever came here.
	Why have you destroyed me? What have you done to me?

Chorus.
	What do you mean?

Philoctetes.
	If you hoped to take me
	to the land of Troy, which I hate.

Chorus.
	I think that is the best thing.

Philoctetes.
	Then leave me. Now.

Chorus.
	Gladly. Gladly. That is an order I am happy to obey.
	Let us go, let us go,
	each to his place in the ship.

Philoctetes.
	No. By Zeus who hears a curse, do not go. I beg you.

Chorus.
	Be reasonable.

Philoctetes.
	Strangers. Stay. By the gods.

Chorus.
	Why are you shouting?

Philoctetes.
	Aiai. Aiai. My god, my god.
	I am finished. Foot, my foot,
	what am I going to do with you
	for the rest of my life?
	Strangers, come back to me. Come back.

Chorus.
	To do what? With what different mind
	from what you showed us before?

Philoctetes.
	There is no call for anger
	when a man is out of his wits
	with a storm of pain, and talks nonsense.

Chorus.
	Then come, poor man, the way I tell you.

Philoctetes.
	Never. Never. Understand that. It is fixed.
	Not even if the lord of the fire and the lightning
	comes to burn me in the blaze of his thunder.
	Damn Troy, and every man under its walls
	who could bear to send this foot of mine away.
	But, strangers, give me one thing I ask.

Chorus.
	What thing is that?

Philoctetes.
	A sword, if you have one anywhere,
	or an axe, or any weapon. Hand it to me.

Chorus.
	So you can do what with it?

Philoctetes.
	Cut off all this flesh, cut off my limbs, with my own hand.
	My mind is on killing. Killing, now.

Chorus.
	Why?

Philoctetes.
	Looking for my father.

Chorus.
	Where on earth?

Philoctetes.
	In Hades. He is not in the light any more.
	My city. My father's country.
	How could I look at you, wretched man that I am,
	who left your holy river
	and went to help the Greeks, my enemies.
	I am nothing now. Nothing.

(He goes into the cave.)

Chorus.
I would have been on my way to my ship long ago, if I had not seen
Odysseus coming this way, and the son of Achilles with him, heading for
us.

(Neoptolemus comes in fast, carrying the bow. Odysseus is behind him.)

Odysseus.
Will you tell me why you have turned round and come back up this road in
such a hurry?

Neoptolemus.
To undo the wrong I did before.

Odysseus.
That is an alarming thing to say. What wrong?

Neoptolemus.
The one I did when I obeyed you and the whole army —

Odysseus.
What did you do that was beneath you?

Neoptolemus.
I took a man with shameful tricks and lies.

Odysseus.
Which man? Oh no. You are not planning something new?

Neoptolemus.
Nothing new. But to the son of Poeas —

Odysseus.
What are you going to do? Something is frightening me.

Neoptolemus.
— from whom I took this bow, back again —

Odysseus.
Zeus. What are you going to say? You do not mean to give it back?

Neoptolemus.
I do. I got it shamefully, and with no right to it.

Odysseus.
By the gods, are you saying this to mock me?

Neoptolemus.
If telling the truth is mockery.

Odysseus.
What are you saying, son of Achilles? What are these words?

Neoptolemus.
Do you want me to go over the same thing twice and three times?

Odysseus.
I would rather not have heard it once.

Neoptolemus.
Then you have heard all I have to say. Be sure of that.

Odysseus.
There is someone, there is, who will stop you doing it.

Neoptolemus.
What? Who is going to stop me?

Odysseus.
The whole people of the Greeks. And I am one of them.

Neoptolemus.
Clever as you were born, there is nothing clever in what you are saying.

Odysseus.
And nothing clever in what you say, or in what you want to do.

Neoptolemus.
But if it is just, it is better than clever.

Odysseus.
And how is it just, to let go of what you won by my planning?

Neoptolemus.
I made a shameful mistake. I am going to try to take it back.

Odysseus.
And the Greek army does not frighten you, doing this?

Neoptolemus.
With justice on my side, I am not afraid of the fear you are offering
me.

Odysseus.
With justice on my side, my hand will make you.

Neoptolemus.
Not your hand either. I am not going to be made to do it.

Odysseus.
Then it is not the Trojans we fight. It is you.

Neoptolemus.
Let what is coming come.

Odysseus.
Do you see my right hand, on the hilt of my sword?

Neoptolemus.
You will see mine do the same. And not slowly.

Odysseus.
Still, I will leave you be. I am going to tell all this to the whole
army, and it will punish you.

Neoptolemus.
Now you are being sensible. Go on thinking like that and perhaps you
will keep your feet out of trouble.

(Odysseus goes, but not far.)

Son of Poeas. Philoctetes, I mean you. Come out. Leave that stone roof
of yours.

(Philoctetes appears at the mouth of the cave.)

Philoctetes.
What is this shouting again at my cave? Why are you calling me out? What
do you want, strangers? Oh. This is bad. Have you come to bring me new
evils on top of the old?

Neoptolemus.
Take heart. Listen to what I have come to tell you.

Philoctetes.
I am afraid to. Fine words did me harm before, when I believed what you
said.

Neoptolemus.
Is there no room for a man to change his mind?

Philoctetes.
You talked like this when you were stealing my bow. To be trusted, and
ruin underneath.

Neoptolemus.
Not now. Not this time. I want to hear from you: have you decided to
stay here and stick it out, or to sail with us?

Philoctetes.
Stop. Not another word. Anything you say will be said for nothing.

Neoptolemus.
That is decided?

Philoctetes.
More firmly than I can say.

Neoptolemus.
Well. I would have liked you to be persuaded by what I say. But if
nothing I say is any use, I am finished.

Philoctetes.
Everything you say is for nothing. You will never have my good will, you
who took my life away with tricks, robbed me of it, and then come here
to lecture me. The worst son of the best father. Damn all of you: the
sons of Atreus first, then the son of Laertes, then you.

Neoptolemus.
No more curses. Take these weapons from my hand.

Philoctetes.
What did you say? Am I being tricked a second time?

Neoptolemus.
No. I swear it by the holy majesty of Zeus most high.

Philoctetes.
The dearest words I ever heard, if they are true.

Neoptolemus.
What I do will make it plain. Hold out your right hand. Be master of
your weapons.

(He gives him the bow. Odysseus steps out.)

Odysseus.
And I forbid it, with the gods as my witnesses, in the name of the sons
of Atreus and the whole army.

Philoctetes.
Boy, whose voice was that? Was that Odysseus I heard?

Odysseus.
It was. And you can see him, close by: the man who is going to ship you
to the plains of Troy by force, whether the son of Achilles likes it or
not.

Philoctetes.
Not gladly, you will not. Not if this arrow flies straight.

(He fits an arrow to the string. Odysseus runs.)

Neoptolemus.
No. No, by the gods, do not let it go.

Philoctetes.
Let go of my hand, by the gods. Dear boy, let go.

Neoptolemus.
I will not.

Philoctetes.
Ah. Why did you stop me killing him? A man who hates me, an enemy, with
my own bow.

Neoptolemus.
Because it would be no credit to me, and none to you.

Philoctetes.
Well, know this much anyway: the first men of the army, the Greeks'
lying heralds, are cowards with a spear and bold with words.

Neoptolemus.
Good. You have the bow, and there is nothing left for you to be angry
with me about, or to blame me for.

Philoctetes.
Agreed. You have shown the stock you sprang from, boy. Not Sisyphus for
a father. Achilles, who had the best name of all men while he was alive,
and has it now among the dead.

Neoptolemus.
I am glad to hear you speak well of my father, and of me. Now hear what
I want from you. Men have to bear what the gods send them. But when men
sit inside a harm they chose for themselves, as you do, there is no
justice in forgiving them, and no justice in pitying them either. You
have gone wild. You will take no advice; if someone tries to set you
right, meaning nothing but good, you hate him and count him an enemy who
wishes you ill.

I am going to say it anyway, and I call on Zeus who watches over oaths.
Understand this, and write it inside your mind. You are sick with this
pain because a god sent it, because you came too close to the guardian
of Chryse, the snake that keeps hidden watch over her open-air shrine.
And know that you will never find rest from this heavy sickness, as long
as this same sun rises on one side of the sky and sets on the other,
until you come of your own free will to the plains of Troy and meet the
two sons of Asclepius, the healers, who are with us, and are eased of
this sickness, and are seen to take the towers of Troy, with this bow
and with me.

How I know this is so, I will tell you. We have a man taken from Troy,
Helenus, the best of the prophets, who says plainly that this must
happen. And more: that this very summer all Troy must fall. If he is
lying, he offers himself up freely to be killed.

So now that you know it, give way, and willingly. It is a fine thing to
gain: to be judged the one best man of the Greeks, and first to come
into hands that heal you, and then to take Troy, which has cost so many
tears, and win the highest fame there is.

Philoctetes.
Hateful life. Why, why do you keep me up here looking at the light, and
not let me go down to Hades? Oh, what am I to do? How can I refuse to
believe what this man says, when he meant well by me in saying it? Then
give way? And then how do I come out into the light, in my bad luck,
having done that? Who would I speak to?

You eyes that have watched everything that was done around me, how could
you bear to see it: me, keeping company with the sons of Atreus, who
destroyed me? Or with that son of Laertes, ruin to everything he
touches? It is not the pain of what is past that bites me. It is what I
think I can see coming, what I will have to suffer at their hands still.
When a man's mind has once given birth to wrongdoing, it teaches him to
be a wrongdoer in everything after.

And you. I am amazed at you in this. You ought never to go back to Troy
yourself, and you ought to keep me from it. They did you outrage. They
stripped you of your father's prize, when they judged poor Ajax a lesser
man than Odysseus in the contest for the arms. And after that you are
going to fight beside them, and force me to as well? No, boy. Not that.
Do what you swore to me: send me home. Stay on Scyros yourself, and let
bad men die badly. Do that, and you will have thanks from me twice over,
and twice over from your father, and you will not look like a man born
the same as the bad men because you helped them.

Neoptolemus.
What you say is reasonable. But all the same I want you to trust the
gods, and trust what I say, and sail out of this land with me, a friend.

Philoctetes.
To the plains of Troy? To the hateful son of Atreus? On this wretched
foot?

Neoptolemus.
To the men who will stop the pain in that foot, with the pus in it, and
save you from your sickness.

Philoctetes.
That is a terrible thing to recommend. What are you saying?

Neoptolemus.
What I see is best for you and for me, once it is done.

Philoctetes.
You say that, and feel no shame before the gods?

Neoptolemus.
How could a man be ashamed of helping his own?

Philoctetes.
Helping the sons of Atreus, do you mean, or me?

Neoptolemus.
You, I should think. I am your friend, and that is a friend talking.

Philoctetes.
How, when you want to hand me to my enemies?

Neoptolemus.
Sir. Learn not to be so defiant when things are bad.

Philoctetes.
You will destroy me with this talk. I know you.

Neoptolemus.
I will not. I say you do not understand.

Philoctetes.
Do I not know the sons of Atreus threw me out?

Neoptolemus.
They threw you out. Now see whether they will not save you.

Philoctetes.
Never. Not if it means agreeing to look at Troy.

Neoptolemus.
Then what are we to do, if nothing I say can persuade you of anything?
The easiest thing is for me to stop talking, and for you to go on living
as you live now, with no way out.

Philoctetes.
Let me suffer what I have to suffer. But what you promised me with your
right hand in mine, to take me home, do that for me, boy. Do not delay,
and do not mention Troy to me again. I have had enough of weeping and
lamenting.

Neoptolemus.
If that is what you want, let us go.

Philoctetes.
That is a noble thing to say.

Neoptolemus.
Now set your feet firmly against mine.

Philoctetes.
As far as I have the strength.

Neoptolemus.
But how do I escape the blame of the Greeks?

Philoctetes.
Do not think about it.

Neoptolemus.
And if they come and lay waste my country?

Philoctetes.
I will be there —

Neoptolemus.
And what help will you be?

Philoctetes.
— with the arrows of Heracles —

Neoptolemus.
Meaning what?

Philoctetes.
— I will keep them off.

Neoptolemus.
Then kiss the ground goodbye, and come.

(Heracles appears above them.)

Heracles.
	Not yet. Not until you have heard
	what I have to say, son of Poeas.
	Know that it is the voice of Heracles
	your ears are hearing, and his face your eyes see.
	For your sake I have come,
	leaving my seat in the sky,
	to tell you the plans of Zeus
	and to hold you back from the road you are taking.
	Listen to my words.
	First I will tell you my own fortunes:
	how many labours I laboured through to the end,
	and won undying excellence, as you can see.
	For you too, be sure of it, this is what is owed:
	out of these sufferings, to make your life famous.
	You will go with this man to the city of Troy.
	First you will be cured of your cruel sickness.
	Then, judged first of the whole army in excellence,
	you will take Paris, the cause of all this evil,
	and with my bow you will part him from his life.
	You will sack Troy. You will send the spoils home,
	the prize for the bravest, taken from the army,
	to your father Poeas, on the high ground of Oeta.
	And whatever spoils you take from that army,
	carry to my pyre, in memory of my bow.
	And this is for you as well, son of Achilles:
	you cannot take the plain of Troy without him,
	and he cannot without you.
	Guard each other, like two lions who hunt together,
	he you, and you him.
	I will send Asclepius to Troy to end your sickness.
	It is fated to fall a second time to my bow.
	But remember this, when you sack the land:
	show reverence to the gods.
	Father Zeus counts everything else second.
	Reverence does not die when men die.
	Alive or dead, they do not lose it.

Philoctetes.
	Voice I longed for. You sent it to me
	and showed yourself at last.
	I will not disobey what you say.

Neoptolemus.
	And I set my judgement the same way.

Heracles.
	Then do not wait long. Act.
	The moment is right, and the wind
	is pushing at your stern.

Philoctetes.
	Then let me call to the land as I go.
	Goodbye, house that kept watch with me.
	Goodbye, nymphs of the water-meadows,
	and you, deep pounding of the sea on the headland,
	where so often my head was wet
	far inside the cave from the blows of the south wind,
	and often the hill of Hermes sent my own voice back,
	groan for groan, when I was in the storm.
	Now, springs, and you Lycian water,
	I am leaving you, leaving you at last.
	I never thought I would stand on this hope.
	Goodbye, Lemnos, ground with the sea all round you.
	Send me off with a fair wind, and no blame,
	to where great Fate is carrying me,
	and the judgement of my friends, and the god
	who tames everything, who brought this to pass.

Chorus.
	Let us all go now, together,
	after a prayer to the nymphs of the sea
	to come and see us safely home.
