Antigone
(Part 2 of 3)

Creon.
You. Yes, you, with your head bowed to the ground. Do you admit that
you did this, or do you deny it?

Antigone.
I did it. I say so, and I do not deny it.

Creon.
You can take yourself off wherever you like. You are clear of a heavy
charge.

(The guard goes out.)

Now you. Tell me — not at length, briefly — did you know that a
proclamation had forbidden this?

Antigone.
I knew. How could I not? It was public.

Creon.
And you still dared to step over these laws?

Antigone.
Yes. Because it was not Zeus who made that proclamation to me. And
Justice, who lives with the gods below, laid down no such laws among
human beings. I did not think your orders had so much strength in them
that a man — a mortal — could outrun the unwritten and unshakable laws
of the gods. Those are not laws of today or yesterday. They live for
ever, and nobody knows when they first appeared. I was not going to
pay the penalty for breaking them, in front of the gods, because I was
afraid of what some man thinks.

I knew I was going to die — of course I knew — even if you had never
proclaimed a word. And if I die before my time, I call that a gain.
Anyone who lives the way I live, in the middle of so much evil — how
is dying not a gain for them? So to meet this death is a pain that
counts for nothing. But if I had let my own mother's son lie dead and
unburied, that would have hurt. This does not hurt me. And if what I
am doing looks like folly to you, then it may be a fool who is
charging me with folly.

Chorus.
The girl shows the raw stock she comes from — a raw father's raw
daughter. She does not know how to bend to trouble.

Creon.
Then let her know this: it is the stiffest spirits that fall hardest.
The toughest iron, baked in the fire until it is brittle, is the iron
you see snap and shatter most often. And I have seen horses with big
tempers brought to order by a small bit. There is no room for big
thoughts in anyone who is the slave of the people next door.

This girl already knew all about outrage when she stepped over the
laws that had been laid down. And now that she has done it, here is a
second outrage: to boast of it, and to laugh at having done it. Well
then, I am not the man now — she is the man — if she gets this victory
and pays nothing for it. No. Whether she is my sister's child, or
closer to me by blood than anyone who worships Zeus at the altar of my
house, she and her sister will not escape the worst of deaths. Yes,
her sister too. I charge her with an equal share in planning this
burial. Call her out. I saw her inside just now, raving, not in
possession of her wits. The mind of people plotting something crooked
in the dark usually gets caught as a thief before the act. But what I
hate most is when someone is caught in a crime and then wants to make
it look beautiful.

Antigone.
Do you want anything more than to take me and kill me?

Creon.
Nothing. With that, I have everything.

Antigone.
Then what are you waiting for? Nothing you say pleases me, and I hope
nothing ever will; and everything I do is just as hateful to you.
Still, where could I have won a brighter glory than by putting my own
brother in a grave? Every one of these men would say they approved of
it — if fear had not locked their tongues. But a tyrant is lucky in a
great many ways, and one of them is that he can do and say whatever he
likes.

Creon.
You are the only one of these Thebans who sees it that way.

Antigone.
They see it too. They keep their mouths shut for you.

Creon.
And you are not ashamed to think differently from them?

Antigone.
There is no shame in honouring my own flesh and blood.

Creon.
Was the man who died on the other side not your blood too?

Antigone.
My blood. Same mother, same father.

Creon.
Then why pay an honour that is an insult to him?

Antigone.
The dead man will not say so.

Creon.
He will, if you honour him no higher than the traitor.

Antigone.
It was not a slave who died. It was his brother.

Creon.
Attacking this country. The other died defending it.

Antigone.
Hades wants these laws kept all the same.

Creon.
But the good man does not get an equal share with the bad.

Antigone.
Who knows what counts as clean down there?

Creon.
An enemy is never one of your own. Not even when he is dead.

Antigone.
I was not born to share in hating. I was born to share in loving.

Creon.
Then go down there and love them, if you must love someone. While I am
alive, no woman is going to rule.

(Ismene is brought out of the house by two attendants.)

Chorus.
	Look — here is Ismene at the doors,
	weeping a sister's tears;
	a cloud over her brow
	spoils her flushed face
	and wets her lovely cheek.

Creon.
	And you — you who slid about my house like a viper
	and drank my blood while I never saw it,
	never knew I was rearing two ruins,
	two risings against my throne —
	come, tell me. Do you say you had a share
	in this burial too? Or will you swear you knew nothing?

Ismene.
	I did it — if she agrees.
	I take my share of the blame, and I carry it.

Antigone.
	No. Justice will not let you.
	You did not want to, and I did not let you in.

Ismene.
	But with you in trouble like this, I am not ashamed
	to make myself your shipmate in the suffering.

Antigone.
	Whose deed it was, Hades and the dead below know.
	One of my own who loves me in words — I do not love her.

Ismene.
	Sister, do not shut me out of the honour —
	let me die with you, and make the dead man clean.

Antigone.
	Do not die with me. Do not claim what you never touched.
	It is enough that I die.

Ismene.
	What life is there for me, once I have lost you?

Antigone.
	Ask Creon. He is the one you look after.

Ismene.
	Why hurt me like this? It does you no good.

Antigone.
	If I am laughing at you, it hurts me to do it.

Ismene.
	Then what can I do for you, even now?

Antigone.
	Save yourself. I do not grudge you your escape.

Ismene.
	Oh, this is misery. Am I to miss your death as well?

Antigone.
	You chose to live. I chose to die.

Ismene.
	Not without my saying what I had to say.

Antigone.
	One side thought you were right. The other thought I was.

Ismene.
	And yet the fault is the same for both of us.

Antigone.
	Take heart. You are alive. My life died long ago,
	so that I could be of use to the dead.

Creon.
	One of these two girls, I tell you, has just now shown
	she is a fool. The other has been one since the day she was born.

Ismene.
	Whatever sense we are born with, my king,
	does not stay with people in trouble. It leaves them.

Creon.
	Yours left you, when you chose to do evil with the evil.

Ismene.
	What life is there for me alone, without her?

Creon.
	Do not say "her." She no longer exists.

Ismene.
	You will kill your own son's bride?

Creon.
	There are other fields for him to plough.

Ismene.
	None fitted to him the way she was.

Creon.
	I hate a bad wife for a son of mine.

Antigone.
	Haemon — dearest — how your father dishonours you.

Creon.
	You are wearing me out, you and your marriage.

Chorus.
	Will you really take her from your own son?

Creon.
	Hades is the one who will stop this wedding.

Chorus.
	Then it is settled, it seems. She dies.

Creon.
	Settled — for you, and by me. No more delay.
	Take them inside, slaves. From now on
	they must be women, and not left loose.
	Even the bold run for it
	when they see Hades closing in on their life.

(The attendants take Antigone and Ismene into the house. Creon
stays.)

Chorus.
	Lucky are those whose lives have never tasted evil.
	For once a house is shaken by the gods
	no ruin is missing from it; it creeps down
	over the whole crowd of the family —
	like the swell of the sea, when Thracian gales
	blow hard across the dark water under the surface,
	and it rolls up black sand from the bottom,
	and the headlands that take the blows of the wind
	roar back with a groan.

Chorus.
	I see the old sorrows of the house of Labdacus
	falling on the sorrows of the dead.
	One generation does not free the next;
	some god drives it down, and there is no release.
	Just now a light had been spread
	over the last root in the house of Oedipus,
	and now the bloody dust of the gods below
	mows it down in its turn — that,
	and folly in speech, and a fury in the mind.

Chorus.
	Zeus, what overstepping by men
	could hold your power in check?
	Sleep, who catches everything, never takes it;
	the tireless months of the gods do not;
	ageless through time, you hold in your power
	the marble glitter of Olympus.
	For what comes next, and what is far off,
	and what went before, this law will hold:
	nothing enormous enters the life of mortals
	without ruin.

Chorus.
	Hope goes wandering wide, and to many men
	it is a comfort; to many it is a cheat,
	a trap of light-headed desires.
	It creeps up on a man who knows nothing
	until he sets his foot on hot fire.
	Somebody wise once made a saying famous:
	evil looks like good, sooner or later,
	to the man whose mind a god
	is leading towards ruin —

Chorus.
	and he lives only the briefest time clear of ruin.

(Haemon comes in.)

Chorus.
	But here is Haemon, the last born
	of your children. Does he come grieving
	for Antigone, the bride he was promised —
	sick at heart over the cheat of his marriage?

Creon.
We shall know soon enough, better than any prophet could tell us. My
son — you have heard the final vote on the girl you were going to
marry. Have you come here raging at your father? Or are we still your
own, whatever we do?

Haemon.
Father, I am yours. You have good judgement, and you set me straight
by it, and I will follow it. No marriage will count for more with me
than your good guidance.

Creon.
Yes, my boy. That is how to carry it in your heart: stand behind your
father's judgement in everything. That is why men pray to father
obedient children and keep them in their homes — so that they pay back
their father's enemy with harm and honour his friend exactly as their
father does. And the man who fathers useless children — what has he
done, would you say, except breed trouble for himself and a great deal
of laughter for his enemies?

So do not, my boy, ever throw your good sense away for pleasure,
because of a woman. Know that it turns into a cold thing to hold in
your arms — a bad woman sharing your bed and your house. What wound
could go deeper than a false friend? No. Spit her out like an enemy,
and let her go and marry somebody in Hades. I caught her — the one
person in the whole city — in open disobedience, and I will not make a
liar of myself in front of the city. I will kill her. Let her sing her
hymns to Zeus of the blood-tie. If I rear my own family to be
disorderly, the people outside the family will certainly go the same
way. A man who is straight in his own household will be seen to be
just in the city too. But anyone who oversteps and does violence to
the laws, or thinks he can give orders to those in power, gets no
praise from me. Whoever the city sets up, that man must be obeyed — in
small things and in just things and in their opposites. That is the
man I would trust to rule well, and to be willing to be ruled well,
and to hold the post he was given in a storm of spears, a just and
good man at your side in the line.

There is no worse evil than anarchy. It destroys cities; it turns
houses upside down; it breaks the allied ranks and sends them running.
But where men stand firm, it is obedience that saves most of their
lives. So we must defend good order, and never, in any way, let
ourselves be beaten by a woman. If we must fall, better to fall to a
man. Then nobody could call us weaker than women.

Chorus.
To us — unless age has robbed us of our wits — you seem to speak
sensibly about what you are speaking of.

Haemon.
Father, the gods plant reason in human beings, and of everything we
own it is the highest thing. To say that you are wrong in what you
have said — I could not do it, and I hope I never learn how. But
another man might have something right to say too. And it is my
nature to watch, on your behalf, everything people say or do or find
to blame. Your eye is a frightening thing to an ordinary citizen, when
it comes to words you would not enjoy hearing. But I can hear these
things, in the dark: how the city is grieving for this girl — how of
all women she least deserves to die so shamefully, for deeds so
glorious. Her own brother had fallen in the slaughter, and she would
not leave him unburied, to be destroyed by wild dogs or by some bird.
Does she not deserve golden honour? That is the dark talk that goes
quietly round.

For me, father, nothing I own is worth more than your doing well. What
greater pride is there for children than a father's flourishing good
name — or for a father than his children's? So do not carry one way
of thinking in you and one only — that what you say, and nothing else,
is right. A man who thinks he alone has sense, or a tongue or a soul
that nobody else has — when you open men like that up, they turn out
empty. Even a wise man is not shamed by learning many things, and by
not pulling too tight. You see how, beside a winter torrent, the trees
that yield keep their branches, and the ones that stand against it are
torn out root and all. And the sailor who keeps the sheet taut and
never slackens it turns his boat over, and finishes his voyage with
the benches underneath him.

Give way. Let your anger shift. If I, a younger man, can contribute
one judgement, it is this: by far the best thing is for a man to be
born full of knowledge. But since that seldom tips the right way, it
is good to learn from those who speak well.

Chorus.
My king, if he says something to the point, it is right that you
should learn from him — and you, Haemon, from your father. There has
been good speaking on both sides.

Creon.
Men of my age are to be taught sense by a man of his?

Haemon.
Nothing that is not just. If I am young, look at what I do, not at my
years.

Creon.
Is honouring the disorderly something to do?

Haemon.
I would never tell anyone to respect the bad.

Creon.
And is she not sick with exactly that disease?

Haemon.
The whole people of this city of Thebes say no.

Creon.
Is the city going to tell me how to rule?

Haemon.
Do you hear how young that sounds?

Creon.
Am I to rule this land for someone other than myself?

Haemon.
There is no city that belongs to one man.

Creon.
Is the city not held to belong to the man in power?

Haemon.
You would rule a desert beautifully. Alone.

Creon.
This one, it seems, is fighting on the woman's side.

Haemon.
If you are a woman. It is you I care about.

Creon.
You filthy thing — taking your father to court?

Haemon.
Because I see you going wrong, against justice.

Creon.
I go wrong by respecting my own authority?

Haemon.
You do not respect it. You are trampling the honours of the gods.

Creon.
Foul character — and coming second to a woman.

Haemon.
You will never catch me giving in to what is shameful.

Creon.
Every word you say is for her.

Haemon.
And for you, and for me, and for the gods below.

Creon.
You will never marry her while she is alive.

Haemon.
Then she will die, and dying she will destroy someone.

Creon.
So you have got as far as threats now? That bold?

Haemon.
What threat is there in speaking against empty judgements?

Creon.
You will pay in tears for teaching sense, with no sense in you.

Haemon.
If you were not my father, I would say you were out of your mind.

Creon.
A woman's slave. Do not try to wheedle me.

Haemon.
Do you want to talk, and talk, and hear nothing back?

Creon.
Is that so? By this Olympus above us — you can be sure of it — you
will not go on abusing me and get any joy of it. Bring out that
hateful thing. She dies now, this minute, here in front of his eyes,
at her bridegroom's side.

Haemon.
Not at my side. Never think it. She will not die beside me, and you
will never set eyes on my face again. Go mad with whichever of your
friends can stand it.

(He goes.)

Chorus.
The man is gone, my king, fast, in a rage. A mind that young is heavy
when it is hurt.

Creon.
Let him do it. Let him go and think bigger thoughts than a man should.
He will not save those two girls from their death.

Chorus.
Both of them? You mean to kill them both?

Creon.
Not the one who did not touch it. You are right.

Chorus.
And what death do you have in mind for the other?

Creon.
I will take her where the path is empty of people and hide her alive
in a hollow of rock, with just as much food set out as keeps off
pollution, so that the whole city escapes the stain. And there she can
pray to Hades — the only god she worships — and perhaps she will win
herself a way out of dying. Or else she will learn, at last, that
worshipping what is in Hades is wasted labour.

(He goes in.)

Chorus.
	Love, unbeaten in battle — Love,
	who falls on what men own,
	who keeps the night watch on a girl's soft cheek,
	you cross the sea, you go into the huts in the wild fields;
	not one of the immortals can escape you,
	nor any of us who live for a day.
	Whoever has you goes mad.

Chorus.
	You pull the minds of just men off the line
	into injustice, to their ruin;
	you have stirred up this quarrel too,
	between men of one blood.
	And the bright desire in the eyes of the bride
	made for the bed — that wins.
	It sits beside the great laws as a fellow ruler.
	The goddess Aphrodite plays with us,
	and there is no fighting her.

(Antigone is brought out of the house under guard.)

Chorus.
	And now I too am carried outside the laws
	as I look at this. I cannot hold back
	the springs of my tears any longer,
	when I see Antigone going
	to the bridal chamber where everyone sleeps.

Antigone.
	Look at me, citizens of my father's land,
	walking the last road,
	looking at the last light of the sun,
	and never again. Hades, who puts everyone to bed,
	is leading me alive to the shore of Acheron.
	No wedding song was my share; no bridal hymn
	was ever sung for me. I will be married to Acheron.

Chorus.
	But you go famous, and with praise,
	down into this hiding place of the dead,
	not struck by wasting sickness,
	not paid the wages of the sword.
	You go by your own law, alive —
	the only mortal who ever did —
	down into Hades.

Antigone.
	I have heard how the Phrygian stranger died
	the most pitiful of deaths — Niobe, the daughter of Tantalus,
	on the peak of Sipylus, whom the rock grew over
	like clinging ivy, and mastered her; and the rains,
	so people say, never leave her as she wastes away,
	nor the snow; and under her weeping brows
	she wets the ridges of the hill.
	The god is putting me to sleep like her. Exactly like her.

Chorus.
	But she was a goddess, and born of gods,
	and we are mortals, born to die.
	Still, for a woman who is dead it is a great thing to hear
	that she drew the same lot as the godlike —
	in her life, and afterwards in her death.

Antigone.
	Oh — I am being laughed at. By the gods of our fathers,
	why not wait until I am gone to mock me,
	and not do it to my face?
	City — rich men of my city —
	springs of Dirce, sacred grove of Thebes and her chariots —
	you at least I call as witnesses
	how unwept by anyone I love, and by what laws,
	I go to the heaped rock that shuts in
	my strange new tomb.
	Oh, I am wretched. I have no home among the living
	and none among the dead —
	not with those who live, not with those who have died.

Chorus.
	You went to the far edge of daring
	and struck the high seat of Justice,
	child, and fell hard.
	You are paying off some debt of your father's.

Antigone.
	You have touched the thought that hurts me most:
	my father's grief, told three times over,
	and the whole doom of us,
	the famous house of Labdacus.
	Oh, the ruin of my mother's bed,
	my poor mother sleeping with her own son,
	my father —
	and I was born of them, wretched.
	To them I go, cursed, unmarried,
	to live with them.
	Brother — your marriage was doomed;
	you died, and dying you killed me while I still lived.

Chorus.
	There is a kind of reverence in showing reverence;
	but power, to the man whose care is power,
	can never be stepped over.
	Your own self-willed temper destroyed you.

Antigone.
	Unwept, unloved, no wedding song,
	I am led, wretched, down the road that waits.
	I may not look any longer, poor girl,
	at this holy eye of light.
	No tear falls for my fate.
	Not one of my own is grieving.
