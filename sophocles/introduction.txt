Sophocles lived from about 496 to 406 BC, all of it in Athens, through the city's best years and most of the war that ended them. He wrote well over a hundred plays and won the prize more often than anyone. He also held public office, and was elected one of the ten generals. Seven of the plays survive. They are the ones everybody has heard of and hardly anybody has read: a king who hunts down a murderer and finds it is himself, a young woman who buries her brother against the law and is walled up alive for it, a soldier who goes mad in the night, slaughters a field of sheep believing they are the generals who have wronged him, and kills himself when he understands what he has done.

All seven are here, which means the four that usually get dropped are here too: Heracles poisoned by his wife with what she believes is a love charm, Electra waiting fifteen years for her brother to come home and kill their mother, Philoctetes left on an island because the army could not stand the smell of his wound, and the old blind Oedipus arriving at Athens to die, which was produced after the author's own death, in 401.

The practical facts of the theatre are worth having. The plays were written for a competition at a festival of Dionysus, performed once, outdoors, in daylight, to an audience in the thousands who had to follow every word in real time. All the parts were played by men in masks, and there were only three speaking actors, so one actor took several roles and two characters could never be on stage as the same man. Alongside them stood a chorus of fifteen, who sang and danced. Sophocles is the one credited with adding that third actor.

The chorus sang; the scenes were spoken; and the Greek metre says which is which, line by line. This edition keeps the two apart, so the spoken scenes are modern prose and the sung odes are set as verse, indented and unrhymed. The rhyme in the old translations is the translator's own addition and has gone. This is one of three volumes here, with Aeschylus and Euripides, and together they hold every Greek tragedy that survives.

Nothing is softened. Ajax's reasons for killing himself are given as the argument they are. Antigone says she would not have done this for a husband or a child. The messenger describes the blinding exactly. The gods in these plays are not just, and nobody the play agrees with ever says they are.
