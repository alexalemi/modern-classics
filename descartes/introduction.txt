René Descartes was born in France in 1596, trained by the Jesuits, took a law degree, and then spent several years as a gentleman volunteer in other people's armies before settling in the Dutch Republic, where he lived for about twenty years and did nearly all of his work. He was a working mathematician as well as a philosopher: the practice of writing curves as equations and locating points by two coordinates is his, which is why those are still called Cartesian. He died in Stockholm in 1650, having gone there to teach philosophy to Queen Christina of Sweden.

This volume holds three separate works, and they are not equally easy. The Discourse on the Method came first, in 1637, and it is the one to start with. It is short, it is autobiographical, and Descartes wrote it in French rather than Latin so that people outside the universities could read it — he tells the story of his own education, his disappointment with it, the day he shut himself in a stove-heated room and decided to throw out everything he believed and see what survived, and the four rules he adopted instead. The famous sentence, I think, therefore I am, arrives in Part Four, in the middle of that story. Part Six explains why he had been sitting on his physics: he had heard that Galileo was condemned in Rome in 1633, and withdrew the book he had ready.

The Meditations on the First Philosophy, of 1641, is the same argument done properly and it is considerably harder. Six days of first-person reasoning take the doubt much further than the Discourse does — the senses, the body, the waking world, even arithmetic, all suspended by way of an imagined deceiving demon — and then rebuild from the thinking self to God and to the physical world. Descartes wrote it in Latin, for professional theologians and philosophers, and dedicated it to the theology faculty of Paris. The dedication, his preface and his own chapter-by-chapter synopsis are all here before the six meditations themselves.

The Principles of Philosophy, of 1644, is a textbook, written in numbered paragraphs so it could be taught. Its first part covers knowledge and God, the second matter and motion, and the third and fourth the universe and the earth; the later, more technical physical parts appear here in selection. It is prefaced by a long letter Descartes wrote to his French translator, which is as readable as the Discourse and contains his image of philosophy as a tree with metaphysics for roots.

A short appendix closes the volume: the argument of the Meditations set out again in the manner of a geometry book, as definitions, axioms and propositions, which Descartes produced at the request of objectors who wanted to see it laid out that way.
