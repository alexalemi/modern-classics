Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) taught mathematics at Trinity College, Cambridge, for twenty-five years. Bertrand Russell was first his student and then his collaborator, and the two of them spent the decade before the First World War on Principia Mathematica, the attempt to derive mathematics from logic. Its first volume appeared in 1910. This book appeared in 1911 in the Home University Library, a series of cheap introductions by leading authorities for general readers, the same series in which Russell's The Problems of Philosophy came out a year later.

It is not a textbook and it teaches no techniques. Whitehead's subject is what mathematics is. He begins by admitting that "the study of mathematics is apt to commence in disappointment", because the ideas are buried under the procedure invented to handle them. He then takes the ideas one at a time: variables, the application of mathematics to the world, symbolism, the growth of the idea of number to negative, rational and imaginary numbers, coordinate geometry, conic sections, functions, periodicity, series, the differential calculus, geometry, and quantity. The mathematics used is never beyond a good school course, and each idea is followed out to where it matters in physics or astronomy. The chapter on symbolism contains the sentence most often quoted from the book: "Civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them."

Two things are worth knowing. The history in the book is part of its method, not decoration: Whitehead introduces each idea through the Greeks, or Descartes, or Newton and Leibniz, because he wants the reader to see how it was arrived at. And its word "abstract" has the sense he defines in Chapter I, that mathematical statements hold of any things whatever that satisfy the conditions. It does not mean vague.

This is a restored edition: Whitehead's words are unchanged. It is built from the typeset transcription made for Project Gutenberg, which follows the revised printing of about 1911–12 and records every change its transcriber made. Misprints stay corrected, and changes made only for consistency are reversed to what was printed. Two 1911 copies were compared with it word by word. The differences are Whitehead's own revisions, and the revised text is kept. Three slips in the transcription itself, a dropped minus sign among them, were corrected against the page images, and so was one misprint the printed book shares ("sign" for sine). The formulas are typeset, the figures are rendered from the transcription's vector drawings, and references to other pages now name the chapter or the Notes. The index is omitted. The edition also adds a description of every figure, modern typesetting, a proper ebook, and this introduction.
