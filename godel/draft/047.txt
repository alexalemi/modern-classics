The analysis consists in noting the various types of signs that occur in a calculus, indicating how to combine them into formulas, prescribing how formulas can be obtained from other formulas, and determining whether formulas of a given kind are derivable from others through explicitly stated rules of operation. Hilbert believed it might be possible to exhibit every mathematical calculus as a sort of "geometrical" pattern of formulas, in which the formulas stand to each other in a finite number of structural relations. He therefore hoped to show, by exhaustively examining these structural properties of expressions within a system, that formally contradictory formulas cannot be obtained from the axioms of given calculi. An essential requirement of Hilbert's program in its original conception was that demonstrations of consistency involve only such procedures as make no reference either to an infinite number of structural properties of formulas or to an infinite number of operations with formulas. Such procedures are called "finitistic"; and a proof of consistency conforming to this requirement is called "absolute." An "absolute" proof achieves its objectives by using a minimum of principles of inference, and does not assume the consistency of some other set of axioms.
