## II

## The Problem of Consistency

The nineteenth century witnessed a tremendous expansion and intensification of mathematical research. Many fundamental problems that had long withstood the best efforts of earlier thinkers were solved; new departments of mathematical study were created; and in various branches of the discipline new foundations were laid, or old ones entirely recast with the help of more precise techniques of analysis. To illustrate: the Greeks had proposed three problems in elementary geometry: with compass and straight-edge to trisect any angle, to construct a cube with a volume twice the volume of a given cube, and to construct a square equal in area to that of a given circle. For more than 2,000 years unsuccessful attempts were made to solve these problems; at last, in the nineteenth century it was proved that the desired constructions are logically impossible. There was, moreover, a valuable by-product of these labors.
