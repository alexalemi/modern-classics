Nineteen plays by Euripides survive, more than by Aeschylus and Sophocles together, and this volume has all of them, complete. He was the youngest of the three, born around 480 BC, and he won the prize at the Athenian festival only four times in his life, far less often than either of the others. Aristophanes made a career of mocking him, and put him on stage in a comedy the year after he died, arguing with Aeschylus over which of them was the better poet. He left Athens near the end and died in Macedonia in 406.

The number nineteen is partly an accident. The other two survive in what looks like a set of school selections; Euripides survives in that plus a run of plays from a complete edition, alphabetically consecutive in Greek, which happened to come through. So this book contains the famous ones and also plays nobody chose. Medea kills her children to punish their father and is given the last word. Pentheus is torn apart by his own mother, who walks on stage carrying his head and thinking it is a lion's. Hecuba, an old queen with nothing left, blinds the man who murdered her son. Helen turns out never to have gone to Troy at all. And there is Cyclops, the only complete satyr play left from the Greek theatre, in which Odysseus blinds a giant with the help of a chorus of drunk and cowardly goat-men. Rhesus is here too; its authorship was already disputed in antiquity.

He wrote the plainest Greek of the three, and was mocked for it in his own time as the language of the marketplace. His people talk like people: nurses, farmers, slaves, a terrified Phrygian eunuch, and kings who argue like men in a lawcourt. The formal debate is the engine of nearly every play, and both sides are good, which is the difficulty and the point. He also invented the solo aria, and there are more songs by actors here than by the chorus.

Greek tragedy has two registers, and the metre says which is which. This edition keeps them apart: the spoken scenes are modern prose, the sung parts are set as verse, indented and unrhymed. The rhyme in the old verse translations was the translator's own and has gone. This is one of three volumes here, with Aeschylus and Sophocles, and together they hold every Greek tragedy that survives.

Nothing is softened. The speeches against women stand as their speakers make them, and so do the accusations against the gods that got the author called an atheist. The plays about the fall of Troy are about women being handed out as slaves, and say so.
