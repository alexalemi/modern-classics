Alcestis (Part 1 of 3) -- Coleridge's prose, crib only

[Spoken]

Apollo.
1 Halls of Admetus, wherein I steeled my heart to be content with a servant’s board, god though I was. Zeus was to blame; he slew my son Asclepius, piercing his bosom with a thunderbolt;
5 whereat I was enraged and smote his Cyclopes, forgers of the heavenly fire; so my sire in recompense for this forced me to become a slave in a mortal’s home. Then came I to this land and kept a stranger’s flocks, and to this day have been the saviour of this house.
10 For in Pheres’ son I found a man as holy as myself, and him I saved from death by cheating Destiny, for they promised me, those goddesses of fate, that Admetus should escape the impending doom, if he found a substitute for the powers below.
15 So he went through all his list of friends, made trial of each, his father and the aged mother that bare him, but none he found save his wife alone that was willing to die for him and forego the light of life; she now within the house is upheld in his arms,
20 gasping out her life; for to-day is she doomed to die and pass from life to death. But I, for fear pollution overtake me in the house, am leaving the shelter of this roof I love so well, for already I see Death hard by,
25 the priest of souls departed, who is on his way to lead her to the halls of Hades; true to time he comes, watching this day that calls her to her doom.

[Sung]

Death.
	28 Ha! What dost thou at this house? why is it thou art ranging here,
	30 Phoebus? Once again thou wrongest me, circumscribing and limiting the honours of the nether world. Wert thou not content to hinder the death of Admetus, by thy knavish cunning baulking Destiny? but now again
	35 hast thou armed thee with thy bow and art keeping guard o’er her, this daughter of Pelias, who undertook, of her free will, to die for her lord and set him free.

Apollo.
	38 Never fear; I have, be sure, justice and fair pleas to urge.

Death.
	39 What has that bow to do, if thou hast justice on thy side?

Apollo.
	40 ’Tis my habit ever to carry it.

Death.
	41 Ay, and to help this house more than is right.

Apollo.
	42 The reason is, I cannot bear a friend’s distress.

Death.
	43 Wilt rob me of this second corpse likewise?

Apollo.
	44 Come! I did not take the other from thee by violence.

Death.
	45 Then how is it he lives above the earth and not beneath?

Apollo.
	46 He gave his wife instead, her whom now thou art come to fetch.

Death.
	47 Yea, and I will bear her hence to the nether world.

Apollo.
	48 Take her and go, for I do not suppose I can persuade thee.

Death.
	49 To slay my rightful victim? Why, that is my appointed task.

Apollo.
	50 Nay, but to lay thy deadly hand on those who soon would die.

Death.
	51 I see thy drift, thy eager plea.

Apollo.
	52 Is it then possible that Alcestis should attain old age?

Death.
	53 It is not possible; I too, methinks, find a pleasure in my rights.

Apollo.
	54 Thou canst not anyhow take more than one life.

Death.
	55 When young lives die I reap a higher honour.

Apollo.
	56 Should she die old, a sumptuous funeral will she have.

Death.
	57 Phoebus, the law thou layest down is all in favour of the rich.

Apollo.
	58 What mean’st thou? art so wise, and I never knew it?

Death.
	59 Those who have wealth would buy the chance of their dying old.

Apollo.
	60 It seems then thou wilt not grant me this favour.

Death.
	61 Not I; my customs well thou knowest.

Apollo.
	62 That I do, customs men detest and gods abhor.

Death.
	63 Thou canst not realise every lawless wish.

Apollo.
	64 Mark me, thou shalt have a check for all thy excessive fierceness;
	65 such a hero shall there come to Pheres’ halls, by Eurystheus sent to fetch a team of steeds from the wintry world of Thrace; he, a guest awhile in these halls of Admetus, will wrest this woman from thee by sheer force.
	70 So wilt thou get no thanks from me but yet wilt do this all the same, and earn my hatred too.

Death.
	72 Thou wilt not gain thy purpose any the more for all thy many words; that woman shall to Hades’ halls go down, I tell thee.
	75 Lo! I am going for her, that with the sword I may begin my rites, for he whose hair this sword doth hallow is sacred to the gods below.

First Semichorus.
	77 What means this silence in front of the palace? why is the house of Admetus stricken dumb?

Second Semichorus.
	79 Not one friend near
	80 to say if we must mourn our queen as dead, or if she liveth yet and sees the sun, Alcestis, daughter of Pelias, by me and all esteemed the best of wives
	85 to her husband.

First Semichorus.
	86 Doth any of you hear a groan, or sound of hands that smite together, or the voice of lamentation, telling all is over and done? Yet is there no servant
	90 stationed about the gate, no, not one. O come, thou saving god, to smooth the swelling waves of woe!

Second Semichorus.
	93 Surely, were she dead, they would not be so still.

First Semichorus.
	94 Maybe her corpse is not yet from the house borne forth.

Second Semichorus.
	95 Whence that inference? I am not so sanguine. What gives thee confidence?

First Semichorus.
	96 How could Admetus let his noble wife go unattended to the grave?

Second Semichorus.
	98 Before the gates I see no lustral water from the spring, as custom doth ordain should be
	100 at the gates of the dead, no shorn lock lies on the threshold, which, as thou knowest, falls in mourning for the dead, no choir of maidens smites its youthful palms together.

First Semichorus.
	105 And yet this is the appointed day.

Second Semichorus.
	106 What meanest thou by this?

First Semichorus.
	107 The day appointed for the journey to the world below.

Second Semichorus.
	108 Thou hast touched me to the heart, e’en to the soul.

Chorus.
	109 Whoso from his youth up has been accounted virtuous,
	110 needs must weep to see the good suddenly cut off.

Chorus.
	112 ’Tis done; no single spot in all the world remains whither one might steer a course, either to Lycia
	115 or to the parched abodes of Ammon to release the hapless lady’s soul; on comes death with step abrupt,
	120 nor know I to whom I should go of all who at the gods’ altars offer sacrifice.

Chorus.
	122 Only the son of Phoebus, if he yet saw this light of day—
	125 Ah! then then might she have left the dark abode and gates of Hades and have come again, for he would raise the dead to life, till that the thunderbolt’s forked flame, hurled by Zeus, smote him.
	130 But now what further hope of life can I welcome to me?

Chorus.
	132 Our lords have ere this done all they could; on every altar streams the blood of abundant sacrifice;
	135 yet our sorrows find no cure.

[Spoken]

Chorus.
136 Lo! from the house cometh a handmaid weeping; what shall I be told hath chanced? Grief may well be pardoned, if aught happeneth to one’s master; yet I fain would learn whether our lady
140 still is living or haply is no more.

Maid.
141 Alive, yet dead thou may’st call her.

Chorus.
142 Why, how can the same person be alive, yet dead?

Maid.
143 She is sinking even now, and at her last gasp.

Chorus.
144 My poor master! how sad thy lot to lose so good a wife!

Maid.
145 He did not know his loss, until the blow fell on him.

Chorus.
146 Is there then no more a hope of saving her?

Maid.
147 None; the fated day comes on so fast.

Chorus.
148 Are then the fitting rites already taking place o’er her body?

Maid.
149 Death’s garniture is ready, wherewith her lord will bury her.

Chorus.
150 Well let her know, though die she must, her fame ranks far above any other wife’s beneath the sun.

Maid.
152 Far above! of course it does; who will gainsay it? What must the woman be who hath surpassed her? For how could any wife have shown a clearer regard
155 for her lord than by offering in his stead to die? Thus much the whole city knows right well; but thou shalt hear with wonder what she did within the house. For when she knew the fatal day was come, she washed her fair white skin with water from the stream,
160 then from her cedar chests drew forth vesture and ornaments and robed herself becomingly; next, standing before the altar-hearth, she prayed, Mistress mine, behold! I pass beneath the earth; to thee in suppliant wise will I my latest prayer address;
165 be mother to my orphans, and to my boy unite a loving bride, to my daughter a noble husband. Let them not die, as I, their mother, perish now, untimely in their youth, but let them live their glad lives out, happy in their native land.
170 To every altar in Admetus’ halls she went and crowned them and prayed, plucking from myrtle boughs their foliage, with never a tear or groan, nor did her coming trouble change the colour of her comely face.
175 Anon into her bridal bower she burst, and then her tears brake forth and thus she cried, O couch, whereon I loosed my maiden state for the man in whose cause I die, farewell! no hate I feel for thee; for me alone hast thou undone,
180 dying as I die from fear of betraying thee and my lord. Some other wife will make thee hers, more blest maybe than me, but not more chaste. And she fell upon her knees and kissed it, till with her gushing tears the whole bed was wet.
185 At last, when she had had her fill of weeping, she tore herself from the bed and hurried headlong forth, and oft as she was leaving the chamber turned she back and cast herself once more upon the couch; while her children were weeping as they clung to their mother’s robes;
190 but she took them each in turn in her arms and kissed them fondly, as a dying mother might. And all the servants in the house fell a-crying in sorrow for their mistress; but she held out her hand to each, nor was there one so mean
195 but she gave him a word and took his answer back. Such are the sorrows in the halls of Admetus. Dying he had died once for all, but by avoiding death he hath a legacy of grief that he will ne’er forget.

Chorus.
199 Doubtless Admetus sorrows in this calamity,
200 if he must lose so good a wife.

Maid.
201 Ah yes! he weeps, holding in his arms his darling wife, and prays her not to leave him, impossible request! for she is worn and wasted with illness, and lies exhausted, a sad burden in his arms.
205 Still, though her breath comes short and scant, she yearns to gaze yet on the sunshine, for nevermore, but now the last and latest time her eye shall see his radiant orb. But I will go, thy presence to announce,
210 for ’tis not all who have the goodwill to stand by their masters with kindly hearts in adversity. But thou of old hast been my master’s friend.

[Sung]

Chorus.
	213 O Zeus, what way out of these sorrows can be found? how can we loose the bonds of fate that bind our lord?
	215 Comes some one forth? Am I at once to cut my hair, and cast the sable robe about me? Too plainly, ay too plainly, friends; still let us to heaven pray; for the gods’ power is very great.
	220 O king Paean, devise for Admetus some means of escape from his sorrows. Yes, yes, contrive it; for thou in days gone by didst find salvation for him, so now be thou a saviour from the toils of death
	225 and stay bloodthirsty Hades.

Chorus.
	226 Woe! woe! alas! Thou son of Pheres, woe! Ah, thy fate in losing thy wife! Is not this enough to make thee slay thyself, ah! more than cause enough to tie the noose aloft and fit it to the neck?
	230 Yea, for to-day wilt thou witness the death of her that was not merely dear, but dearest of the dear. Look, look! she cometh even now, her husband with her, from the house. Cry aloud and wail, O land of Pherae,
	235 wail for the best of women, as with sickness worn she passes ’neath the earth to Hades, lord below.

Chorus.
	238 Never, never will I say that marriage brings more joy than grief, as I
	240 conjecture by the past and witness these misfortunes of our king, for he when widowed of this noble wife will for the future lead a life that is no life at all.

Alcestis.
	244 O sun-god, lamp of day!
	245 O scudding clouds that dance along the sky!

Admetus.
	246 He sees us both with anguish bowed, albeit guiltless of any crime against the gods, for the which thy death is due.

Alcestis.
	248 O earth, O sheltering roof, and ye my maiden chambers in my native land Iolcos!

Admetus.
	250 Lift thyself, unhappy wife, forsake me not; entreat the mighty gods to pity us.

Alcestis.
	252 I see the two-oared skiff, I see it; and Charon, death’s ferryman, his hand upon the boatman’s pole,
	255 is calling me e’en now, Why lingerest thou? Hasten. Thou art keeping me. Thus in his eager haste he hurries me.

Admetus.
	257 Ah me! bitter to me is this voyage thou speakest of. Unhappy wife, what woes are ours!

Alcestis.
	259 One draws me, draws me hence, seest thou not?
	260 to the courts of death, winged Hades glaring from beneath his dark brows. What wilt thou with me? Unhand me. On what a journey am I setting out, most wretched woman I!

Admetus.
	264 Bitter journey to thy friends, yet most of all to me
	265 and to thy babes, the partners in this sorrow.

Alcestis.
	266 Hands off! hands off at once! Lay me down, I cannot stand. Hades standeth near; and with its gloom steals night upon my eyes.
	270 O my children, my children, ye have no mother now. Fare ye well, my babes, live on beneath the light!

Admetus.
	273 Woe is me! this is a message of sorrow to me, worse than aught that death can do.
	275 Steel not thy heart to leave me, I implore, by heaven, by thy babes whom thou wilt make orphans; nay, raise thyself, have courage. For if thou die I can no longer live; my life, my death are in thy hands; thy love is what I worship.

[Spoken]

Alcestis.
280 Admetus, lo! thou seest how it is with me; to thee I fain would tell my wishes ere I die. Thee I set before myself, and instead of living have ensured thy life, and so I die, though I need not have died for thee,
285 but might have taken for my husband whom I would of the Thessalians, and have had a home blest with royal power; reft of thee, with my children orphans, I cared not to live, nor, though crowned with youth’s fair gifts, wherein I used to joy, did I grudge them.
290 Yet the father that begat thee, the mother that bare thee, gave thee up, though they had reached a time of life when to die were well, so saving thee their child, and winning noble death. For thou wert their only son, nor had they any hope, when thou wert dead, of other offspring.
295 And I should have lived and thou the remnant of our days, nor wouldst thou have wept thy wife’s loss, nor have had an orphan family. But some god hath caused these things to be even as they are. Enough! Remember thou the gratitude due to me for this;
300 yea, for I shall never ask thee for an adequate return, for naught is prized more highly than our life; but just is my request, as thou thyself must say, since thou no less than I dost love these children, if so be thou think’st aright. Be content to let them rule my house,
305 and do not marry a new wife to be a stepmother to these children, for she from jealousy, if so she be a woman worse than me, will stretch out her hand against the children of our union. Then do not this, I do beseech thee. For the stepmother that succeeds, hateth children
310 of a former match, cruel as the viper’s are her tender mercies. A son, ’tis true, hath in his sire a tower of strength, to whom he speaks and has his answer back; but thou, my daughter, how shall thy maidenhood be passed in honour? What shall thy experience be of thy father’s wife?
315 She may fasten on thee some foul report in thy youthful bloom, and frustrate thy marriage. Never shall thy mother lead thee to the bridal bed, nor by her presence in thy travail hearten thee, my child, when a mother’s kindness triumphs over all.
320 No, for I must die; and lo! this evil cometh to me not to-morrow nor yet on the third day of the month, but in a moment shall I be counted among the souls that are no more. Fare ye well, be happy; and thou, husband, canst boast thou hadst a peerless wife,
325 and you, children, that you had such an one for mother.

Chorus.
326 Take heart; I do not hesitate to answer for him; he will perform all this, unless his mind should go astray.

Admetus.
328 It shall be so, fear not, it shall; alive thou wert the only wife I had, and dead shalt thou,
330 none else, be called mine; no Thessalian maid shall ever take thy place and call me lord; not though she spring from lineage high nor though besides she be the fairest of her sex. Of children I have enough; god grant I may in them
335 be blessed! for in thee has it been otherwise. No year-long mourning will I keep for thee, but all my life through, lady: loathing the mother that bare me, and hating my father, for they were friends in word but not in deed.
340 But thou didst give thy dearest for my life and save it. May I not then mourn to lose a wife like thee? And I will put an end to revelry, to social gatherings o’er the wine, forego the festal crown and music which once reigned in my halls.
345 For nevermore will I touch the lyre nor lift my soul in song to the Libyan flute, for thou hast taken with thee all my joy in life. But in my bed thy figure shall be laid full length, by cunning artists fashioned;
350 thereon will I throw myself and, folding my arms about thee, call upon thy name, and think I hold my dear wife in my embrace, although I do not; chill comfort this, no doubt, but still I shall relieve my soul of its sad weight; and thou wilt come
355 to me in dreams and gladden me. For sweet it is to see our friends, come they when they will, e’en by night. Had I the tongue, the tuneful voice of Orpheus to charm Demeter’s daughter or her husband by my lay and bring thee back from Hades,
360 I had gone down, nor Pluto’s hound, nor Charon, ferryman of souls, whose hand is on the oar, had held me back, till to the light I had restored thee alive. At least do thou await me there, against the hour I die, prepare a home for me to be my true wife still.
365 For in this same cedar coffin I will bid these children lay me with thee and stretch my limbs by thine; for never even in death may I be severed from thee, alone found faithful of them all.

Chorus.
369 Lo! I too will share with thee thy mourning for her, friend with friend;
370 for this is but her due.

Alcestis.
371 My children, ye with your own ears have heard your father’s promise, that he will never wed another wife to set her over you, nor e’er dishonour me.

Admetus.
374 Yea, so I promise now, and accomplish it I will.

Alcestis.
375 On these conditions receive the children from my hand.

Admetus.
376 I receive them, dear pledges by a dear hapd given.

Alcestis.
377 Take thou my place and be a mother to these babes.

Admetus.
378 Sore will be their need when they are reft of thee.

Alcestis.
379 O my children, I am passing to that world below, when my life was needed most.

Admetus.
380 Ah me, what can I do bereft of thee?

Alcestis.
381 Thy sorrow Time will soothe; ’tis the dead who are as naught.

Admetus.
382 Take me, O take me, I beseech, with thee ’neath the earth.

Alcestis.
383 Enough that I in thy stead am dying.

Admetus.
384 O Destiny! of what a wife art thou despoiling me!

Alcestis.
385 Lo! the darkness deepens on my drooping eyes.

Admetus.
386 Lost indeed am I, if thou, dear wife, wilt really leave me.

Alcestis.
387 Thou mayst speak of me as naught, as one whose life is o’er.

Admetus.
388 Lift up thy face, leave not thy children.

Alcestis.
389 ’Tis not my own free will; O my babes, farewell!
