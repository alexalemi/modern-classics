Chorus.
392 She is gone, the wife of Admetus is no more.

[Sung]

Eumelus.
	393 O my hard fate! My mother has passed to the realms below; she lives no more,
	395 dear father, ’neath the sun. Alas for her! she leaves us ere her time and to me bequeaths an orphan’s life. Behold that staring eye, those nerveless hands!
	400 Hear me, mother, hear me, I implore! ’tis I who call thee now, I thy tender chick, printing my kisses on thy lips.

Admetus.
	404 She cannot hear, she cannot see;
	405 a heavy blow hath fortune dealt us, you children and me.

Eumelus.
	406 O father, I am but a child to have my loving mother leave me here alone; O cruel my fate, alas!
	410 and thine, my sister, sharer in my cup of woe. Woe to thee, father! in vain, in vain didst thou take a wife and hast not reached the goal of eld with her; for she is gone before, and now that thou art dead,
	415 my mother, our house is all undone.

Chorus.
	416 Admetus, these misfortunes thou must bear. Thou art by no means the first nor yet shalt be the last of men to lose a wife of worth; know this, we all of us are debtors unto death.

Admetus.
	420 I understand; this is no sudden flight of ill hither; I was ware of it and long have pined. But since I am to. carry the dead forth to her burial, stay here with me and to that inexorable god in Hades raise your antiphone. While raise your antiphone.
	425 While to all Thessalians in my realm I do proclaim a general mourning for this lady, with hair shorn off and robes of sable hue; all ye who harness steeds for cars, or single horses ride, cut off their manes with the sharp steel.
	430 Hush’d be every pipe, silent every lyre throughout the city till twelve full moons are past; for never again shall I bury one whom I love more, no! nor one more loyal to me; honour from me is her due, for she for me hath died, she and she alone. [Exeunt ADMETUS and EUMELUS, with the other children.

Chorus.
	435 Daughter of Pelias, be thine a happy life in that sunless home in Hades’ halls! Let Hades know, that swarthy god,
	440 and that old man who sits to row and steer alike at his death-ferry, that he hath carried o’er the lake of Acheron in his two-oared skiff a woman peerless amidst her sex.

Chorus.
	445 Oft of thee the Muses’ votaries shall sing on the seven-stringed mountain shell and in hymns that need no harp, glorifying thee, oft as the season in his cycle cometh round at Sparta in that Carnean month
	450 when all night long the moon sails high o’erhead, yea, and in splendid Athens, happy town. So glorious a theme has thy death bequeathed to tuneful bards.

Chorus.
	455 Would it were in my power and range to bring thee to the light from the chambers of Hades and the streams of Cocytus with the oar that sweeps yon nether flood!
	460 For thou, and thou alone, most dear of women, hadst the courage to redeem thy husband from Hades in exchange for thy own life. Light lie the earth above thee, lady! And if ever thy lord take to him a new wife, I vow he will earn my hatred
	465 and thy children’s too.

Chorus.
	466 His mother had no heart to plunge into the darkness of the tomb for her son, no! nor his aged sire. Their own child they had not the courage to rescue,
	470 the wretches! albeit they were grey-headed. But thou in thy youth and beauty hast died for thy lord and gone thy way. O be it mine to have for partner such a loving wife! for this lot is rare in life. Surely she should be my help-meet all my life
	475 and never cause one tear.

[Spoken]

Heracles.
476 Mine hosts, dwellers on this Phersean soil! say, shall I find Admetus in the house?

Chorus.
478 The son of Pheres is within, Heracles. Tell me what need is bringing thee to the Thessalian land,
480 to visit this city of the Pheraeans?

Heracles.
481 I am performing a labour for Tirynthian Eurystheus.

Chorus.
482 And whither art thou journeying? on what wandering art thou forced to go?

Heracles.
483 To fetch the chariot-steeds of Thracian Diomedes.

Chorus.
484 How canst thou? art a stranger to the ways of thy host?

Heracles.
485 I am; for never yet have I gone to the land of the Bistones.

Chorus.
486 Thou canst not master his horses without fighting.

Heracles.
487 Still I cannot refuse these labours.

Chorus.
488 Then shalt thou slay them and return, or thyself be slain and stay there.

Heracles.
489 It will not be the first hard course that I have run.

Chorus.
490 And what will be thy gain, suppose thou master their lord?

Heracles.
491 The steeds will I drive away to the Tirynthian king.

Chorus.
492 No easy task to bit their jaws.

Heracles.
493 Easy enough, unless their nostrils vomit fire.

Chorus.
494 With ravening jaws they rend the limbs of men.

Heracles.
495 Thou speakest of the food of mountain beasts, not of horses.

Chorus.
496 Their mangers blood-bedabbled thou shalt see.

Heracles.
497 Whose son doth he who feeds them boast to be?

Chorus.
498 Ares’ son, king of the golden targe of Thrace.

Heracles.
499 This toil again is but a piece of my ill-luck;
500 hard it ever is and still is growing steeper, if I with Ares’ own-begotten sons must fight, first with Lycaon, next with Cycnus, while now I am bound on this third contest to engage the horses and their master.
505 Yet shall no man ever see Alcmena’s son trembling at his foemen’s prowess.

Chorus.
507 See where Admetus, lord of this land, comes in person from the palace forth.

Admetus.
509 Hail! son of Zeus, from Perseus sprung.

Heracles.
510 Joy to thee also, Admetus, king of Thessaly.

Admetus.
511 Would there were! yet thy kindly heart I know full well.

Heracles.
512 Why dost thou appear with head shorn thus in mourning?

Admetus.
513 To-day I am to bury one who is dead.

Heracles.
514 Heaven avert calamity from thy children!

Admetus.
515 The children I have begotten are alive within my house.

Heracles.
516 Thy father maybe is gone; well, he was ripe to go.

Admetus.
517 No, Heracles, he lives; my mother too.

Heracles.
518 It cannot be thy wife is dead, thy Alcestis?

Admetus.
519 I can a twofold tale tell about her.

Heracles.
520 Dost mean that she is dead, or living still?

Admetus.
521 She lives, yet lives no more; that is my grief.

Heracles.
522 I am no wiser yet; thy words are riddles to me.

Admetus.
523 Knowest thou not the doom she must undergo?

Heracles.
524 I know she did submit to die in thy stead.

Admetus.
525 How then is she still alive, if so she promised?

Heracles.
526 Ah! weep not thy wife before the day, put that off till then.

Admetus.
527 The doomed is dead; the dead no more exists.

Heracles.
528 Men count to be and not to be something apart.

Admetus.
529 Thy verdict this, O Heracles, mine another.

Heracles.
530 Why weepest then? which of thy dear ones is the dead?

Admetus.
531 ’Tis a woman; I spoke of a woman just now.

Heracles.
532 A stranger, or one of thine own kin?

Admetus.
533 A stranger, yet in another sense related to my house.

Heracles.
534 How then came she by her death in house of thine?

Admetus.
535 Her father dead, she lived here as an orphan.

Heracles.
536 Ah! would I had found thee free from grief, Admetus!

Admetus.
537 With what intent dost thou devise this speech?

Heracles.
538 I will seek some other friendly hearth.

Admetus.
539 Never, O prince! Heaven forefend such dire disgrace!

Heracles.
540 A guest is a burden to sorrowing friends, if come he should.

Admetus.
541 The dead are dead. Come in.

Heracles.
542 To feast in a friend’s house of sorrow is shameful.

Admetus.
543 The guest chambers lie apart, whereto we will conduct thee.

Heracles.
544 Let me go; ten thousandfold shall be my thanks to thee.

Admetus.
545 Thou must not go to any other hearth. (To a Servant.) Go before, open the guest-rooms that face not these chambers, and bid my stewards see there is plenty of food; then shut the doors that lead into the courtyard; for ’tis not seemly that guests when at their meat
550 should hear the voice of weeping or be made sad. [Exit HERACLES.

Chorus.
551 What doest thou? With such calamity before thee, hast thou the heart, Admetus, to welcome visitors? What means this folly?

Admetus.
553 Well, and if I had driven him from my house and city when he came to be my guest, wouldst thou have praised me more?
555 No indeed! for my calamity would have been no whit less, while I should have been more churlish. And this would have been another woe to add to mine, that my house should be called no friend to guests. Yea, and I find him myself the best of hosts
560 whene’er to Argos’ thirsty land I come.

Chorus.
561 Why then didst thou conceal thy present misfortune, if, as thy own lips declare, it was a friend that came?

Admetus.
563 He would never have entered my house, had he known aught of my distress.
565 Maybe there are who think me but a fool for acting thus, and these will blame me; but my halls have never learnt to drive away or treat with scorn my guests.

[Sung]

Chorus.
	569 O home of hospitality, thrown open by thy lord to all now and ever!
	570 In thee it was that Pythian Apollo, the sweet harper, deigned to make his home, and in thy halls was content to lead a shepherd’s life,
	575 piping o’er the sloping downs shepherd’s madrigals to thy flocks.

Chorus.
	578 And spotted lynxes couched amid his sheep in joy to hear his melody,
	580 and the lions’ tawny troop left the glen of Othrys and came; came too the dappled fawn on nimble foot from beyond the crested pines and frisked about thy lyre, O Phoebus,
	585 for very joy at thy gladsome minstrelsy.

Chorus.
	588 And so it is thy lord inhabits a home rich in countless flocks
	590 by Boebe’s lovely mere, bounding his tilled corn-land and his level pastures with the clime of the Molossi near the sun’s dark stable,
	595 and holding sway as far as the harbourless strand of the Aegean ’neath Pelion’s shadow.

Chorus.
	597 Now too hath he opened wide his house and welcomed a guest although his eye is wet with tears in mourning for his wife so dear
	600 but lately dead within his halls; yea, for noble birth to noble feeling is inclined. And in the good completest wisdom dwells; and at my heart sits the bold belief
	605 that heaven’s servant will be blesedblessed.

[Spoken]

Admetus.
606 Men of Pherae, kindly gathered here, lo! even now my servants are bearing the corpse with all its trappings shoulder-high to the funeral pyre for burial; do ye, as custom bids,
610 salute the dead on her last journey starting.

Chorus.
611 Look! I see thy father advancing with aged step, and servants too bearing in their arms adornment for thy wife, offerings for the dead.

Pheres.
614 My son, I come to share thy sorrow,
615 for thou hast lost a noble, peerless wife; that no man will deny. Yet must thou needs bear this blow, hard though it be. Accept this garniture, and let it go beneath the earth, for rightly is her body honoured,
620 since she died to save thy life, my son, and gave me back my child, suffering me not to lose thee and pine away in an old age of sorrow. Thus by the generous deed she dared, hath she made her life a noble example for all her sex.
625 Farewell to thee, who hast saved this son of mine and raised me up when falling; be thine a happy lot even in Hades’ halls! Such marriages I declare are gain to man, else to wed is not worth while.

Admetus.
629 Thou hast come uncalled by me to this burial,
630 nor do I count thy presence as a friendly act. Never shall she be clad in any garniture of thine, nor in her burial will she need aught of thine. Thou shouldst have shewn thy sympathy at the time my doom was sealed. But thou didst stand aloof and let another die,
635 though thou wert old, the victim young; shalt thou then mourn the dead? Methinks thou wert no real sire of mine nor was she my true mother who calls herself and is called so, but I was sprung of slave’s blood and privily substituted at thy wife’s breast.
640 Brought to the test thou hast shewn thy nature; I cannot think I am thy child by birth. By heaven, thou art the very pattern of cowards, who at thy age, on the borderland of life, wouldst not, nay! couldst not find the heart to die
645 for thy own son; but ye, my parents, left to this stranger, whom I henceforth shall justly hold e’en as mother and as father too, and none but her. And yet ’twas a noble exploit to achieve, to die to save thy son, and in any case the
650 remnant of thy time to live was but short; and I and she would have lived the days that were to be, nor had I lost my wife and mourned my evil fate. Moreover thou hast had all treatment that a happy man should have; in princely pomp thy youth was spent,
655 thou hadst a son, myself, to be the heir of this thy home, so thou hadst no fear of dying childless and leaving thy house desolate, for strangers to pillage. Nor yet canst thou say I did dishonour thy old age and give thee up to die, seeing I have ever been
660 to thee most dutiful, and for this thou, my sire, and she, my mother, have made me this return. Go then, get other sons to tend thy closing years, prepare thy body for the grave, and lay out thy corpse.
665 For I will never bury thee with hand of mine; for I am dead for all thou didst for me; but if I found a saviour in another and still live, his son I say I am, and his fond nurse in old age will be. ’Tis vain, I see, the old man’s prayer for death,
670 his plaints at age and life’s long weariness. For if death do but draw near, not one doth wish to die; old age no more they count so burdensome.

Chorus.
674 Peace! enough the present sorrow, O my son; goad not thy father’s soul to fury.

Pheres.
675 Child, whom think’st thou art reviling? some Lydian or Phrygian bought with thy money? Art not aware I am a freebom Thessalian, son of a Thessalian sire? Thou art too insolent; yet from hence thou shalt not go as thou earnest,
680 after shooting out thy braggart tongue at me. To rule my house I begat and bred thee up; I own no debt of dying in thy stead; this is not the law that I received from my ancestors that fathers should die for children, nor is it a custom in Hellas.
685 For weal or woe, thy life must be thine own; whate’er was due from me to thee, thou hast. Dominion wide is thine, and acres broad I will leave to thee, for from my father did I inherit them. How, pray, have I wronged thee? of what am I robbing thee?
690 Die not thou for me, nor I for thee. Thy joy is in the light; think’st thou thy sire’s is not? By Heaven! ’tis a weary while, I trow, that time beneath the earth, and life, though short, is sweet. Thou at least didst struggle hard to ’scape thy death,
695 lost to shame, and by her death dost live beyond thy destined term. Dost thou then speak of cowardice in me, thou craven heart! no match for thy wife, who hath died for thee, her fine young lord? A clever scheme hast thou devised to stave off death for ever,
700 if thou canst persuade each new wife to die instead of thee; and dost thou then taunt thy friends, who will not do the like, coward as thou art thyself? Hold thy peace; reflect, if thou dost love thy life so well, this love by all is shared; yet if thou wilt speak ill of me,
705 thyself shalt hear a full and truthful list of thy own crimes.

Chorus.
706 Too long that list both now and heretofore; cease, father, to revile thy son.

Admetus.
708 Say on, for I have said my say; but if it vexes thee to hear the truth, thou shouldst not have sinned against me.

Pheres.
710 My sin had been the deeper, had I died for thee.

Admetus.
711 What! is it all one for young or old to die?

Pheres.
712 To live one life, not twain, is all our due.

Admetus.
713 Outlive then Zeus himself!

Pheres.
714 Dost curse thy parents, though unharmed by them?

Admetus.
715 Yea, for I see thy heart is set on length of days.

Pheres.
716 Is it not to save thyself thou art carrying to the tomb this corpse?

Admetus.
717 A proof of thy cowardice, thou craven heart!

Pheres.
718 At any rate her death was not due to me; this thou canst not say.

Admetus.
719 Ah! mayst thou some day come to need my aid!

Pheres.
720 Woo many wives, that there may be the more to die.

Admetus.
721 That is thy reproach, for thou didst refuse to die.

Pheres.
722 Dear is the light of the sun-god, dear to all.

Admetus.
723 A coward soul is thine, not to be reckoned among men.

Pheres.
724 No laughing now for thee at bearing forth my aged corpse.

Admetus.
725 Thy death will surely be a death of shame, come when it will.

Pheres.
726 Once dead I little reck of foul report.

Admetus.
727 Alas! how void of shame the old can be!

Pheres.
728 Hers was no want of shame; ’twas want of sense in her that thou didst find.

Admetus.
729 Begone! and leave me to bury my dead.

Pheres.
730 I go; bury thy victim, thyself her murderer. Her kinsmen yet will call for an account. Else surely has Acastus ceased to be a man, if he avenge not on thee his sister’s blood.

Admetus.
734 Perdition seize thee and that wife of thine!
735 grow old, as ye deserve, childless, though your son yet lives, for ye shall never enter the same abode with me; nay! were it needful I should disown thy paternal hearth by heralds’ voice, I had disowned it. (Exit PHERES). Now, since we must bear our present woe,
740 let us go and lay the dead upon the pyre. [Exit ADMETUS.

[Sung]

Chorus.
	741 Woe, woe for thee! Alas, for thy hardihood! Noble spirit, good beyond compare, farewell! May Hermes in the nether world, and Hades, too, give thee a kindly welcome! and if even in that other life
	745 the good are rewarded, mayst thou have thy share therein and take thy seat by Hades’ bride!

Attendant.
	747 Many the guests ere now from every comer of the world I have seen come to the halls of Admetus, for whom I have spread the board, but never yet
	750 have I welcomed to this hearth a guest so shameless as this; a man who, in the first place, though he saw my master’s grief, yet entered and presumed to pass the gates, then took what cheer we had in no sober spirit, though he knew our sorrow;
	755 no! was there aught we failed to bring? he called for it. Next in his hands he took a goblet of ivy-wood and drank the pure juice of the black grape, till the mounting fumes of wine heated him, and he crowned his head with myrtle-sprays,
	760 howling discordantly, while two-fold strains were there to hear, for he would sing without a thought for the troubles in Admetus’ halls, while we servants mourned our mistress, though we did not let the stranger see our streaming eyes, for such was the bidding of Admetus.
	765 So now here am I entertaining as a guest some miscreant thief maybe, or robber, while she is gone forth from the house, nor did I follow her nor stretch my hand towards her bier, in mourning for my lady, who, to me, and all her servants,
	770 was a mother, for she would save us from countless trouble, appeasing her husband’s angry mood. Have I not good cause then to loathe this guest who cometh in our hour of woe?
