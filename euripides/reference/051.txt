Iphigenia.
	631 Do not be angry with me, mother, if I run from your side and throw myself on my father’s breast.

Clytemnestra.
	633 Hail! my honored lord, king Agamemnon! we have obeyed your commands and have come.

Iphigenia.
	635 O my father! I long to outrun others and embrace you after this long while; for I yearn to see your face; do not be angry with me.

Clytemnestra.
	638 You may do so, daughter; for of all the children I have borne, you have always loved your father best.

Iphigenia.
	640 I see you, father, joyfully after a long time.

Agamemnon.
	641 And I, your father, see you; your words do equal duty for both of us.

Iphigenia.
	642 All hail, father! you did well in bringing me here to you.

Agamemnon.
	643 I know not how I am to say yes or no to that, my child.

Iphigenia.
	644 Ah! how wildly you are looking, spite of your joy at seeing me.

Agamemnon.
	645 A man has many cares when he is king and general too.

Iphigenia.
	646 Be mine, all mine today; do not turn to moody thoughts.

Agamemnon.
	647 Why so I am, all yours today; I have no other thought.

Iphigenia.
	648 Then smooth your knitted brow, unbend and smile.

Agamemnon.
	649 See! my child, my joy at seeing you is even as it is.

Iphigenia.
	650 And do you then have tears streaming from your eyes?

Agamemnon.
	651 Yes, for long is the absence from each other, that awaits us.

Iphigenia.
	652 I do not know, dear father, I do not know of what you are speaking.

Agamemnon.
	653 You are moving my pity all the more by speaking so sensibly.

Iphigenia.
	654 My words shall turn to senselessness if that will cheer you more.

Agamemnon.
	655 Alas! this silence is too much. You have my thanks.

Iphigenia.
	656 Stay with your children at home, father.

Agamemnon.
	657 My own wish! But to my sorrow I may not

Iphigenia.
	658 Ruin seize their wars and the woes of Menelaus!

Agamemnon.
	659 First will that, which has been my life-long ruin, bring ruin to others.

Iphigenia.
	660 How long you were absent in the bays of Aulis!

Agamemnon.
	661 Yes, and there is still a hindrance to my sending the army forward.

Iphigenia.
	662 Where do men say the Phrygians live, father?

Agamemnon.
	663 In a land where I wish Paris, the son of Priam, never had dwelt.

Iphigenia.
	664 It is a long voyage you are bound on, father, after you leave me.

Agamemnon.
	665 You will meet your father again, my daughter.

Iphigenia.
	666 Ah! would it were seemly for you to take me as a fellow voyager!

Agamemnon.
	667 You too have a voyage to make to a haven where you will remember your father.

Iphigenia.
	668 Shall I sail there with my mother or alone?

Agamemnon.
	669 All alone, without father or mother.

Iphigenia.
	670 What! have you found me a new home, father?

Agamemnon.
	671 Enough of this! it is not for girls to know such things.

Iphigenia.
	672 Please hurry home from Troy, father, as soon as you have triumphed there.

Agamemnon.
	673 There is a sacrifice I have first to offer here.

Iphigenia.
	674 Yes, it is your duty to heed religion with aid of holy rites.

Agamemnon.
	675 You will witness it, for you will be standing near the libations.

Iphigenia.
	676 Am I to lead the dance then round the altar, father?

Agamemnon.
	677 I count you happier than myself because you know nothing. Go within—it is wrong for maidens to be seen—after you have given me your hand and a kiss,
	680 on the eve of your lengthy sojourn far from your father’s side.
	681 Breast, cheek, and golden hair! ah, how grievous you have found Helen and the Phrygians’ city! I can speak no more; the tears come welling to my eyes, the moment I touch you.
	685 Go into the house. Exit Iphigenia.
	685b Agamemnon turns to Clytemnestra. I beg your pardon, daughter of Leda, if I showed excessive grief at the thought of giving my daughter to Achilles; for though we are sending her to taste of bliss, still it wrings a parent’s heart, when he, the father who has toiled so hard for them,
	690 commits his children to the homes of strangers.

Clytemnestra.
	691 I am not so senseless; But I think I will go through this as well, when I lead the girl from the chamber to the sound of the marriage hymn; so I do not chide you; but custom will combine with time to make the smart grow less.
	695 As for him, to whom you have betrothed our daughter, I know his name, it is true, but want to learn his lineage and the land of his birth.

Agamemnon.
	697 There was one Aegina, the daughter of Asopus.

Clytemnestra.
	698 Who wedded her? Some mortal or a god?

Agamemnon.
	699 Zeus, and she bore Aeacus, the prince of Oenone.

Clytemnestra.
	700 What son of Aeacus secured his father’s halls?

Agamemnon.
	701 Peleus, who wedded the daughter of Nereus.

Clytemnestra.
	702 With the god’s consent, or when he had taken her in spite of gods?

Agamemnon.
	703 Zeus betrothed her, and her guardian gave consent.

Clytemnestra.
	704 Where did he marry her? in the billows of the sea?

Agamemnon.
	705 In Chiron’s home, at sacred Pelion’s foot.

Clytemnestra.
	706 What! the abode ascribed to the race of Centaurs?

Agamemnon.
	707 It was there the gods celebrated the marriage feast of Peleus.

Clytemnestra.
	708 Did Thetis or his father train Achilles?

Agamemnon.
	709 Chiron brought him up, to prevent his learning the ways of the wicked.

Clytemnestra.
	710 Ah! wise the teacher, still wiser the one who gave his son.

Agamemnon.
	711 Such is the future husband of your daughter.

Clytemnestra.
	712 A blameless lord; but what city in Hellas is his?

Agamemnon.
	713 He dwells on the banks of the river Apidanus, in the borders of Phthia.

Clytemnestra.
	714 Will you convey our daughter there?

Agamemnon.
	715 He who takes her to himself will see to that.

Clytemnestra.
	716 Happiness attend the pair! Which day will he marry her?

Agamemnon.
	717 As soon as the full moon comes to give its blessing

Clytemnestra.
	718 Have you already offered the goddess a sacrifice to usher in the maiden’s marriage?

Agamemnon.
	719 I am about to do so; that is the very thing I was engaged in.

Clytemnestra.
	720 And then will you celebrate the marriage feast afterwards?

Agamemnon.
	721 Yes, when I have offered a sacrifice required by the gods of me.

Clytemnestra.
	722 But where am I to make ready the feast for the women?

Agamemnon.
	723 Here beside our gallant Argive ships.

Clytemnestra.
	724 Finely here! but still I must; good come of it for all that!

Agamemnon.
	725 Do you know what to do, lady? Then obey me.

Clytemnestra.
	726 In what matter? for I was ever accustomed to obey you.

Agamemnon.
	727 Here, where the bridegroom is, I will—

Clytemnestra.
	728 Which of my duties will you perform in the mother’s absence?

Agamemnon.
	729 Give your child away with help of Danaids.

Clytemnestra.
	730 And where am I to be then?

Agamemnon.
	731 Go to Argos, and take care of your unwedded daughters.

Clytemnestra.
	732 And leave my child? Then who will raise her bridal torch?

Agamemnon.
	733 I will provide the proper wedding torch.

Clytemnestra.
	734 That is not the custom; but you think lightly of these things.

Agamemnon.
	735 It is not good for you to be alone among a soldier-crowd.

Clytemnestra.
	736 It is good that a mother should give her own child away.

Agamemnon.
	737 Yes, and that those maidens at home should not be left alone.

Clytemnestra.
	738 They are well guarded in their maiden bowers.

Agamemnon.
	739 Obey.

Clytemnestra.
	730b No, by the goddess-queen of Argos!
	740 Go, manage matters out of doors; but in the house it is my place to decide what is proper for maidens at their wedding.

Agamemnon.
	742 Woe is me! my efforts are baffled; I am disappointed in my hope, anxious as I was to get my wife out of sight; foiled at every point,
	745 I form my plots and subtle schemes against my best-beloved. But I will go, in spite of all, with Calchas the priest, to inquire the goddess’s good pleasure, fraught with ill-luck as it is to me, and with trouble to Hellas. He who is wise should keep in his house
	750 a good and useful wife or none at all.

[Spoken]

Achilles.
801 Where is Achaea’s general? Which of his servants will announce to him that Achilles, the son of Peleus, is at his gates seeking him? For this delay at the Euripus is not the same for all of us;
805 there are some, for instance, who, bing still unwed, have left their houses desolate and are idling here upon the beach, while others are married but without children; so strange the longing for this expedition that has fallen on their hearts by the will of the gods.
810 My own just plea I must declare, and whoever else has any wish will speak for himself. Though I have left Pharsalia, and Peleus, still I linger here by reason of these light breezes at the Euripus, restraining my Myrmidons, while they are always pressing on me,
815 saying: Why do we tarry, Achilles? how much longer must we count the days to the start for Ilium? do something if you are so minded; or lead home your men, and do not wait for the tardy action of these Atridae.

Clytemnestra.
819 Hail to you, son of the Nereid goddess! I heard your voice
820 from within the tent and came forth.

Achilles.
821 O modesty revered! who can this lady be whom I behold, so richly dowered with beauty’s gifts?

Clytemnestra.
823 No wonder you do not know me, seeing I am one you have never before set eyes on; I praise your reverent address to modesty.

Achilles.
825 Who are you, and why have you come to the mustering of the Danaids—you, a woman, to a fenced camp of men?

Clytemnestra.
827 I am the daughter of Leda; my name is Clytemnestra; and my husband king Agamemnon.

Achilles.
829 Well and shortly answered on all important points,
830 but it is shameful for me to stand talking to women.

Clytemnestra.
831 Stay; why seek to escape? give me your hand, a prelude to a happy marriage.

Achilles.
833 What is it you say? I give you my hand? To lay a finger where I have no right, I could never meet Agamemnon’s eye.

Clytemnestra.
835 The best of rights you have, seeing it is my child you will wed, O son of the sea-goddess, daughter of Nereus.

Achilles.
837 What wedding do you speak of? Words fail me, lady; can your wits have gone astray and are you inventing this?

Clytemnestra.
839 All men are naturally shy in the presence of new relations,
840 when these remind them of their wedding.

Achilles.
841 Lady, I have never courted your daughter, nor have the sons of Atreus ever mentioned marriage to me.

Clytemnestra.
843 What can it mean? Your turn now to marvel at my words, for yours are very strange to me.

Achilles.
845 Hazard a guess; that we can both do in this matter; for it may be we are both correct in our statements.

Clytemnestra.
847 What! have I suffered such indignity? The marriage I am courting has no reality it seems; I am ashmed of it.

Achilles.
849 Some one perhaps has made a mock of you and me;
850 pay no heed to it; make light of it.

Clytemnestra.
851 Farewell; I can no longer face you with unfaltering eyes, after being made a liar and suffering undeservedly.

Achilles.
853 It is farewell I bid you too, lady; and I go within the tent to seek your husband.

Old man.
855 Stranger of the race of Aeacus, stay awhile! Ho there! I mean you, O goddess-born, and you, daughter of Leda.

Achilles.
857 Who is it calling through the half-opened door? What fear his voice betrays!

Old man.
858 A slave; of that I am not proud, for fortune does not permit it.

Achilles.
859 Whose slave are you? not mine; for mine and Agamemnon’s goods are separate.

Old man.
860 I belong to this lady who stands before the tent, a gift to her from Tyndareus her father.

Achilles.
861 I am waiting; tell me, if you are desirous, why you have stopped me.

Old man.
862 Are you really all alone here at the door?

Achilles.
863 To us alone will you address yourself; come forth from the king’s tent.

Old man.
864 O Fortune and my own foresight, preserve whom I desire!

Achilles.
865 That speech will save them in the future; it has a certain pompous air.

Clytemnestra.
866 Delay not for the sake of touching my right hand, if there is anything that you would say to me.

Old man.
867 Well, you know my character and my devotion to you and your children.

Clytemnestra.
868 I know you have grown old in the service of my house.

Old man.
869 Likewise you know it was in your dowry king Agamemnon received me.

Clytemnestra.
870 Yes, you came to Argos with me, and have been mine this long time past.

Old man.
871 True; and I bear all goodwill to you, less to your husband.

Clytemnestra.
872 Come, come, unfold whatever you have to say.

Old man.
873 Her father, he that begot her, is on the point of slaying your daughter with his own hand.

Clytemnestra.
874 How? That for your story, old man! you are mad.

Old man.
875 Severing with a sword the hapless girl’s white throat.

Clytemnestra.
876 Ah, alas for me! Does my husband happen to have gone mad?

Old man.
877 No; he is sane, except where you and your daughter are concerned; there he is mad.

Clytemnestra.
878 What is his reason? what vengeful fiend impels him?

Old man.
879 Oracles, at least so Calchas says, in order that the army may start—

Clytemnestra.
880 Where? Alas for me, and for the one her father is going to kill!

Old man.
881 To the halls of Dardanus, that Menelaus may recover Helen.

Clytemnestra.
882 So Helen’s return then was fated to affect Iphigenia?

Old man.
883 You know all; her father is about to offer your child to Artemis.

Clytemnestra.
884 But that marriage—what pretext had it for bringing me from home?

Old man.
885 An inducement to you to bring your daughter cheerfully, to wed her to Achilles.

Clytemnestra.
886 On a deadly errand have you come, my daughter, both you, and I, your mother.

Old man.
887 Piteous the lot of both of you, and dreadful Agamemnon’s venture.

Clytemnestra.
888 Alas, I am undone; my eyes can no longer hold their tears.

Old man.
889 If the loss of children is painful, shed your tears.

Clytemnestra.
890 From where, old man, do you say you had this news?

Old man.
891 I had started to carry you a letter referring to the former writing.

Clytemnestra.
892 Forbidding or combining to urge my bringing the child to her death?

Old man.
893 No, forbidding it, for your lord was then in his sober senses.

Clytemnestra.
894 How comes it then, if you were really bringing me a letter, that you do not now deliver it into my hands?

Old man.
895 Menelaus snatched it from me, he who caused this trouble.

Clytemnestra.
896 Do you hear that, son of Peleus, the Nereid’s child?

Achilles.
897 I have been listening to the tale of your sufferings, and I do not bear my own lightly.

Clytemnestra.
898 They will slay my child; they have tricked her with your marriage.

Achilles.
899 Like you I blame your lord, nor do I view it with mere indifference.

Clytemnestra.
900 No longer will I let shame prevent my kneeling to you, a mortal to one goddess-born; why do I affect reserve? whose interests should I consult before my child’s? Throwing herself before Achilles. Oh! help me, goddess-born, in my sore distress, and her that was called your bride, in vain, it is true, yet called she was.
905 For you it was I wreathed her head and led her forth as if to marriage, but now it is to slaughter I am bringing her. On you will come reproach because you did not help her; for though not wedded to her, yet were you the loving husband of my hapless girl in name at any rate. By your beard, your right hand, and mother too I do implore you;
910 for your name it was that worked my ruin, and you are bound to stand by that. Except your knees I have no altar to fly to; and not a friend stands at my side. You have heard the cruel abandoned scheme of Agamemnon; and I, a woman, have come, as you see, to a camp of lawless sailor-folk, bold in evil’s cause,
915 though useful when they wish; Now if you boldly stretch forth your arm in my behalf, our safety is assured; but if not, we are lost.

Chorus Leader.
917 A strange thing is motherhood, carrying with it a potent spell, in which all share, so that for their children’s sake they will endure affliction.

Achilles.
919 My proud spirit is stirred to range aloft, butI have learned to grieve in misfortune
920 and rejoice in high prosperity with equal moderation. For these are the men who can count on ordering all their life rightly by wisdom’s rules. True, there are cases where it is pleasant not to be too wise,
925 but there are others, where some store of wisdom helps. Brought up in godly Chiron’s halls myself, I learned to keep a single heart; and provided the Atridae lead well, I will obey them; but when they cease from that, no more will I obey;
930 no, but here and in Troy I will show the freedom of my nature, and, as far as in me lies, do honor to Ares with my spear. You, lady, who have suffered so cruelly from your nearest and dearest, I will, by every effort in a young man’s power, set right, investing you with that amount of pity
935 and never shall your daughter, after being once called my bride, die by her father’s hand; for I will not lend myself to your husband’s subtle tricks; no! for it will be my name that kills your child, although it does not wield the sword. Your own husband
940 is the actual cause, but I shall no longer be guiltless, if, because of me and my marriage, this maiden perishes, she that has suffered past endurance and been the victim of affronts most strangely undeserved.
944 So am I made the poorest wretch in Argos;
945 I a thing of nothing, and Menelaus counting for a man! No son of Peleus I, but the issue of a vengeful fiend, if my name shall serve your husband for the murder. No! by Nereus, who begot my mother Thetis, in his home amid the flowing waves,
950 never shall king Agamemnon touch your daughter, no! not even to the laying of a finger-tip upon her robe; or Sipylus, that frontier town of barbarism, the cradle of those chieftains’ line, will be henceforth a city indeed, while Phthia’s name will nowhere find mention.
955 Calchas, the seer, shall rue beginning the sacrifice with his barley-meal and lustral water. Why, what is a seer? A man who with luck tells the truth sometimes, with frequent falsehoods, but when his luck deserts him, collapses then and there.
959 It is not to secure a bride that I have spoken thus—there are maids unnumbered
960 eager to have my love—no! but king Agamemnon has put an insult on me; he should have asked my leave to use my name as a means to catch the child, for it was I chiefly who induced Clytemnestra to betroth her daughter to me;
965 I would had yielded this to Hellas, if that was where our going to Ilium broke down; I would never have refused to further my fellow soldiers’ common interest. But as it is, I am as nothing in the eyes of those chieftains, and little they care of treating me well or ill.
970 My sword shall soon know if any one is to snatch your daughter from me, for then will I make it reek with the bloody stains of slaughter, before it reach Phrygia. Calm yourself then; as a god in his might I appeared to you, without being so, but such will I show myself for all that.

Chorus Leader.
975 Son of Peleus, your words are alike worthy of you and that sea-born deity, the holy goddess.

Clytemnestra.
977 Ah! would I could find words to utter your praise without excess, and yet not lose the graciousness of it by stinting it; for when the good are praised, they have some sort of feeling
980 of hatred for those who in their praise exceed the mean. But I am ashamed of intruding a tale of woe, since my affliction touches myself alone and you are not affected by troubles of mine; but still it looks well for the man of worth to assist the unfortunate, even when he is not connected with then.
985 Therefore pity us, for our suffering cries for pity; in the first place, I have harbored an idle hope, in thinking to have you marry my daughter; and next, perhaps, the slaying of my child will be to you an evil omen in your wooing hereafter, against which you must guard yourself.
990 Your words were good, both first and last; for if you will it so, my daughter will be saved.
992 Will you have her clasp your knees as a suppliant? it is no maid’s part; yet if it seems good to you, why, come she shall with the modest look of free-born maid;
995 but if I shall obtain the same end from you without her coming then let her abide within, for there is dignity in her reserve; still reserve must only go as far as the case allows.

Achilles.
998 Do not bring your daughter out for me to see, lady, nor let us incur the reproach of the ignorant;
1000 for an army, when gathered together without domestic duties to employ it, loves the evil gossip of malicious tongues. After all, should you both supplicate me, you will attain a like result as if I had never been supplicated; for I am myself engaged in a mighty struggle to rid you of your troubles.
1005 One thing be sure you have heard; I will not tell a lie; if I do that or idly mock you, may I die, but live if I preserve the girl.

Clytemnestra.
1008 Bless you for always helping the distressed!

Achilles.
1009 Hearken then to me, that the matter may succeed.

Clytemnestra.
1010 What is your proposal? for hear you I must.

Achilles.
1011 Let us once more urge her father to a better frame of mind.

Clytemnestra.
1012 He is something of a coward, and fears the army too much.

Achilles.
1013 Still argument overthrows argument.

Clytemnestra.
1014 Cold hope indeed; but tell me what I must do.

Achilles.
1015 Supplicate him first not to slay his children; and if he is stubborn, come to me. For if he consents to your request, my intervention need go no further, since this consent insures your safety. I too shall show myself in a better light to my friend,
1020 and the army will not blame me, if I arrange the matter by reason rather than force; while, should things turn out well, the result will prove satisfactory both to you and your friends, even without my interference.

Clytemnestra.
1024 How sensibly you speak! I must act as seems best to you;
1025 but should I fail of my object, where am I to see you again, where? Must I turn my wretched steps and find you ready to champion my distress?

Achilles.
1028 I will keep watch to guard you, where occasion calls, that none may see you passing through the army of Danaids
1030 with that scared look. Do not shame your father’s house; for Tyndareus does not deserve not to be ill spoken of, being a mighty man in Hellas.

Clytemnestra.
1033 It will be so. Command me; I must play the slave to you. If there are gods, you for your righteous dealing
1035 will find them favorable; if there are none, what need to toil? Exeunt Achilles and Clytemnestra.

[Spoken]

Clytemnestra.
1098 I have come from the tent to look out for my husband, who went away and left its shelter long ago;
1100 while my poor child, hearing of the death her father designs for her, is in tears, uttering in many keys her piteous lamentation. Catching sight of Agamamnon. It seems I was speaking of one not far away; for there is Agamemnon,
1105 who will soon be detected in the commission of a crime against his own child.

Agamemnon.

Clytemnestra.
1109 What critical moment is it that you are seizing?

Agamemnon.
1110 Send the maiden out to join her father, for the lustral water stands there ready, and barley-meal to scatter with the hand on the cleansing flame, and heifers to be slain before the marriage, in honor of the goddess Artemis, their black blood spouting from them.
