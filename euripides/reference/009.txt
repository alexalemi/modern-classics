Hippolytus (Part 2 of 3) -- Coleridge's prose, crib only

[Sung]

Chorus.
	535 Idly, idly by the streams of Alpheus and in the Pythian shrines of Phoebus, Hellas heaps the slaughtered steers; while Love we worship not, Love, the king of men, who holds the key to Aphrodite’s
	540 sweetest bower,—worship not him who, when he comes, lays waste and marks his path to mortal hearts by wide-spread woe.

Chorus.
	545 There was that maiden in Oechalia, a girl unwed, that knew no wooer yet nor married joys; her did the queen of Love snatch from her home across the sea
	550 and gave unto Alcmena’s son, mid blood and smoke and murderous marriage-hymns, to be to him a frantic fiend of hell; woe! woe for his wooing!

Chorus.
	555 Ah! holy walls of Thebes, ah! fount of Dirce, ye could testify what course the love-queen follows. For with the blazing levin-bolt
	560 did she cut short the fatal marriage of Semele, mother of Zeus-bom Bacchus. All things she doth inspire, dread goddess, winging her flight hither and thither like a bee.

[Spoken]

Phaedra.
565 Peace, ladies, peace! I am undone.

Chorus.
566 What, Phaedra, is this dread event within thy house?

Phaedra.
567 Hush! let me hear what those within are saying.

Chorus.
568 I am silent; this is surely the prelude to mischief.

Phaedra.
569 Great gods!
570 how awful are my sufferings!

[Sung]

Chorus.
	571 What a cry was there! what loud alarm! say what sudden terror, lady, doth thy soul dismay.

Phaedra.
	575 I am undone. Stand here at the door and hear the noise arising in the house.

Chorus.
	577 Thou art already by the bolted door; ’tis for thee to note the sounds that issue from within. And tell me, O tell me what mischief can be on foot.

Phaedra.
	581 ’Tis the son of the horse-loving Amazon who calls, Hippolytus, uttering foul curses on my servant.

Chorus.
	585 I hear a noise, but cannot clearly tell which way it comes. Ah! ’tis through the door the sound reached thee.

Phaedra.
	589 Yes, yes, he is calling her plainly enough a go-between in vice,
	590 traitress to her master’s honour.

Chorus.
	591 Woe, woe is me! thou art betrayed, dear mistress! What counsel shall I give thee? thy secret is out; thou art utterly undone.

Phaedra.
	594 Ah me! ah me!

Chorus.
	595 Betrayed by friends!

Phaedra.
	596 She hath ruined me by speaking of my misfortune; ’twas kindly meant, but an ill way to cure my malady.

Chorus.
	598 O what wilt thou do now in thy cruel dilemma?

Phaedra.
	599 I only know one way,
	600 one cure for these my woes and that is instant death.

Hippolytus.
	601 O mother earth! O sun’s unclouded orb! What words, unfit for any lips, have reached my ears!

Nurse.
	603 Peace, my son, lest some one hear thy outcry.

Hippolytus.
	604 I cannot hear such awful words and hold my peace.

Nurse.
	605 I do implore thee by thy fair right hand.

Hippolytus.
	606 Let go my hand, touch not my robe.

Nurse.
	607 O by thy knees I pray, destroy me not utterly.

Hippolytus.
	608 Why say this, if, as thou pretendest, thy lips are free from blame?

Nurse.
	609 My son, this is no story to be noised abroad.

Hippolytus.
	610 A virtuous tale grows fairer told to many.

Nurse.
	611 Never dishonour thy oath, thy son.

Hippolytus.
	612 My tongue an oath did take, but not my heart.

Nurse.
	613 My son, what wilt thou do? destroy thy friends?

Hippolytus.
	614 Friends indeed! the wicked are no friends of mine.

Nurse.
	615 O pardon me; to err is only human, child.

Hippolytus.
	616 Great Zeus, why didst thou, to man’s sorrow, put woman, evil counterfeit, to dwell where shines the sun? If thou wert minded that the human race should multiply, it was not from women they should have drawn their stock,
	620 but in thy temples they should have paid gold or iron or ponderous bronze and bought a family, each man proportioned to his offering, and so in independence dwelt, from women free.
	625 But now as soon as ever we would bring this plague into our home we bring its fortune to the ground.1 ’Tis clear from this how great a curse a woman is; the very father, that begot and nurtured her, to rid him of the mischief gives her a dower and packs her off;
	630 while the husband, who takes the noxious weed into his home, fondly decks his sorry idol in fine raiment and tricks her out in robes, squandering by degrees, unhappy wight! his house’s wealth. For he is in this dilemma;
	635 say his marriage has brought him good connections, he is glad then to keep the wife he loathes; or, if he gets a good wife but useless relations, he tries to stifle the bad luck with the good. But it is easiest for him who has settled in his house as wife a mere nobody, incapable from simplicity.
	640 I hate a clever woman; never may she set foot in my house who aims at knowing more than women need; for in these clever women Cypris implants a larger store of villainy, while the artless woman is by her shallow wit from levity debarred.
	645 No servant should ever have had access to a wife, but men should put to live with them beasts, which bite, not talk, in which case they could not speak to any one nor be answered back by them. But, as it is, the wicked in their chambers plot wickedness,
	650 and their servants carry it abroad.
	651 Even thus, vile wretch, thou cam’st to make me partner in an outrage on my father’s honour; wherefore I must wash that stain away in running streams, dashing the water into my ears. How could I commit so foul a crime
	655 when by the very mention of it I feel myself polluted? Be well assured, woman, ’tis only my religious scruple saves thee. For had not I unawares been caught by an oath, ’fore heaven! I would not have refrained from telling all unto my father. But now I will from the house away, so long as
	660 Theseus is abroad, and will maintain strict silence. But, when my father comes, I will return and see how thou and thy mistress face him, and so shall I learn by experience the extent of thy audacity.
	664 Perdition seize you both! (To the audience). I can never satisfy my hate for
	665 women, no! not even though some say this is ever my theme, for of a truth they always are evil. So either let some one prove them chaste, or let me still trample on them for ever.

Phaedra.
	669 O the cruel, unhappy fate of women!
	670 What arts, what arguments have we, once we have made a slip, to loose by craft the tight-drawn knot? I have met my deserts. O earth, O light of day! How can I escape the stroke of fate? How my pangs conceal, kind friends?
	675 What god will appear to help me, what mortal to take my part or help me in unrighteousness? The present calamity of my life admits of no escape. Most hapless I of all my sex!

Chorus.
	680 Alas, alas! the deed is done, thy servant’s schemes have gone awry, my queen, and all is lost.

Phaedra.
	682 Accursed woman! traitress to thy friends! How hast thou ruined me! May Zeus, my ancestor, smite thee with his fiery bolt and uproot thee from thy place.
	685 Did I not foresee thy purpose, did I not bid thee keep silence on the very matter which is now my shame? But thou wouldst not be still; wherefore my fair name will not go with me to the tomb. But now I must another scheme devise. Yon youth, in the keenness of his fury,
	690 will tell his father of my sin, and the aged Pittheus of my state, and fill the world with stories to my shame. Perdition seize thee and every meddling fool who by dishonest means would serve unwilling friends!

Nurse.
	695 Mistress, thou may’st condemn the mischief I have done, for sorrow’s sting o’ermasters thy judgment; yet can I answer thee in face of this, if thou wilt hear. ’Twas I who nurtured thee; I love thee still; but in my search for medicine to cure thy sickness I found what least I sought.
	700 Had I but succeeded, I had been counted wise, for the credit we get for wisdom is measured by our success.

Phaedra.
	702 Is it just, is it any satisfaction to me, that thou shouldst wound me first, then bandy words with me?

Nurse.
	704 We dwell on this too long; I was not wise, I own;
	705 but there are yet ways of escape from the trouble, my child.

Phaedra.
	706 Be dumb henceforth; evil was thy first advice to me, evil too thy attempted scheme. Begone and leave me, look to thyself; I will my own fortunes for the best arrange. (Exit Nurse).
	710 Ye noble daughters of Troezen, grant me the only boon I crave; in silence bury what ye here have heard.

Chorus.
	713 By majestic Artemis, child of Zeus, I swear I will never divulge aught of thy sorrows.

Phaedra.
	715 ’Tis well. But I, with all my thought, can but one way discover out of this calamity, that so I may secure my children’s honour, and find myself some help as matters stand. For never, never will I bring shame upon my Cretan home,
	720 nor will I, to save one poor life, face Theseus after my disgrace.

Chorus.
	722 Art thou bent then on some cureless woe?

Phaedra.
	723 On death; the means thereto must I devise myself.

Chorus.
	724 Hush!

Phaedra.
	724b Do thou at least advise me well.
	725 For this very day shall I gladden Cypris, my destroyer, by yielding up my life, and shall own myself vanquished by cruel love. Yet shall my dying be another’s curse, that he may learn not to exult at my misfortunes;
	730 but when he comes to share the self-same plague with me, he will take a lesson in wisdom.

Chorus.
	732 O to be nestling ’neath some pathless cavern, there by god’s creating hand to grow into a bird amid the winged tribes!
	735 Away would I soar to Adrians wave-beat shore and to the waters of Eridanus; where a father’s hapless daughters in their grief for Phaethon
	740 distil into the glooming flood the amber brilliance of their tears.

Chorus.
	742 And to the apple-bearing strand of those minstrels in the west I then would come, where ocean’s lord
	745 no more to sailors grants a passage o’er the deep dark main, finding there the heaven’s holy bound, upheld by Atlas, where water from ambrosial founts wells up beside the couch of Zeus inside his halls,
	750 and holy earth, the bounteous mother, causes joy to spring in heavenly breasts.

Chorus.
	752 O white-winged bark, that o’er the booming ocean-wave didst bring
	755 my royal mistress from her happy home, to crown her queen ’mongst sorrow’s brides! Surely evil omens from either port, at least from Crete, were with that ship, what time to glorious Athens it sped its way,
	760 and the crew made fast its twisted cable-ends upon the beach of Munychus, and on the land stept out.

Chorus.
	764 Whence comes it that her heart
	765 is crushed, cruelly afflicted by Aphrodite with unholy love; so she by bitter grief o’erwhelmed will tie a noose within her bridal bower to fit it to her fair white neck,
	770 too modest for this hateful lot in life, prizing o’er all her name and fame, and striving thus to rid her soul of passion’s sting.

[Spoken]

Messenger.
776 Help! ho! To the rescue all who near the palace stand! She hath hung herself, our queen, the wife of Theseus.

Chorus.
778 Woe worth the day! the deed is done; our royal mistress is no more, dead she hangs in the dangling noose.

Messenger.
780 Haste! some one bring a two-edged knife wherewith to cut the knot about her neck!

1st Half Chorus.
782 Friends, what shall we do? think you we should enter the house, and loose the queen from the tight-drawn noose?

2nd Half Chorus.
784 Why should we? Are there not young servants here?
785 To do too much is not a safe course in life.

Messenger.
786 Lay out the hapless corpse, straighten the limbs. This was a bitter way to sit at home and keep my master’s house! [Exit Messenger.

Chorus.
788 She is dead, poor lady, so I hear. Already are they laying out the corpse.

Theseus.
790 Ladies, can ye tell me what the uproar in the palace means? There came the sound of servants weeping bitterly to mine ear. None of my household deign to open wide the gates and give me glad welcome as a traveller from prophetic shrines.
794 Hath aught befallen old Pittheus?
795 No. Though he be well advanced in years, yet should I mourn, were he to quit this house.

Chorus.
797 ’Tis not against the old, Theseus, that fate, to strike thee, aims this blow; prepare thy sorrow for a younger corpse.

Theseus.
799 Woe is me! is it a child’s life death robs me of?

Chorus.
800 They live; but, cruellest news of all for thee, their mother is no more.

Theseus.
801 What! my wife dead? By what cruel mischance?

Chorus.
802 About her neck she tied the hangman’s knot.

Theseus.
803 Had grief so chilled her blood? or what had befallen her?

Chorus.
804 I know but this, for I am myself but now arrived at the house
805 to mourn thy sorrows, O Theseus.

Theseus.
806 Woe is me! why have I crowned my head with woven garlands, when misfortune greets my embassage? Unbolt the doors, servants,
825 loose their fastenings, that I may see the piteous sight,
810 my wife, whose death is death to me. [The palace opens, disclosing the corpse.

[Sung]

Chorus.
	811 Woe! woe is thee for thy piteous lot! thou hast done thyself a hurt deep enough to overthrow this family. Ah! ah! the daring of it! done to death by violence and unnatural means, the desperate effort
	815 of thy own poor hand! Who cast the shadow o’er thy life, poor lady?

Theseus.
	817 Ah me, my cruel lot! sorrow hath done her worst on me. O fortune, how heavily hast thou set thy foot on me and on my house,
	820 by fiendish hands inflicting an unexpected stain? Nay, ’tis complete effacement of my life, making it impossible; for I see, alas! so wide an ocean of grief that I can never swim to shore again, nor breast the tide of this calamity.
	826 How shall I speak of thee, my poor wife, what tale of direst suffering tell? Thou art vanished like a bird from the covert of my hand, taking one headlong leap from me to Hades’ halls.
	830 Alas, and woe! this is a bitter, bitter sight! This must be a judgment sent by God for the sins of an ancestor, which from some far source I am bringing on myself.

Chorus.
	834 My prince, ’tis not to thee alone such sorrows come;
	835 thou hast lost a noble wife, but so have many others.

Theseus.
	836 Fain would I go hide me ’neath earth’s blackest depth, to dwell in darkness with the dead in misery, now that I am reft of thy dear presence! for thou hast slain me than thyself e’en more.
	840 Who can tell me what caused the fatal stroke that reached thy heart, dear wife? Will no one tell me what befell?
	845 doth my palace all in vain give shelter to a herd of menials? Woe, woe for thee, my wife! sorrows past speech, past bearing, I behold within my house; myself a ruined man, my home a solitude, my children orphans!

Chorus.
	848 Gone and left us hast thou, fondest wife and noblest of all women
	850 ’neath the sun’s bright eye or night’s star-lit radiance.

Chorus.
	852 Poor house, what sorrows are thy portion now! My eyes are wet with streams of tears to see thy fate;
	855 but the sequel to this tragedy has long with terror filled me.

Theseus.
	856 Ha! what means this letter? clasped in her dear hand it hath some strange tale to tell. Hath she, poor lady, as a last request, written her bidding as to my marriage and her children?
	860 Take heart, poor ghost; no wife henceforth shall wed thy Theseus or invade his house. Ah! how yon seal of my dead wife stamped with her golden ring affects my sight! Come, I will unfold the sealed packet
	865 and read her letter’s message to me.

Chorus.
	866 Woe unto us! Here is yet another evil in the train by heaven sent. Looking to what has happened, I should count my lot in life no longer worth one’s while to gain. My master’s house,
	870 alas! is ruined, brought to naught, I say. Spare it, O Heaven, if it may be. Hearken to my prayer, for I see, as with prophetic eye, an omen boding mischief.

Theseus.
	874 O horror! woe on woe!
	875 and still they come, too deep for words, too heavy to bear. Ah me!

Chorus.
	876 What is it? speak, if I may share in it.

Theseus.
	877 This letter loudly tells a hideous tale! where can I escape my load of woe? For I am ruined and undone, so awful are the words I find here written clear
	880 as if she cried them to me; woe is me!

Chorus.
	881 Alas! thy words declare themselves the harbingers of woe.

Theseus.
	882 I can no longer keep the cursed tale within the portal of my lips, cruel though its utterance be. Ah me!
	885 Hippolytus hath dared by brutal force to violate my honour, recking naught of Zeus, whose awful eye is over all. O father Poseidon, once didst thou promise to fulfil three prayers of mine ; answer one of these and slay my son, let him not escape this single day,
	890 if the prayers thou gavest me were indeed with issue fraught.

Chorus.
	891 O king, I do conjure thee, call back that prayer; hereafter thou wilt know thy error. Hear, I pray.

Theseus.
	893 Impossible! Moreover I will banish him from this land, and by one of two fates shall he be struck down;
	895 either Poseidon, out of respect to my prayer, will cast his dead body into the house of Hades; or exiled from this land, a wanderer to some foreign shore, shall he eke out a life of misery.

Chorus.
	899 Lo! where himself doth come, thy son Hippolytus, in good time;
	900 dismiss thy hurtful rage, King Theseus, and bethink thee what is best for thy family.

Hippolytus.
	902 I heard thy voice, father, and hasted to come hither; yet know I not the cause of thy present sorrow, but would fain learn of thee.
	905 Ha! what is this? thy wife a corpse I see; this is passing strange; ’twas but now I left her; a moment since she looked upon the light.
	909 How came she thus? the manner of her death?
	910 this would I learn of thee, father. Art dumb? silence availeth not in trouble; nay, for the heart that fain would know all must show its curiosity even in sorrow’s hour. Be sure it is not right, father,
	915 to hide misfortunes from those who love, ay, more than love thee.

Theseus.
	916 O ye sons of men, victims of a thousand idle errors, why teach your countless crafts, why scheme and seek to find a way for everything, while one thing ye know not nor ever yet have made your prize,
	920 a way to teach them wisdom whose souls are void of sense?

Hippolytus.
	921 A very master in his craft the man, who can force fools to be wise! But these ill-timed subtleties of thine, father, make me fear thy tongue is running riot through trouble.

Theseus.
	925 Fie upon thee! man needs should have some certain test set up to try his friends, some touchstone of their hearts, to know each friend whether he be true or false; all men should have two voices, one the voice of honesty, expediency’s the other,
	930 so would honesty confute its knavish opposite, and then we could not be deceived.

Hippolytus.
	932 Say, hath some friend been slandering me and hath he still thine ear? am I, though guiltless, banned? I am amazed indeed;
	935 thy random, frantic words fill me with wild alarm.
