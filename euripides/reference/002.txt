[Sung]

Heracles.
	773 Ho! sirrah, why that solemn, thoughtful look? ’Tis not the way for servants to scowl on guests,
	775 but with courteous soul to welcome them. But thou, seeing a friend of thy master arrive, receivest him with sullen, lowering brow, though ’tis but a stranger that is the object of thy mourning. Come hither, that thou too mayst learn more wisdom.
	780 Dost know the nature of this mortal state? I trow not; how shouldst thou? Well, lend an ear to me. Death is the common debt of man; no mortal really knows if he will live to see the morrow’s light;
	785 for Fortune’s issues are not in our ken, beyond the teacher’s rule they lie, no art can master them. Hearken then to this and learn of me, be merry, drink thy cup, and count the present day thine own, the rest to Fortune yield.
	790 And to Cypris too, sweetest of the gods by far to man, thy tribute pay, for kindly is her mood. Let be those other cares, and heed my counsel if thou think’st I speak aright; methinks I do. Come, banish this excessive grief,
	795 and drink a cup with me when thou hast passed beyond these doors and wreathed thy brow; and I feel sure the plash of wine within the cup will bring thee to a better haven from this crabbed mood, this cabined state of mind. Mortals we are, and mortals’ thoughts should have;
	800 for all they who frown and scowl do miss,—leastways I think so,—the true life and get themselves misfortune.

Attendant.
	803 I know all that, but our present state has little claim on revelry or laughter.

Heracles.
	805 The dead was a stranger woman; grieve not to excess; for the rulers of thy house are living.

Attendant.
	807 How, living? Thou knowest not the trouble in the house.

Heracles.
	808 I do, unless thy master did in aught deceive me.

Attendant.
	809 Too hospitable is he.

Heracles.
	810 Was I to miss good cheer because a stranger had died?

Attendant.
	811 A stranger surely! quite a stranger she!

Heracles.
	812 Is there some trouble that he withheld from me?

Attendant.
	813 Farewell, go thy way! my master’s troubles are my care.

Heracles.
	814 This word of thine heralds not a grief for strangers felt.

Attendant.
	815 Had it been, the sight of thy merriment had not grieved me so.

Heracles.
	816 Can it be mine host hath strangely wronged me?

Attendant.
	817 Thou earnest at no proper time for our house to welcome thee, for sorrow is come upon us; lo! thou seest our shorn heads and robes of sable hue.

Heracles.
	819b Who is it that is dead?
	820 Is it a child or his aged sire that hat hath passed away?

Attendant.
	821 Nay, sir guest, ’tis Admetus’ wife that is no more.

Heracles.
	822 What sayest thou? and did ye then in spite of that admit me to your cheer?

Attendant.
	823 Yes, for his regard would not let him send thee from his door.

Heracles.
	824 Unhappy husband, what a wife hast thou lost!

Attendant.
	825 We are all undone, not she alone.

Heracles.
	826 I knew it when I saw his streaming eye, shorn head and downcast look, yet did he persuade me, saying it was a stranger he was bearing to burial. So I did constrain myself and passed his gates
	830 and sat drinking in his hospitable halls, when he was suffering thus. And have I wreathed my head and do I revel still? But—thou to hold thy peace when such a crushing sorrow lay upon the house! Where is he burying her? Whither shall I go to find her?

Attendant.
	835 Beside the road that leadeth straight to Larissa, shalt thou see her carved tomb outside the suburb.

Heracles.
	837 O heart, O soul, both sufferers oft, now show the mettle of that son Tirynthian Alcmena, daughter of Electryon, bare to Zeus.
	840 For I must save this woman, dead but now, setting Alcestis once again within this house, and to Admetus this kind service render. So I will go and watch for death the black-robed monarch of the dead, and him methinks I shall find
	845 as he drinks of the blood-offering near the tomb. And if, from ambush rushing, once I catch and fold him in my arms’ embrace, none shall ever wrest him thence with smarting ribs, ere he give up the woman unto me.
	850 But should I fail to find my prey and he come not to the clotted blood, I will go to the sunless home of those beneath the earth, to Persephone and her king, and make to them my prayer, sure that I shall bring Alcestis up again, to place her in the hands of him, my host,
	855 who welcomed me to his house nor drove me thence, though fortune smote him hard, but this his noble spirit strove to hide out of regard for me. What host more kind than him in Thessaly? or in in the homes of Hellas? Wherefore shall he never say
	860 his generous deeds were lavished on a worthless wretch. [Exeunt HERACLES and Servant.

Admetus.
	861 Ah me! I loathe this entering in, and loathe to see my widowed home. Woe, woe is me! Whither shall I go? Where stand? what say? or what suppress? Would God that I were dead!
	865 Surely in an evil hour my mother gave me birth. The dead I envy, and would fain be as they, and long to dwell within their courts. No joy to me to see the light, no joy to tread the earth;
	870 such a hostage death hath reft me of and handed o’er to Hades.

Chorus.
	872 Move forward, go within the shelter of thy house.

Admetus.
	872b Woe is me!

Chorus.
	873 Thy sufferings claim these cries of woe.

Admetus.
	873b Ah me!

Chorus.
	874 Through anguish hast thou gone, full well I know.

Admetus.
	874b Alas! alas!

Chorus.
	875 Thou wilt not help the dead one whit.

Admetus.
	875b O misery!

Chorus.
	876 Nevermore to see thy dear wife face to face is grief indeed.

Admetus.
	878 Thy words have probed the sore place in my heart. What greater grief can come to man
	880 than the loss of a faithful wife? Would I had never married or shared with her my home! I envy those ’mongst men who have nor wife nor child. Theirs is but one life; to grieve for that is no excessive burden;
	885 but to see children fall ill and bridal beds emptied by death’s ravages is too much to bear, when one might go through life without wife or child.

Chorus.
	889 A fate we cannot cope with is come upon us.

Admetus.
	889b Woe is me!

Chorus.
	890 But thou to sorrow settest no limit.

Admetus.
	890b Ah! ah!

Chorus.
	891 ’Tis hard to bear, but still—

Admetus.
	891b Woe is me!

Chorus.
	892 Thou art not the first to lose—

Admetus.
	892b O! woe is me!

Chorus.
	893 A wife; misfortune takes a different shape for every man she plagues.

Admetus.
	895 O the weary sorrow! O the grief for dear ones dead and gone! Why didst thou hinder me from plung-ing into the gaping grave, there to lay me down and die with her, my peerless bride?
	900 Then would Hades for that one have gotten these two faithful souls at once, crossing the nether lake together.

Chorus.
	903 I had a kinsman once, within whose home died
	905 his only son, worthy of a father’s tears; yet in spite of that he bore his grief resignedly, childless though he was, his hair already turning grey, himself far on in years, upon
	910 life’s downward track.

Admetus.
	912 O house of mine, how can I enter thee? how can I live here, now that fortune turns against me? Ah me! How wide the gulf ’twixt then and now!
	915 Then with torches cut from Pelion’s pines, with marriage hymns I entered in, holding my dear wife’s hand; and at our back a crowd of friends with cheerful cries, singing the happy lot of my dead wife and me,
	920 calling us a noble pair made one, children both of highborn lineage; but now the voice of woe instead of wedding hymns, and robes of black instead of snowy white usher me
	925 into my house to my deserted couch.

Chorus.
	926 Hard upon prosperous fortune came this sorrow to thee, a stranger to adversity; yet hast thou saved thy soul alive.
	930 Thy wife is dead and gone; her love she leaves with thee. What new thing is here? Death ere now from many a man hath torn a wife.

Admetus.
	935 My friends, I count my dead wife’s lot more blest than mine, for all it seems not so; for nevermore can sorrow touch her for ever; all her toil is over, and glorious is her fame. While I, who had no right to live, have passed the bounds of fate
	940 only to live a life of misery; I know it now. For how shall I endure to enter this my house? Whom shall I address, by whom be answered back, to find aught joyful in my entering in? Whither shall I turn? Within, the desolation will drive me forth,
	945 whensoe’er I see my widowed couch, the seat whereon she sat, the floor ail dusty in the house, and my babes falling at my knees with piteous tears for their mother, while my servants mourn the good mistress their house hath lost.
	950 These are the sorrows in my home, while abroad the marriages among Thessalians and the thronging crowds of women will drive me mad, for I can never bear to gaze upon the compeers of my wife. And whoso is my foe will taunt me thus,
	955 Behold him living in his shame, a wretch who quailed at death himself, but of his coward heart gave up his wedded wife instead, and escaped from Hades; doth he deem himself a man after that? And he loathes his parents, though himself refused to die. Such ill report shall I to my evils add.
	960 What profit, then, my friends, for me to live, in fame and fortune ruined.

Chorus.
	962 Myself have traced the Muses’ path, have soared amid the stars, have laid my hold on many a theme,
	965 and yet have found naught stronger than necessity, no spell inscribed on Thracian tablets written there by Orpheus, the sweet singer,
	970 no! nor aught among the simples culled by Phoebus for the toiling race of men, and given to Asclepius’ sons.

Chorus.
	973 The only goddess she, whose altar or whose image man cannot approach;
	975 victims she heedeth not. O come not to me, dread goddess, in greater might than heretofore in my career. Even Zeus requires thy aid to bring to pass whatso he wills.
	980 Thou too it is that by sheer force dost bend the steel among the Chalybes; nor is there any pity in thy relentless nature.

Chorus.
	984 This is the goddess that hath gripped thee too in chains thou canst not ’scape;
	985 yet steel thy heart, for all thy weeping ne’er will bring to light again the dead from the realms below. Even sons of gods perish in darkness
	990 in the hour of death. We loved her while she was with us, we love her still though dead; noblest of her sex was she, the wife thou tookest to thy bed.

Chorus.
	995 Her tomb let none regard as the graves of those who die and are no more, but let her have honours equal with the gods, revered by every traveller;
	1000 and many a one will cross the road and read this verse aloud, This is she that died in days gone by to save her lord; now is she a spirit blest. Hail, lady revered; be kind to us!
	1005 Such glad greeting shall she have.

[Spoken]

Chorus.
1006 But see, Admetus! yonder, I believe, comes Alcmena’s son toward thy hearth.

Heracles.
1008 Admetus, to a friend we should speak freely, not hold our peace and harbour in our hearts complaints.
1010 I came to thee in thy hour of sorrow and claimed the right to prove myself thy friend, but thou wouldst not tell me that she, thy wife, lay stretched in death; but didst make me a welcome guest in thy halls, as though thy whole concern was centred on a stranger’s loss.
1015 So I crowned my head and poured drink-offerings to the gods in that thy house of sorrow. Wherefore I do blame thee for this treatment of me, yet would not grieve thee in thy trouble. So now the reason I have turned my steps and come hither again, I will tell.
1020 This lady take and keep for me until I come bringing hither the steeds of Thrace, after I have slain the lord of the Bistones. But should I fare as fare I fain would not, I give her to thee to serve within thy halls.
1025 With no small toil she came into my hands. ’Twas thus: I found folk just appointing an open contest for athletes, well worth a struggle, and there I won her as a prize and brought her thence; now those who were successful in the lighter contests had
1030 horses for their prize, but those who conquered in severer feats, in boxing and wrestling, won herds of oxen, and this woman was to be added thereto; with such a chance ’twere shame indeed to pass so fair a guerdon by.
1035 So thou must take her in thy charge, as I said; for not by theft but honest toil I won the prize I bring; and maybe e’en thou in time wilt thank me.

Admetus.
1037 ’Twas not because of any slight or unkind thought of thee that I concealed my wife’s sad fate; but this were adding grief to grief
1040 if thou hadst gone from hence to the halls of some other friend; and it sufficed that I should mourn my sorrow. But I do beseech thee, prince, if ’tis possible, bid some other Thessalian, one who hath not suffered as I have, keep the maiden for thee,—and thou hast many
1045 friends in Pherae; remind me not of my misfortune. For I could not see her in my house and stay my tears. Oh! add not new affliction to my stricken heart, for sure by sorrow am I bowed enough. And where within my halls could a tender maiden live?
1050 for such she is, as her dress and vesture show. Is she to dwell where men consort? Then how shall she retain her maiden purity, if ’mid our youths she come and go? O Heracles, it is no easy task to check a young man’s fancy, and I am anxious for thy sake.
1055 Or am I to take her to my dead wife’s bower and care for her? How can I bring her there to fill the other’s bed? Twofold reproach I fear; first, some fellow-townsman may taunt me with betraying my benefactress in eagerness to wed a new young bride;
1060 next, there is my dead wife, whom I should much regard, for she doth merit all my reverence. Thou too, lady, whosoe’er thou art, believe me, art the very counterfeit presentment of Alcestis, the picture of her form, ah me! O take this maiden, I conjure thee, from my sight;
1065 slay me not already slain. For in her I seem once more to see my wife; and my heart is darkly troubled, and the fountains of my eyes are loosed. Ah, woe is me! Now do I taste the bitterness of this my grief.

Chorus.
1070 Indeed I cannot call thy fortune blest, yet heaven’s gift must thou endure, whoe’er the god that comes to bring it.

Heracles.
1072 Would I had the power to bring thy wife up to the light from the halls of death, and confer this kindness on thee!

Admetus.
1075 Right well I know thou wouldst. But what of that? The dead can never come to life again.

Heracles.
1077 Do not exceed the mark, but bear thy grief with moderation.

Admetus.
1078 ’Tis easier to advise than to suffer and endure.

Heracles.
1079 Yet what thy gain, if thou for aye wilt mourn?

Admetus.
1080 I too know that myself, but some strange yearning leads me on.

Heracles.
1081 Love for the dead compels a tear.

Admetus.
1082 Her death was mine, more than any words of mine can tell.

Heracles.
1083 Thou hast lost a noble wife; who shall gainsay it?

Admetus.
1084 Life henceforth hath lost all charm for me.

Heracles.
1085 Time will soothe the smart; as yet thy grief is young.

Admetus.
1086 Time! use that word, if death and time are one.

Heracles.
1087 A new wife and a longing for a fresh marriage will stay thy sorrow.

Admetus.
1088 Peace! What words are thine? I ne’er of thee had thought it.

Heracles.
1089 What! wilt never wed, but preserve thy widowed state?

Admetus.
1090 There is no woman living that shall share my couch.

Heracles.
1091 Dost think that this will help the dead at all?

Admetus.
1092 My reverence she deserves, where’er she is.

Heracles.
1093 I praise thee, yes; but still thou bringest on thyself the charge of folly.

Admetus.
1094 So that thou never call’st me bridegroom, praise me if thou wilt.

Heracles.
1095 I praise thee for thy loyalty to thy wife.

Admetus.
1096 Come death! if ever I betray her, dead though she be.

Heracles.
1097 Well, take this maiden to the shelter of thy noble house.

Admetus.
1098 Spare me, I entreat thee by Zeus, thy sire.

Heracles.
1099 Be sure, if thou refuse, ’twill be a sad mistake.

Admetus.
1100 If I comply, remorse will gnaw my heart.

Heracles.
1101 Yield; for in god’s good time maybe thou wilt give me thanks.

Admetus.
1102 Ah! would thou hadst never won her in the games!

Heracles.
1103 Yet thou too sharest in my victory.

Admetus.
1104 True; still let this maiden go away.

Heracles.
1105 Go she shall, if go she must; but first see if this is needful.

Admetus.
1106 I needs must, else wilt thou be wroth with me.

Heracles.
1107 I have a reason good to press the matter thus.

Admetus.
1108 Have thy way then. Yet know well thy deed I disapprove.

Heracles.
1109 A day will come that thou wilt praise me; only yield.

Admetus.
1110 (to his servants). Take her in, if I needs must give her welcome in my house.

Heracles.
1111 To thy servants will I not hand her over.

Admetus.
1112 Conduct her then thyself within, if so thou thinkest good.

Heracles.
1113 Nay, but into thy hands shall mine consign her.

Admetus.
1114 I will not touch her, though she is free to go within my halls.

Heracles.
1115 To thy hand, and thine alone I her entrust.

Admetus.
1116 Prince, against my will thou dost constrain me to this deed.

Heracles.
1117 Boldly stretch out thy hand and touch the stranger maid.

Admetus.
1118 There, then, I stretch it out as toward the Gorgon’s severed head.

Heracles.
1119 Hast hold of her?

Admetus.
1119b I have.

Heracles.
1119c (removes the veil). So; keep her safely then, and
1120 in days to come thou wilt confess the son of Zeus proved himself a noble guest. Look well at her, if haply to thy gaze she have a semblance of thy wife; and now that thou art blest, cease from sorrowing.

Admetus.
1123 Great gods, what shall I say? a marvel past all hope is here! My wife, my own true wife I see,
1125 or is some mocking rapture sent by heaven to drive me mad?

Heracles.
1126 No, no; ’tis thy own wife thou seest here.

Admetus.
1127 Beware it be not a phantom from that nether world.

Heracles.
1128 No necromancer was this guest whom thou didst welcome.

Admetus.
1129 Do I behold my wife, her whom I buried?

Heracles.
1130 Be well assured thereof; still I marvel not thou dost distrust thy luck.

Admetus.
1131 May I touch her, may I speak to her as my living wife?

Heracles.
1132 Speak to her. For thou hast all thy heart’s desire.

Admetus.
1133 O form and features of my well-loved wife! past all hope I hold thee, never expecting to see thee again.

Heracles.
1135 So thou dost; may no jealous god rise against thee!

Admetus.
1136 O noble son of almighty Zeus, good luck to thee! may the father that begat thee hold thee in his keeping; for thou and none else hast raised my fallen fortunes. How didst thou bring her from the world below to this light of day?

Heracles.
1140 By encountering the god who had her in his power.

Admetus.
1141 Where didst thou engage with Death? tell me this.

Heracles.
1142 Just by the tomb I from my ambush sprang and caught him in my grip.

Admetus.
1143 But why thus speechless stands my wife?

Heracles.
1144 ’Tis not lawful yet for thee to hear her speak,
1145 ere she be purified from the gods below and the third day be come. So lead her in; and hereafter, e’en as now, be just and kind to guests, Admetus. Now farewell! for I must go to perform my appointed task
1150 for the lordly son of Sthenelus.

Admetus.
1151 Abide with us and be our welcome guest.

Heracles.
1152 Another time; now must I use all haste.

Admetus.
1153 Good luck to thee! and mayst thou come again! To the citizens and ail my realm
1155 I make this proclamation, that they institute dances in honour of the glad event, and make the altars steam with sacrifice, and offer prayers; for now have I moored my bark of life in a happier haven than before, and so will own myself a happy man.
