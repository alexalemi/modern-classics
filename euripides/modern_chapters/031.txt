Iphigenia Among the Taurians
(Part 3 of 3)

Iphigenia.
Then how can we arrange it so that we do not die, and still get what
we want? That is where our journey home is sick. The wanting is
there.

Orestes.
Could we kill the king?

Iphigenia.
That is a terrible thing to say. Strangers who have landed in a
country, murdering the man who rules it.

Orestes.
But if it will save you and me, we have to risk it.

Iphigenia.
I could not do it. But I admire the spirit.

Orestes.
What if you hid me secretly in the temple?

Iphigenia.
So that we could take the dark and slip away?

Orestes.
Night belongs to thieves. The light belongs to truth.

Iphigenia.
There are temple guards inside. We would never get past them.

Orestes.
Oh. Then we are finished. How can we be saved?

Iphigenia.
I think I have found something. Something new.

Orestes.
What kind of thing? Share the idea, so I can learn it too.

Iphigenia.
I am going to use your sufferings as my trick.

Orestes.
Women are terribly good at inventing schemes.

Iphigenia.
I will say that you have come from Argos, and that you killed your
mother.

Orestes.
Use my troubles, if you can make a profit on them.

Iphigenia.
And that it is not lawful to sacrifice you to the goddess.

Orestes.
On what grounds? I have a suspicion.

Iphigenia.
Because you are not clean. Their sense of what is holy I will turn
into fear.

Orestes.
And how does that get us any nearer to taking the goddess's image?

Iphigenia.
I will say I want to wash you clean in the springs of the sea.

Orestes.
The statue we sailed here for is still inside the house.

Iphigenia.
I will say that has to be washed too, because you touched it.

Orestes.
Where? Do you mean the wet inlet where the sea runs out?

Iphigenia.
Where your ship rides at anchor on its linen cables.

Orestes.
And will you carry the image in your own hands, or someone else?

Iphigenia.
I will. I am the only one who may touch it.

Orestes.
And Pylades here, where does he fit into our labour?

Iphigenia.
He will be said to carry the same pollution on his hands as you do.

Orestes.
Will you do all this behind the king's back, or with his knowledge?

Iphigenia.
I will talk him into it. I could never hide it from him.

Orestes.
Well, the ship is there, and her oars are ready to strike.

Iphigenia.
The rest is yours to see to. Make sure it goes well.

Orestes.
One thing more is needed: these women have to keep the secret with
us. Go to them and find the words that will persuade them. A woman has
power when it comes to pity. As for the rest, perhaps. May it all turn
out well.

Iphigenia.
My dearest women, I am looking to you. Everything I am is in your
hands now: whether I come through this, or become nothing and lose my
country, and my dear brother, and my dearest sister. Let me begin what
I have to say with this. We are women, a race that loves its own kind,
and there is nobody safer at keeping a common secret safe. Keep silent
for us, and work with us for our escape. A tongue that can be trusted
is a fine thing for the one who has it.

You see how one fortune holds three people who love each other more
than anyone: either we reach our fathers' land, or we die. And if I am
saved, you will share my fortune. I will bring you safely home to
Greece. So I beg you, by your right hand, and you, and you; you by
your dear cheek, and you by your knees, and by everything you love at
home, mother and father and children, whoever has them. What do you
say? Which of you says yes to this, and which of you is not willing?
Speak. If you do not agree to what I ask, I am destroyed, and my poor
brother with me.

Chorus.
Take heart, dear mistress. Only save yourself. Everything you charge
us with will be kept silent, as far as I am concerned. Great Zeus be
my witness.

Iphigenia.
May you have joy of those words, and be happy. Now it is your work,
and yours, to go into the house. The ruler of this land will be here
any moment, to check whether the strangers' sacrifice has been done.

Lady, you who saved me in the terrible clefts of Aulis from my
father's murdering hand, save me now too, and these men. Or else,
because of you, mortals will never again believe that Apollo's mouth
tells the truth. Come away kindly from this barbarian land to Athens.
It is not right for you to live here, when a happy city is yours to
have.

(Orestes and Pylades go into the temple. Iphigenia follows them.)

Chorus.
	Bird by the rocky ridges of the sea,
	halcyon, sea-kingfisher,
	who sing your fate as a lament,
	a cry the wise understand,
	always calling in your songs for your husband:
	I set my own dirges beside yours,
	a bird without wings,
	longing for the gatherings of Greece,
	longing for Artemis who helps women in childbirth,
	who lives by the Cynthian hill on Delos,
	by the palm with its soft leaves
	and the strong young laurel
	and the holy shoot of the grey-green olive,
	the tree Leto held when her pains came,
	and the lake that rolls its water round in a ring,
	where the singing swan
	serves the Muses.

Chorus.
	Oh, the streams of tears
	that fell on my cheeks
	when the towers came down and I went
	in the enemy's ships, among oars and spears.
	Bought and sold for gold,
	I came to a barbarian home,
	where I serve the girl who attends
	the goddess who kills the deer,
	Agamemnon's daughter, and altars
	where it is not sheep that die,
	and I envy the woman whose luck was bad
	from the start: if you grow up in hardship
	you do not tire under it.
	Bad luck changes.
	It is being brought low after happiness
	that is a heavy life for mortals.

Chorus.
	And you, lady, the Argive ship
	with its fifty oars will carry home.
	The wax-bound reed of Pan of the mountains
	will whistle and call the time to the rowers,
	and Apollo the prophet, with the ring
	of his seven-stringed lyre,
	will sing you all the way
	to the shining land of the Athenians.
	You will leave me here
	and go with the oars splashing.
	In the breeze the forestays will spread
	the sail's foot over the prow, past the beak
	of the fast ship.

Chorus.
	If only I could walk the bright race-course
	where the fire of the sun goes,
	and over the rooms of my own home
	fold the wings on my back
	and stop beating them.
	If only I could stand in the dances again,
	where I stood as a girl at glorious weddings,
	whirling beside my dear mother's feet
	with the bands of girls my own age,
	rising up to the contest of grace,
	the rich rivalry of hair,
	and shadowed my cheeks
	with my many-coloured veil
	and the curls I wound about them.

(Thoas comes in with his attendants.)

Thoas.
Where is the woman who keeps the gate of this house, the Greek? Has she
begun the rite on the strangers yet? Are their bodies blazing in the
fire in the holy inner room?

Chorus.
Here she is, my lord. She will tell you everything plainly.

(Iphigenia comes out of the temple with the image of the goddess in her
arms.)

Thoas.
Ah. Daughter of Agamemnon, why are you lifting the goddess's image in
your arms, off the base it is never moved from?

Iphigenia.
My lord, stop where you are. Stay in the doorway.

Thoas.
What is it, Iphigenia? What is new in the house?

Iphigenia.
I spit it out. Pollution. That word I give to Holiness, the goddess.

Thoas.
What is this strange opening of yours? Say it plainly.

Iphigenia.
The victims you caught for me are not clean, my lord.

Thoas.
What taught you that? Or are you just telling me your opinion?

Iphigenia.
The goddess's image turned round on its base and faced the other way.

Thoas.
By itself? Or did an earthquake turn it?

Iphigenia.
By itself. And it closed its eyes.

Thoas.
And the cause? Was it the filth on the strangers?

Iphigenia.
That, and nothing else. They have done terrible things.

Thoas.
What, did they kill one of our barbarians on the shore?

Iphigenia.
They came here with a murder from home already on them.

Thoas.
What murder? I want to know. I have fallen in love with knowing.

Iphigenia.
They cut their mother down. The two of them, with one sword.

Thoas.
Apollo. Not even among barbarians would anyone have dared that.

Iphigenia.
They have been hunted and driven out of the whole of Greece.

Thoas.
And is that why you are carrying the image outside?

Iphigenia.
Yes, under the holy open sky, to move it away from the blood.

Thoas.
How did you find out the strangers' pollution?

Iphigenia.
I questioned them, when the goddess's image turned its back.

Thoas.
Greece has brought you up clever. How well you saw it.

Iphigenia.
And yet they dangled a sweet bait for my heart.

Thoas.
Bringing you some charm of news from Argos?

Iphigenia.
That Orestes, my only brother, is doing well.

Thoas.
So that you would save them, for the pleasure of their news.

Iphigenia.
And that my father is alive and prospering.

Thoas.
But you swam back to the goddess's side, and rightly.

Iphigenia.
Yes. I hate all Greece. It destroyed me.

Thoas.
Then what do we do about the strangers? Tell me.

Iphigenia.
We are bound to respect the law as it stands.

Thoas.
Then why are the holy water and your sword not at work?

Iphigenia.
First I want to wash them clean, with a holy purification.

Thoas.
In spring water, or the spray of the sea?

Iphigenia.
The sea washes away all the evils of mankind.

Thoas.
They would certainly fall holier for the goddess that way.

Iphigenia.
And my own business would go better that way too.

Thoas.
But does the surf not break against the temple itself?

Iphigenia.
It needs solitude. There are other things we have to do as well.

Thoas.
Take them wherever you want. I have no wish to see what may not be
seen.

Iphigenia.
The goddess's image must be purified too.

Thoas.
Yes, if the stain of a mother's blood has fallen on it.

Iphigenia.
I would never have lifted it off its base otherwise.

Thoas.
Your piety is right, and so is your forethought.

Iphigenia.
Now, do you know what I need done?

Thoas.
It is for you to tell me.

Iphigenia.
Put chains on the strangers.

Thoas.
Where could they run from you?

Iphigenia.
Greece knows nothing about keeping faith.

Thoas.
Go and fetch chains, attendants.

Iphigenia.
And have them bring the strangers out here.

Thoas.
It will be done.

Iphigenia.
With their heads covered in their robes.

Thoas.
Out of the sun's blaze.

Iphigenia.
And send some of your servants with me.

Thoas.
These men will go with you.

Iphigenia.
And send someone to the city to announce...

Thoas.
Announce what?

Iphigenia.
That everyone is to stay indoors.

Thoas.
So that they do not run into the murder?

Iphigenia.
Yes. Things like this are polluting.

Thoas.
You. Go and announce it.

Iphigenia.
That nobody is to come near the sight.

Thoas.
You take good care of the city.

Iphigenia.
And of the friends I owe the most to.

Thoas.
You mean me by that.

Thoas.
The whole city admires you, and it has good reason.

Iphigenia.
And you, stay here in front of the temple, and for the goddess...

Thoas.
Do what?

Iphigenia.
Purify the house with a torch.

Thoas.
So that it is clean when you come back.

Iphigenia.
And when the strangers come out through the door...

Thoas.
What must I do?

Iphigenia.
Hold your robe in front of your eyes.

Thoas.
So that I do not take the killer's stain.

Iphigenia.
And if I seem to be taking too long...

Thoas.
How long should I allow?

Iphigenia.
Do not be surprised at anything.

Thoas.
Do the goddess's business well. There is no hurry.

Iphigenia.
If only this purification would fall out as I want it.

Thoas.
I pray for that with you.

(Orestes and Pylades are brought out of the temple by the guards, in
chains, their heads covered, with attendants carrying lambs and
torches.)

Iphigenia.
Here they come, the strangers, out of the house now, and the goddess's
ornaments, and the new-born lambs, so that I can wash polluted blood
away with blood, and the blaze of the torches, and everything else I
set out to purify the strangers and the goddess. I proclaim to the
citizens: keep clear of this pollution. Any keeper of the temple who
keeps his hands clean for the gods, anyone on his way to make a
marriage, any woman heavy with a child: run, stand back, so that this
filth does not fall on anyone.

Lady, virgin daughter of Zeus and Leto, if I wash the blood off these
men and make the sacrifice where it should be made, you will live in a
clean house, and we will be fortunate. The rest I do not say. But I
signal it to the gods, who know more, and to you, goddess.

(Iphigenia goes out toward the shore, carrying the image, with the
prisoners and Thoas's men behind her. Thoas goes into the temple.)

Chorus.
	A fine child, the son of Leto,
	whom the Delian mother bore once
	in the fruitful valleys, golden-haired,
	clever with the lyre; and with him the sister
	who glories in the true aim of her arrows.
	And the mother left the famous place of her labour
	and carried him from the ridge by the sea
	to the peak of Parnassus, with its rushing waters,
	where the dances run wild for Dionysus.
	There the wine-dark serpent
	with the dappled back,
	armoured in bronze under the shady, leafy laurel,
	the earth's enormous monster,
	kept guard over the oracle of Earth.
	Still a baby, still a baby, still leaping
	in your dear mother's arms,
	you killed him, Apollo, and stepped into the holy oracle,
	and you sit on the golden tripod, on the throne that never lies,
	dealing out to mortals the prophecies the gods send,
	up from the inner shrine, neighbour of Castalia's streams,
	holding the house at the centre of the earth.

Chorus.
	But when he came and drove Themis,
	the daughter of Earth, away from the holy oracle of Pytho,
	Earth gave birth to phantoms, dreams of the night,
	which told the cities of men
	what had been, and what came after,
	and everything that was going to happen,
	in the dark beds of sleep on the ground.
	So Earth took the honour of prophecy
	away from Apollo, out of jealousy for her daughter.
	And the lord ran fast to Olympus
	and wound his baby arm round the throne of Zeus,
	to have the earth-goddess's anger lifted
	from the Pythian house, and the night voices silenced.
	And Zeus laughed, that the child had come so quickly
	wanting the worship that pays in gold.
	He shook his hair, and the voices of the night stopped,
	and he took away from mortals
	the truth that shows its face in the dark,
	and gave the honours back to Apollo,
	and to mortals confidence in the songs of prophecy
	at the throne where so many strangers come.

(A Messenger, one of the king's men, runs in from the shore.)

Messenger.
You, guards of the temple, you who stand by the altar, where has
Thoas, the lord of this land, gone? Open the well-bolted gates and call
the ruler of the country out of this house.

Chorus.
What is it, if I may speak without being told to?

Messenger.
The two young men are gone. Away. By the plotting of Agamemnon's
daughter they are escaping from this country, and they have the holy
image with them, in the hold of a Greek ship.

Chorus.
That is an unbelievable story. But the man you want to see, the lord
of this country, has left the temple in a hurry.

Messenger.
Where? He has to know what is being done.

Chorus.
We do not know. But go and chase after him, and wherever you find him,
give him your news.

Messenger.
Look at that. What a faithless race you women are. You have your share
in what has been done as well.

Chorus.
You are out of your mind. What do we have to do with the strangers'
escape? Go to the king's door, as fast as you can.

Messenger.
No. Not until this interpreter tells me the word: is the ruler of the
land inside, or is he not? Hey there. Undo the bolts, I am talking to
you inside, and tell the master I am at the gates with a cargo of
fresh bad news.

(Thoas comes out of the temple.)

Thoas.
Who is raising this shouting round the goddess's house, hammering on
the gates and sending the noise inside?

Messenger.
Ah. These women told me you had gone out, and they were driving me
away from the house. And you were inside all along.

Thoas.
What profit were they expecting? What were they hunting?

Messenger.
I will explain about them later. Hear what is at your feet now. The
young woman who stood at these altars, Iphigenia, has gone out of the
country with the strangers, and she has the goddess's holy image with
her. The purification was a fraud.

Thoas.
What are you saying? What wind of fortune got into her?

Messenger.
She is saving Orestes. That will amaze you.

Thoas.
Which Orestes? The one the daughter of Tyndareus bore?

Messenger.
The one the goddess had consecrated to herself at these altars.

Thoas.
A wonder. What bigger name can I find for it?

Messenger.
Do not turn your mind to that. Listen to me. Look at it clearly, hear
it out, and then think out a pursuit that will hunt the strangers
down.

Thoas.
Speak. You are right. It is no short crossing they are running for, no
voyage that will get them out of range of my spear.

Messenger.
When we came to the shore of the sea, where Orestes's ship had been
moored out of sight, Agamemnon's daughter signalled to us, the men you
sent along holding the strangers' chains, to stand well back, as if she
were about to make the secret fire-sacrifice and the purification she
had come for. And she went on alone behind them, holding the two
strangers' chains in her hands. It looked suspicious, my lord. But your
servants let it pass.

After a while, so that we would think she was doing something, she
raised the ritual cry, and chanted barbarian songs, working her
witchcraft, as if she were really washing away the murder. When we had
been sitting a long time, it came into our minds that the strangers
might have got loose, killed her, and run for it. But we were afraid of
seeing what we were not supposed to see, and we sat still and kept
quiet. In the end we all said the same thing: go to where they were,
whether we were allowed or not.

And there we saw the hull of a Greek ship, winged with the oar-blades
fitted along her sides ready for the stroke, and fifty sailors at the
tholes with their oars in their hands, and the two young men standing
free of their chains at the stern. Some were holding the bow steady
with poles. Others were hauling the anchor up to the cat-heads. Others
were running the stern-cables through their hands and hurrying to let
down the ladders and drop them into the sea for the strangers.

We did not spare ourselves. When we saw the trick they had worked, we
grabbed the woman and the stern-cables, and we tried to pull the
steering-oars out through the sockets of that fine stern. And words
went back and forth. "What right have you to carry off images and
priestesses, stealing them out of this land? Whose son are you? Who are
you, to smuggle this woman out of the country?" And he said, "Orestes,
her brother, so you know it. The son of Agamemnon. I am taking my
sister home, the sister I lost from my house." But we held on to the
woman just the same, and were trying to force her to come with us to
you. And that is where the terrible blows to my face came from. They
had no iron in their hands, and neither did we. But there were fists,
pounding, and the two young men's legs came at us together, kicking at
our ribs and our livers, so that the moment we got to grips with them
our limbs were done for. Stamped with terrible marks, we ran for the
cliff, some with bloody wounds on their heads, some in their eyes. When
we stood on the heights we fought more carefully, and threw rocks. But
there were archers standing on the stern who kept us off with their
arrows and drove us further back.

And then a terrible swell ran the ship aground towards the land, and
the girl was afraid to wet her feet. So Orestes took her on his left
shoulder, walked into the sea, jumped onto the ladder, and set his
sister down inside that well-marked ship, with the thing that fell out
of the sky, the image of the daughter of Zeus. And from the middle of
the ship a voice shouted, "Sailors of Greece, take hold of the ship
with your oars and whiten the surf. We have what we sailed through the
unfriendly strait for, in past the Clashing Rocks." And they gave a
deep, glad groan, and struck the salt water.

The ship, while she was inside the harbour, made for the mouth. But as
she was crossing out she met a violent swell and was thrown hard,
because a terrible wind came up all at once and drove the sails back
stern-first. The men held on, kicking against the wave. But the swell,
running back, was carrying the ship towards the land again. And
Agamemnon's daughter stood up and prayed. "Daughter of Leto, bring me,
your priestess, safe to Greece out of this barbarian land, and forgive
me my thefts. You love your brother too, goddess. Believe that I love
my own blood as well." And the sailors answered the girl's prayer with
the Healer's hymn, and set their shoulders, bare out of their clothes,
to the oar at the word of command. But nearer and nearer to the rocks
the hull kept going. One man jumped into the sea, another was making
loops of rope fast.

And I was sent straight off here to you, my lord, to tell you what has
happened down there. Go. Take chains and nooses in your hands. Unless
the swell goes calm, there is no hope of safety for the strangers.
Holy Poseidon, the lord of the sea, watches over Troy, and he is the
enemy of the house of Pelops. Now, it seems, he will give the son of
Agamemnon into your hands, and the people's, and his sister with him,
the woman who forgot the slaughter at Aulis, and betrayed the goddess,
and is caught.

Chorus.
Poor Iphigenia. You will die with your brother, if you come back into
your masters' hands.

Thoas.
All you citizens of this barbarian land, go on, throw the reins on your
horses. Ride along the coast and take whatever the Greek ship throws
up. Hurry, with the goddess beside you, and hunt down these godless
men. And the rest of you, drag the fast ships down to the sea. By sea,
and by land on horseback, we will take them, and throw them down the
rough rock, or fix their bodies on stakes.

And you women, who knew about these plots, I will punish you later,
when I have the time. Now, with this business in front of us, we will
not sit still.

(Athena appears above the temple.)

Athena.
Where, where are you carrying this pursuit, lord Thoas? Listen to the
words of Athena. I am here. Stop hunting them. Stop pouring out this
flood of an army. Orestes came here because Apollo's oracles ordained
it, running from the anger of the Furies, to bring his sister home to
Argos and carry the holy image to my land, and so find a breathing
space from the sufferings he has now. That is my word to you. And
Orestes, whom you think you will catch in the sea's swell and kill:
Poseidon, as a favour to me, is already making the sea's back smooth
and waveless for his oars to cross.

And you, Orestes, learn my orders, because you can hear the goddess's
voice even where you are. Go, and take the image and your sister with
you. When you come to Athens, the city the gods built, there is a place
at the farthest borders of the Attic land, next to the ridge of
Carystus across the water, a holy place, and my people call it Halae.
Build a temple there and set the image up in it, and let it carry the
name of the Taurian land, and of the labours you toiled through,
wandering all over Greece under the Furies' goads. From now on mortals
will sing hymns to her there as Artemis Tauropolos, the goddess of the
Taurians. And lay down this law: whenever the people keep her festival,
in payment for your slaughter let a sword be held to a man's throat,
and let blood be drawn, so that holiness is satisfied and the goddess
keeps her honours.

And you, Iphigenia, must keep the keys for this goddess on the holy
steps of Brauron. There you will die, and there you will be buried, and
they will dedicate to you, as an ornament, the fine-woven robes that
women who die in childbirth leave behind in their houses. And I charge
you to send these Greek women out of this land, because their judgement
was right. I saved you once before, Orestes, on the hill of Ares, when
I judged the votes equal. And that will be the custom from now on:
whoever gets an equal count of votes wins. Now take your sister out of
this country, son of Agamemnon. And you, Thoas, do not be angry.

Thoas.
Lady Athena, whoever hears the words of the gods and does not believe
them is not thinking straight. I am not angry with Orestes for going
off with the goddess's image, or with his sister. What good is there in
wrestling with the gods, who have the strength? Let them go to your
land with the goddess's image, and let them set it up there, and good
luck to them. I will send these women too to happy Greece, as your
command lays it on me. And I will lower the spear I was raising against
the strangers, and the ships' oars, since that is how you judge it,
goddess.

Athena.
Good. Necessity rules you, and it rules the gods too. Go, winds, carry
the son of Agamemnon over the sea to Athens. I will travel with them,
and keep my sister's holy image safe.

Chorus.
	Go, and be happy in your luck,
	in the destiny that has saved you.
	Pallas Athena, holy among the immortals
	and holy among mortals,
	we will do as you command.
	For the news that has come to my ears
	is glad beyond anything I hoped.
	Great Victory, holy one,
	stay with me all my life,
	and never stop crowning me.
