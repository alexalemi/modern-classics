Andromache
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
I pitied you as I listened. Bad luck is pitiable to everyone alive, even
when it falls on a stranger. But you should have brought your daughter
and this woman to terms, Menelaus, and set her free of her troubles.

Menelaus.
Take hold of her, slaves. Twist her arms behind her. What she is about
to hear will not be to her liking. You: it was to get you off the
goddess's holy altar that I held your child's death up in front of you,
and that is how I drew you into my hands to be killed. So that is how
matters stand with you. Understand it. As for this boy, my daughter will
decide whether she wants him killed or not. Now get into the house, and
learn, slave that you were born, never to insult free people again.

Andromache.
Oh. You crept up on me with a trick. I have been cheated.

Menelaus.
Announce it to the world. I do not deny it.

Andromache.
Is this what passes for clever among you people by the Eurotas?

Menelaus.
And among the people of Troy too: when you have been hurt, you hurt
back.

Andromache.
Do you think the gods are not gods, and that there is no justice in
them?

Menelaus.
When that comes, I will bear it. But you I am going to kill.

Andromache.
And this chick too, that you have dragged out from under my wing?

Menelaus.
No, not I. I will hand him to my daughter to kill, if she likes.

Andromache.
Oh. Then why am I not mourning for you already, my child?

Menelaus.
It is true that the hope waiting for him is not a bold one.

Andromache.
You people of Sparta, most hated of all human beings by all human
beings. Council chambers of treachery. Lords of lies. Stitchers of evil
schemes. Twisted, nothing sound in you, all your thinking goes round
corners: you prosper in Greece and you do not deserve to. What is there
that is not found among you? The most murders? Money made by shameful
means? Saying one thing with your tongue and thinking another, and being
found out at it, every time? I hope you die. For me, death is not as
heavy as you have decided it should be. What destroyed me was that other
thing, the day the poor city of the Phrygians was wiped out, and my
famous husband with it, the man whose spear so often turned you from a
coward on land into a coward at sea, running for your ship. And now you
show yourself a fearsome man-at-arms against a woman, and you kill me.
Then kill me. You will get no flattery from my tongue, you or your
daughter. You were born great in Sparta; well, I was born great in Troy.
And if things are going badly for me now, do not brag about it. It could
happen to you.

Chorus.
	Never will I praise a man for having two wives,
	or sons with two different mothers:
	feuds in the house, and hatred, and grief.
	Let my husband be content with one marriage,
	one bed,
	and share it with no one.

Chorus.
	Nor is a city easier to bear
	with two kings than with one:
	burden on burden, and the citizens divided.
	And when two poets are making one song,
	the Muses love
	to set them quarrelling.

Chorus.
	When a fast wind is carrying the sailors,
	two minds at the steering oar,
	a whole crowd of clever men in a heap,
	are weaker than one plainer head with sole command.
	One man's power, in a house
	and in a city, whenever people
	want to find the right moment.

Chorus.
	The Spartan woman has shown it, the daughter
	of Menelaus the general. She went through fire
	against another woman's bed,
	and she is killing the poor woman from Troy
	and her child, for the sake of a spiteful quarrel.
	Godless, lawless, graceless, this murder.
	Lady, the tide will turn on you yet
	for what you have done.

Chorus.
	And here I see them,
	the pair yoked together, in front of the house,
	condemned to death by a vote.
	Unhappy woman, and you, poor child,
	dying for your mother's bed,
	you who had no part in it
	and did no wrong to the king's family.

Andromache.
	Here I am, my hands bleeding,
	tied tight with the cords,
	being sent down under the earth.

Child.
	Mother, mother, I am going down with you,
	under your wing.

Andromache.
	A cruel sacrifice, you rulers
	of the land of Phthia.

Child.
	Father,
	come and help the people you love.

Andromache.
	You will lie, my darling child,
	at your mother's breast,
	a corpse under the earth, beside a corpse.

Child.
	Oh, oh, what is going to happen to me? We are lost,
	you and I, mother.

Menelaus.
	Go down below the earth. You came here
	from towers that were our enemies. Two of you,
	and you die for two reasons: my vote
	takes your life, and my daughter Hermione's
	takes the boy's. It is great folly
	to leave your enemies' children alive
	when you could kill them
	and take the fear out of your house.

Andromache.
	Husband, husband, if only I had
	your hand and your spear to fight beside me,
	son of Priam.

Child.
	Poor me, what song can I find
	to turn my death aside?

Andromache.
	Beg him, my child. Go up close
	and touch the master's knees.

Child.
	Friend,
	friend, let me off my death.

Andromache.
	My eyes are running with tears,
	I drip like a sunless spring
	off a smooth rock face. Poor woman that I am.

Child.
	Oh, oh, what way out of this evil
	can I ever find?

Menelaus.
	Why fall at my feet, begging me
	as if you were praying to a rock in the sea, or a wave?
	I have been a help to my own people;
	for you I have no love-charm at all. I spent
	a great share of my life
	taking Troy, and your mother with it.
	That is your inheritance from her:
	you go down to Hades under the ground.

(Peleus comes in, leaning on a staff, with an attendant.)

Chorus.
And here I see Peleus close by, hurrying this way on his old legs.

Peleus.
I am asking you, and the man in charge of this slaughter: what is this?
How did it come to this? What is the story that has the house sick like
this? What are you doing, scheming away without a trial? Menelaus, stop.
Do not rush ahead of justice. You there, boy, lead me faster. This is no
business for taking one's time, it seems to me. If I ever wanted my
youth back, and the strength that came with it, I want it now. First of
all, then, I will blow into this woman like a following wind into a
sail. Tell me: by what right have these men tied your hands with cords
and are marching you off, you and your child? You are being led to the
slaughter like a ewe with the lamb under her, while I was away and so
was your master.

Andromache.
Old sir, these men are taking me, with my child, to be killed, just as
you see. What can I tell you? It was not one urgent message I sent for
you by, but thousands of messengers. You have heard, I expect, about the
quarrel in this house with this man's daughter, and why I am being
destroyed. And now they have dragged me from the altar of Thetis, who
bore you your noble son, whom you worship and wonder at, and they are
taking me away, with no trial to judge me, without waiting for the men
who are away from home, because they knew I was alone, and this child
too, who has done nothing, and whom they mean to kill along with me, his
wretched mother. I beg you, old man. I fall at your knees. I cannot take
hold of your dear beard with my hand. Save me, in the gods' name. If you
do not, we will die, and it will be a disgrace to your house and a
misery to me, old man.

Peleus.
I order you to loosen those ropes before someone is sorry, and free her
two hands.

Menelaus.
And I forbid it. I am a second man here, no less than you, and I have
far more authority over her.

Peleus.
How so? Have you come here to run my house? Is it not enough for you to
rule what is in Sparta?

Menelaus.
I took her prisoner out of Troy.

Peleus.
And my son's son received her as his prize.

Menelaus.
Is not what I own his, and what he owns mine?

Peleus.
Yes. To do good with, not harm. Not to kill by force.

Menelaus.
You will never take her out of my hands.

Peleus.
Shall I bloody your head with this staff?

Menelaus.
Touch me and find out. Come one step closer.

Peleus.
You, count as one of the men? You worst of men, from a family of the
worst. What claim have you to a place in the company of men? A man who
let a Phrygian take his wife off him. You left your house and your
hearth unlocked and unguarded, as if the woman you had at home were a
chaste one, when she was the worst of them all. A Spartan girl could not
keep herself in hand if she wanted to. They leave home with the young
men, their thighs bare and their dresses hanging loose, and they share
the running tracks and the wrestling grounds with them, which I cannot
stomach. And then is it any wonder that you do not bring up your women
to keep themselves in hand? Helen was the one to ask that question, the
woman who walked out of your house, and out from under the Zeus who
guards the love between husband and wife, and went carousing off with a
young man to another country. And then, for her sake, you collected that
enormous crowd of Greeks and led them to Troy? You should have spat her
out and never lifted a spear, once you found out what she was; you
should have let her stay where she was, paid a price for the privilege,
and never taken her back into your house. But that was not the way your
mind's wind was blowing. Instead you destroyed many good lives, and left
old women childless in their homes, and robbed grey-haired fathers of
their noble sons. I am one of those wretched fathers. And I look at you
as the killer of Achilles, a man polluted with his blood. You alone came
home from Troy without a wound, and brought your fine armour back in its
fine cases just the way it went out. And I told the boy when he was
marrying: do not make a marriage tie with this man, do not take into
your house the filly of a bad mother. They carry their mothers' shame
out of the house with them. You young men who are looking for wives,
mark this for me: get a daughter from a good mother. And besides all
that, what an outrage you did to your own brother, when you told him to
cut his daughter's throat, and he was fool enough to do it. That is how
afraid you were of being left without your worthless wife. And when you
took Troy, since I will follow you there too, you did not kill the woman
when you had her in your hands. No: the moment you saw her breast, you
threw your sword away and took her kiss, and fawned on the bitch who had
betrayed you, because Aphrodite is stronger than you are, you worst of
men. And then you come to my children's house while they are away and
ransack it, and kill a poor unlucky woman without honour, and a child
who will one day make you weep, you and that daughter of yours in the
house, even if he were three times a bastard. Dry ground has beaten deep
soil for a crop many a time, and many a bastard is a better man than the
legitimate sons. Take your daughter and go. It is better for a man to
have a poor honest man for a son-in-law and a friend than a rich bad
one. And you are nothing.

Chorus.
From a small beginning the tongue supplies people with a great quarrel.
Sensible people take care about that: they do not start a fight with the
people they owe.

Menelaus.
Then why would anyone call old men wise, or the men the Greeks once
thought had sense? When you, Peleus, born of a famous father and tied to
me by marriage, say things that shame you and insult me, and all over a
barbarian woman. You should have driven her out beyond the streams of
the Nile, beyond the river Phasis, and cheered me on the whole way. She
is a woman from the mainland of Asia, where more Greek dead fell to the
spear than anywhere else, and she had a share in your own son's blood.
Paris, who killed your son Achilles, was Hector's brother, and this
woman was Hector's wife. And you go in under the same roof with her, and
think it fit to eat at the same table with her, and let her bear
children in the house who will be your worst enemies. I saw all this
coming, old man, for your sake and for mine, and when I try to kill her
she is snatched out of my hands. Come now. There is no shame in laying
hands on the argument itself. If my daughter has no child and this
woman's children grow up, will you set them up as kings of this land of
Phthia, barbarians by birth, ruling Greeks? And then I am the one with
no sense, because I hate injustice, and you are the one with brains?
Consider this too. If you had given your daughter to some citizen and
she had then been treated like this, would you have sat there in
silence? I doubt it. Yet for the sake of a foreign woman you scream this
at the family you are bound to. And another thing: a wife who is wronged
by her husband grieves as much as a man does, and so, just the same,
does a man with a wife playing the fool in his house. But he has great
strength in his own hands. Her affairs rest with her parents and the
people who owe her. Is it not right, then, that I should help my own?
You are old, old. As for my generalship, you would help me more by
talking about it than by keeping quiet. Helen's trouble was not her own
choice; it came from the gods, and it did Greece the greatest good. They
knew nothing of arms and battle, and they went on to manly deeds.
Practice is the teacher of everything for human beings. And when I came
face to face with my wife and held back from killing her, that was
keeping myself in hand. I wish you had not wanted to kill your brother
Phocus either. I have gone at you with all this in good sense, not out
of anger. If you fly into a temper, you get a sore tongue from all your
talking, and I get the profit of having looked ahead.

Chorus.
Stop now, both of you. That is far the best thing: stop these useless
words, before the two of you go wrong together.

Peleus.
Oh, what a bad custom Greece has. When the army sets up a trophy over
the enemy, people do not count it the work of the men who did the
labour. The general carries off the credit. He was one man among ten
thousand shaking a spear, he did no more than any one of them, and he
gets more of the glory. And they sit in high office in the city, very
solemn, and think themselves greater than the people, and they are
nobodies. The people are ten thousand times cleverer than they are, if
they had the daring and the will at the same time. That is how you and
your brother sit puffed up over Troy and the generalship you had there,
lifted up by other men's labour and other men's pain. I will teach you
to think Paris of Ida a smaller enemy than Peleus, unless you take
yourself off from under this roof as fast as you can, you and your
childless daughter, whom the son of my blood will drive through this
house by the hair. She is a barren heifer and she will not put up with
other women bearing children when she has none of her own. But if she is
unlucky about children, must we be left with none at all? Get away from
her, slaves, so I can find out whether anyone is going to stop me
untying her hands. Lift yourself up. I am shaking, but I will undo these
plaited leather knots. Is this how you tore up her hands, you worst of
men? Did you think you were tying up a bull or a lion? Or were you
afraid she would grab a sword and fight you off? Come here, little one,
under my arm. Help me untie your mother. In Phthia I will raise you to
be a great enemy to these people. If the Spartans had not their
reputation for the spear and the contest of battle, in everything else,
be sure of it, you are no better than anyone.

Chorus.
Old men are a thing let off the leash, and hard to hold back when their
temper is up.

Menelaus.
You are far too quick to rush into abuse. As for me, I came to Phthia,
and I am not going to do anything shabby here by force, or have it done
to me. And now, since I have no spare time to waste, I am going home.
There is a city not far from Sparta which used to be friendly and is now
acting as an enemy. I want to march on it with an army and bring it
under my hand. When I have settled things there the way I want, I will
come back. Then, face to face with my son-in-law, I will make my case
and hear his. If he punishes this woman and keeps himself in hand
towards us from now on, he will find us keeping ourselves in hand. If he
loses his temper, he will find us losing ours, and deed will answer
deed. Your speeches I can bear easily. You stand there like a shadow
with a voice. You cannot do anything; all you can do is talk.

(Menelaus goes out with his men.)

Peleus.
Lead the way, my child. Stand here under my arm. And you too, poor
woman. You ran into a savage storm, and you have come into a harbour out
of the wind.

Andromache.
Old sir, may the gods be good to you and yours, for saving my child and
me, the unluckiest of women. But watch out that these men do not crouch
in wait for the two of us on a lonely stretch of road and take me by
force, seeing that you are old and I am weak and this child is a baby.
Think about that, in case we escape now and are caught later.

Peleus.
Do not bring me a woman's cowardly talk. Walk. Who is going to lay a
hand on you? He will be sorry if he touches you. Thanks to the gods, I
command a crowd of horsemen and many armed men in Phthia. And I still
stand straight. I am not the old man you think. A man like that, I need
only look at him and I will set up a trophy over him, old as I am. One
old man with a good heart is stronger than a lot of young ones. What use
is a fine body to a coward?

(Peleus goes out, with Andromache and the child.)

Chorus.
	Let me not be born, or let me be born
	of good fathers, and share a house with plenty in it.
	If anything comes that cannot be mended,
	the well-born never lack defenders;
	when a man is proclaimed the son of a noble house,
	there is honour and glory in it. Time
	does not wear away what good men leave behind.
	Their excellence shines on when they are dead.

Chorus.
	Better a victory nobody thinks badly of
	than to trip up justice with power and spite.
	It tastes sweet at the time, to human beings,
	but in time it goes dry
	and hangs on the house as a reproach.
	This is the life I praise, this is the life I hold to:
	to have no power beyond what is right,
	in the marriage bed or in the city.

Chorus.
	Old son of Aeacus,
	I believe it: you stood with the Lapiths
	against the Centaurs with your spear,
	the most famous spear of all; and on the Argo
	you passed the unfriendly water of the Clashing Rocks
	on that famous voyage;
	and when, long ago, the son of Zeus, Heracles,
	threw a ring of slaughter round the glorious city of Troy,
	you came home to Europe
	with an equal share of the glory.

(The Nurse comes out of the house.)

Nurse.
Dear women, what a train of evils this day is laying out for us, one on
the heels of another. My mistress inside the house, Hermione I mean,
deserted by her father and realising at the same time what she has done,
planning to kill Andromache and her child, wants to die. She is
terrified of her husband: that for what she has done he will send her
out of this house in disgrace, or kill her, for trying to kill people it
was wrong to kill. The slaves standing guard over her can barely stop
her hanging herself, and they snatch the swords out of her right hand
and take them off her. That is how sorry she is now, and she knows that
what she did before was not well done. I am worn out, friends, keeping
my mistress from the noose. You go in, into the house, and get her out
of death. New faces persuade better than the friends people are used to.

Chorus.
Yes, and we can hear the servants shouting in the house, about the very
thing you came to tell us. The poor woman looks about to show how
bitterly she grieves for the terrible things she did. She is coming out
of the house, out of the servants' hands, wanting to die.

(Hermione comes out, tearing at her hair and her veil.)

Hermione.
	Io moi moi.
	I will tear my hair out in handfuls
	and rake my nails through my face.

Nurse.
	Child, what are you doing? Are you going to disfigure your own body?

Hermione.
	Aiai, aiai.
	Off my hair with you, up into the air,
	fine-woven veil.

Nurse.
	Child, cover your breasts. Fasten your dress.

Hermione.
	Why should I cover
	my breasts with a dress? What I did
	to my husband is plain, and in the open,
	and cannot be hidden.

Nurse.
	Does it hurt, that you stitched up a murder for the woman who shares your husband?

Hermione.
	Yes, I groan for it,
	for the cruel thing I dared to do.
	I am cursed. Cursed
	in the eyes of every human being.

Nurse.
	Your husband will forgive you this mistake.

Hermione.
	Why did you hunt the sword
	out of my hand?
	Give it back, dear, give it back, so I can drive
	a blow straight home. Why do you keep me from the rope?

Nurse.
	And if I let you go, out of your mind as you are, so that you could die?

Hermione.
	Oh, my fate.
	Where is a fire I could love, a flame for me?
	Where can I climb, up onto the rocks,
	over the sea, or into the woods on the mountains,
	so that I can die and belong to the dead below?
