Medea
(Part 1 of 3)

(Corinth, in front of the house where Jason and Medea have lived. The
Nurse comes out alone.)

Nurse.
If only the Argo had never flown through the dark Clashing Rocks to the
land of Colchis. If only the pine had never been cut down in the glens
of Pelion and fallen, to put oars in the hands of the best men alive,
the ones who went to fetch the golden fleece for Pelias. Then my
mistress Medea would never have sailed to the towers of Iolcus, her
heart knocked flat by love for Jason. She would never have talked the
daughters of Pelias into killing their father, and she would not be
living here in Corinth with her husband and her children. And she was
welcome here. The citizens of the country she came to as an exile liked
her, and she herself went along with Jason in everything, which is the
surest safety there is: a wife who does not stand apart from her
husband. But now it is all hatred, and what was dearest is sick. Jason
has betrayed his own children and my mistress, and he sleeps in a royal
marriage. He has married the daughter of Creon, who rules this land. And
Medea, poor woman, cast off and dishonoured, shouts about the oaths he
swore. She calls up the right hand he gave her, the greatest pledge
there is, and she calls the gods to witness what kind of return she is
getting from Jason.

She lies there without eating, her body given up to pain, melting away
the whole of her time in tears, ever since she found out her husband had
wronged her. She does not lift her eyes; she does not raise her face off
the ground. When her friends try to talk sense into her she listens the
way a rock does, or a wave of the sea. Except that now and then she
turns that white neck of hers away and cries to herself for her dear
father, and her country, and the home she betrayed to come away with a
man who has now thrown her aside. Now she knows, poor thing, from what
has happened to her, what it is worth never to leave your own country.
She hates the children. She takes no pleasure in seeing them. And I am
afraid she is planning something new. She has a heavy mind, and she will
not put up with being treated badly. I know her. I am frightened she
will go quietly into the room where the bed is made and drive a
sharpened sword through the liver, or kill the princess and the man who
married her too, and then bring some greater disaster down on herself.
She is dangerous. Nobody who picks a quarrel with that woman is going to
come out of it singing the victory song easily.

But here come the children, finished with their running about. They have
no idea of their mother's troubles. A young mind does not like to hurt.

(The Tutor comes in with the two boys.)

Tutor.
Old servant of my mistress's house, why are you standing out here at the
gate by yourself, groaning your troubles to yourself? How does Medea
come to want to be left alone without you?

Nurse.
Old man, you who walk behind Jason's children: when a master's affairs
go wrong, that is a misfortune for good slaves as well. It takes hold of
their hearts. I have come to such a pitch of grief that a longing came
over me to come out here and tell the earth and the sky what has
happened to my mistress.

Tutor.
Has the poor woman not stopped crying yet?

Nurse.
I envy you. The trouble is at its beginning. It is nowhere near the
middle.

Tutor.
The fool. If I may say that about a master. She knows nothing yet about
the newer troubles.

Nurse.
What is it, old man? Do not begrudge me the telling.

Tutor.
Nothing. I take back even what I said already.

Nurse.
No. By your beard, do not hide it from a fellow slave. I will keep quiet
about it, if I have to.

Tutor.
I heard someone talking, and pretended not to listen, as I came up to
the draughts players, where the oldest men sit, by the holy water of
Pirene. He said that Creon, the ruler of this land, is going to drive
these children out of Corinth, and their mother with them. Whether the
story is true I do not know. I would like it not to be.

Nurse.
And Jason will put up with his sons being treated like that, even if he
has quarrelled with their mother?

Tutor.
Old ties lose out to new ones. That man is no friend to this house.

Nurse.
Then we are finished, if we add a new trouble to the old one before we
have bailed out the first.

Tutor.
As for you, keep quiet and say nothing about it. This is not the moment
for the mistress to know.

Nurse.
Children, do you hear what kind of father you have? Curse him. No. He is
my master. But he is caught being a bad man to the people he owes.

Tutor.
Who is not? Are you only now learning that every man loves himself more
than his neighbour, some of them honestly and some for gain, when their
father does not love these children, all for the sake of a bed?

Nurse.
Go inside the house, children. It will be all right. And you, keep them
out of the way as much as you can. Do not bring them near their mother
while she is in this black mood. I have already seen her glaring at them
like a bull, as if she wanted to do something. She will not stop this
rage, I know it, until she strikes somebody down. Let it be enemies she
does something to, and not the people she loves.

(Medea's voice comes from inside the house.)

Medea.
	Oh.
	Unhappy, miserable, worn down with pain,
	oh, oh, how can I die?

Nurse.
	That is it, dear children. Your mother
	is stirring her heart, stirring her anger.
	Hurry into the house, quickly,
	and keep out of range of her eye,
	do not go near her. Watch out
	for the wild temper, the hateful nature
	of that stubborn mind.
	Go now, get inside as fast as you can.
	It is plain enough: the cloud of grief
	that is lifting off the ground will soon catch fire
	with a greater rage. What will she do,
	that great-hearted, hard-to-stop
	soul, bitten by her wrongs?

(The Tutor takes the children into the house.)

Medea.
	Aiai.
	I have suffered, suffered things
	worth loud crying. Accursed
	children of a hateful mother, die,
	you and your father, and let the whole house go to ruin.

Nurse.
	Oh, oh, poor woman.
	Why should your children share
	in their father's crime? Why hate them? Oh,
	children, I am sick with fear that something will happen to you.
	The tempers of kings are terrible. Somehow,
	taking few orders and giving many,
	they find it hard to shift their anger.
	Being used to living among equals
	is better. For my part, let me grow old
	in no great state, but safe.
	The middle way: first, its very name
	wins, and to live by it is far
	the best thing for mortals. Too much
	does nobody any good in the end,
	and when a god is angry with a house
	it pays back greater ruin.

(The Chorus, women of Corinth, come in.)

Chorus.
	I heard the voice, I heard the cry
	of the unhappy woman from Colchis. Is she still
	not calm? Old woman, tell me.
	At the double doors, from inside the house, I heard a cry,
	and I take no pleasure, woman, in the pains
	of this house. It has become dear to me.

Nurse.
	There is no house. It is gone already.
	A royal bed has him,
	and she is in her room wasting her life away,
	my mistress, and not one friend's word
	brings any warmth to her heart.

Medea.
	Aiai. Zeus, and Earth, and Light:
	let fire from heaven come down
	through my head. What good is living to me now?
	Ah, ah. Let me be done with it in death
	and leave a life I hate.

Chorus.
	Did you hear, Zeus and earth and light,
	what a cry that unhappy
	bride is singing?
	What is this longing of yours
	for the bed no one can go near, you fool?
	Death's ending will hurry on by itself.
	Do not pray for it.
	If your husband
	bows down to a new bed,
	that is his affair. Do not tear yourself over it.
	Zeus will plead your case for you. Do not
	waste yourself away crying over the man who shared your bed.

Medea.
	Great Themis, and lady Artemis,
	do you see what I suffer, I who bound
	that accursed husband
	with great oaths? One day let me see
	him and his bride ground to nothing, house and all,
	for daring to wrong me first.
	Father. City. I left you in shame,
	and killed my brother.

Nurse.
	Do you hear what she says, how she calls
	on Themis, who hears prayers, and on Zeus,
	whom men count the keeper of oaths?
	There is no way my mistress will lay down
	this anger over anything small.

Chorus.
	How could she come out
	where we can see her, and take in
	the sound of the words we would say?
	If only she would let go
	of the heavy anger and the stubbornness of her mind.
	Let my goodwill, at least,
	never fail the people I care for.
	Go and bring her
	out here, out of the house.
	Tell her we are her friends too.
	Hurry, before she does harm to the ones inside.
	This grief of hers is gathering speed.

Nurse.
	I will. But I am afraid I will not
	persuade my mistress.
	Still, I will give you the favour of the effort.
	And yet she glares at her slaves
	like a lioness with cubs, whenever one of us
	comes near her to say a word.
	You would not be wrong to call the men of old
	clumsy, and not clever at all,
	who invented songs for festivals,
	for banquets and for dinners,
	pleasant sounds for life to listen to,
	but nobody has found how to stop
	the hateful griefs of mortals with music
	and many-stringed song, the griefs
	that bring deaths and terrible turns of fortune down on houses.
	And yet it would be a gain if people
	could heal those with singing. Where the dinner is good
	and the tables full, why raise a useless noise?
	The full table in front of them
	has pleasure enough in itself for mortals.

(The Nurse goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	I heard the wail, heavy with groans,
	the shrill and bitter cry she raises
	against the traitor in her bed, the man whose marriage was a crime.
	Wronged, she calls on the gods,
	on Themis, who keeps oaths for Zeus,
	who brought her across
	to Greece on the far shore,
	through the salt sea at night, through the
	narrow lock of the endless deep.

(Medea comes out of the house.)

Medea.
Women of Corinth, I have come out of the house so that you will not
blame me. I know many people get a name for being haughty, some because
they keep out of sight, and some in public; and others, because they go
quietly, are called lazy. There is no justice in people's eyes. Before
they have really learned what a man is inside, they hate him on sight,
though he has done them no wrong. A foreigner especially must fit in
with the city. And I have no praise for the citizen who is stubborn,
either, and makes himself bitter to his fellow citizens out of
ignorance.

But this thing that fell on me out of nowhere has destroyed my soul. I
am finished. I have let go of any pleasure in living, and I want to die,
my friends. The man who was everything to me, as he knows very well, has
turned out to be the worst man alive: my husband.

Of all creatures that live and think, we women are the most wretched.
First we have to buy a husband, at an enormous price, and take a master
over our bodies, which is a worse evil on top of the first. And that is
the greatest contest of all: getting a bad one or a good one. Divorce
brings a woman no honour, and she cannot refuse a husband. She comes
into new habits and new rules, and she has to be a prophet, since nobody
taught her at home, to work out how best to handle the man she sleeps
with. And if we work all this out well, and the husband lives with us
and does not fight the yoke, our life is one to envy. If not, better to
die. A man, when he is sick of the company indoors, goes out and clears
his heart of its disgust, turning to some friend or someone his own age.
We have to look to one soul alone. And they say we live a life without
danger, at home, while they fight with the spear. What fools. I would
rather stand behind a shield three times than give birth once.

But the same argument does not fit you and me. You have this city, and
your fathers' houses, and the good things of life, and friends to be
with. I am alone. I have no city, and my husband treats me with outrage,
a woman carried off as loot from a barbarian country, with no mother, no
brother, not one relative I can put in to shelter from this storm. So
this much, and only this, I want from you. If I can find some way, some
device, to pay my husband back for these wrongs, and the man who gave
him his daughter, and the woman he married, keep silent. A woman is full
of fear about everything else, a coward when it comes to a fight, and
she cannot look at steel. But when she is wronged in her bed, there is
no mind more murderous.

Chorus.
I will. You will be right to make your husband pay, Medea. I do not
wonder that you grieve at what has happened to you. But I see Creon, the
king of this land, coming this way, with news of some new decision.

(Creon comes in with attendants.)

Creon.
You, with the scowl and the fury at your husband. Medea. I order you out
of this country, into exile, and take your two children with you. No
delay. I am the judge of this sentence, and I am not going back into my
house until I have thrown you outside the borders of the land.

Medea.
Aiai. I am destroyed, poor woman, root and branch. My enemies are
letting out every rope of sail, and there is no easy landing from this
ruin. But I will ask, even in this state. Why are you sending me out of
the country, Creon?

Creon.
I am afraid of you. No need to dress it up in words. I am afraid you
will do my daughter some harm that cannot be undone. A great many things
add up to that fear. You were born clever, and you know a great deal
about doing harm, and you are in pain, cheated of your husband's bed.
And I hear you are threatening, so people tell me, to do something to
the father who gave the bride, and the bridegroom, and the bride. So I
am going to guard against it before it happens. Better for me to have
you hate me now, woman, than to go soft and groan about it afterwards.

Medea.
Ah, ah. This is not the first time, Creon. My reputation has hurt me
many times and done me a great deal of harm. No man with any sense
should ever have his children taught to be too clever. Apart from the
name for idleness that they get, they earn the spite and ill will of
their fellow citizens. If you bring new and clever ideas to fools, you
will look useless, and not clever at all; and if the people who think
they know something subtle decide you are better than they are, the city
will find you painful to have around. I share this luck myself. Because
I am clever, some people envy me, some think me withdrawn, some think
the opposite, and some find me difficult. And I am not so very clever.

Anyway, you are afraid of me. Afraid I will do you some injury? I am not
in a position, so do not tremble at me, Creon, to go committing offences
against kings. What have you done to wrong me? You gave your daughter to
the man your heart chose. It is my husband I hate. You, I think, acted
sensibly. And now I do not begrudge you your good fortune. Make your
marriage, and good luck to you all. But let me live in this country. I
have been wronged, and I will keep quiet about it. I have been beaten by
people stronger than me.

Creon.
Soft words to listen to. But inside I am terrified you are planning
something bad, and I trust you that much less than before. A
quick-tempered woman, and a man too, for that matter, is easier to guard
against than a clever one who keeps quiet. So get out, as fast as you
can, and no speeches. It is settled, and you have no trick that will
keep you here among us when you are my enemy.

Medea.
No. By your knees, by your newly married daughter.

Creon.
You are wasting words. You will never persuade me.

Medea.
You will drive me out, and pay no respect to my prayers?

Creon.
I do not love you more than my own house.

Medea.
My country. How I think of you now.

Creon.
Yes. After my children, it is what I love most, by far.

Medea.
Ah, ah. What a great evil love is for mortals.

Creon.
That depends, I imagine, on how things fall out.

Medea.
Zeus, do not let the man who caused this escape you.

Creon.
Go, you fool, and rid me of this trouble.

Medea.
The trouble is mine. I have no shortage of it.

Creon.
In a moment my servants' hands will push you out by force.

Medea.
Not that, not that. I am asking you, Creon...

Creon.
You are going to make a scene, it seems, woman.

Medea.
I will go into exile. That is not what I begged you for.

Creon.
Then why are you forcing me? Why will you not let go of my hand?

Medea.
Let me stay this one day, and finish thinking out how we will go into
exile, and find some start in life for my children, since their father
does not care to arrange anything for his sons. Have pity on them. You
are the father of children yourself. It is natural that you should feel
kindly. I have no thought for myself, if we are exiled. It is them I
weep for, meeting such a disaster.

Creon.
There is nothing of the tyrant in my nature, and I have ruined a great
deal by feeling sorry for people. And now I can see I am making a
mistake, woman, but all the same you will have what you ask. But I warn
you: if tomorrow's sun, the god's torch, sees you and your children
inside the borders of this land, you die. That word is spoken and it
will not lie. So now, if you have to stay, stay for one day. You will
not do any of the terrible things I am afraid of in that time.

(Creon goes out with his attendants.)

Chorus.
	Unhappy woman,
	ah, ah, miserable in your sorrows.
	Where will you turn? To what stranger's house?
	What home, what country will you find
	to save you from these evils?
	Into what a hopeless sea of troubles, Medea,
	a god has carried you.

Medea.
It has gone badly in every direction. Who will deny it? But it is not
going to end this way. Do not think so yet. There are still trials ahead
for the newlyweds, and for the ones who made the match, no small pains.
Do you think I would ever have fawned on that man if I were not getting
something out of it, or working some scheme? I would not have spoken to
him. I would not have touched him with my hands. But he has gone so far
into folly that when he could have wrecked my plans by throwing me out
of the country, he has let me stay this one day, and in it I will make
corpses of three of my enemies: the father, the girl, and my husband.

I have many roads to their deaths, and I do not know which to take
first, my friends. Shall I set fire to the bridal house? Or go quietly
into the room where the bed is made and drive a sharpened sword through
the liver? One thing stands in my way. If I am caught going into the
house and working my scheme, I will die, and give my enemies a laugh.
Best to take the straight road, where we women are cleverest by nature:
take them with poison.

Very well. Say they are dead. What city will take me in? What host will
offer me a land of refuge and a house that stands good for me, and save
my life? There is none. So I will wait a little while longer, and if
some safe tower shows itself to me, I will go after this murder by
stealth and in silence. But if hopeless circumstance drives me out, I
will take the sword in my own hand, even if I am going to die for it,
and kill them, and go the hard way of daring. For by the mistress I
honour above all others and have chosen as my partner in the work,
Hecate, who lives in the inmost corner of my hearth, not one of them is
going to hurt my heart and enjoy it. Bitter and grievous I will make
their marriage, bitter their match-making, bitter my exile from the
land.

Come on, then. Spare nothing of what you know, Medea, plotting and
scheming. Go to the terrible thing. Now is the trial of courage. You see
what is being done to you. You are not to be laughed at over Jason's
marriage into the house of Sisyphus, you, the daughter of a noble
father, born from the Sun. You have the knowledge. And besides, we were
born women: helpless when it comes to doing good, but of every kind of
evil the cleverest builders.

Chorus.
	The springs of the holy rivers are running uphill.
	Justice and everything else are turned round backwards.
	It is men whose plans are treacherous, men
	whose oaths by the gods no longer hold.
	And the talk will turn, and my life will have its good name.
	Honour is coming to the race of women.
	No more will ugly rumour have women in its grip.

Chorus.
	The Muses of the poets of old will stop
	singing of my faithlessness.
	Apollo, lord of song, did not put
	the lyre's inspired music into our minds,
	or I would have sung a hymn back
	at the race of men. The long years have
	much to say about our lot, and about men's too.

Chorus.
	You sailed from your father's house
	with a mind gone mad, and passed between the twin
	rocks of the sea. Now in a strange
	land you live, the bed you slept in
	lost, no husband in it,
	poor woman, and driven out of the country
	in dishonour, an exile.

Chorus.
	The grace of oaths is gone. Shame
	no longer stays in great Greece. It has flown up into the sky.
	And you have no father's house,
	poor woman, to shelter in
	from your troubles, and another queen,
	stronger than you in your own bed,
	has taken over the house.

(Jason comes in.)

Jason.
This is not the first time I have seen it. I have seen it often: a harsh
temper is an evil nothing can be done about. You could have had this
country and this house, if you had taken your betters' decisions
lightly, and instead, for the sake of foolish talk, you are being thrown
out of the country. It makes no difference to me. Never stop calling
Jason the worst man alive, if you like. But for what you have said
against the royal family, count it clear gain that exile is your only
punishment. I kept trying to take the edge off the king's anger, all the
time, and I wanted you to stay. But you would not let go of your folly,
always speaking badly of the royal family, and so you are being thrown
out. Even so, even after all this, I have not given up on the people I
owe. I have come here looking after your interests, woman, so that you
will not be thrown out with the children penniless, or short of
anything. Exile drags a great many evils along with it. And even if you
hate me, I could never wish you harm.

Medea.
You utter, utter coward. That is the worst thing my tongue can call you:
that you are no man. You have come to me? You come here, when you have
made yourself the worst enemy of the gods, and of me, and of the whole
human race? This is not courage. It is not daring, to wrong the people
you owe and then look them in the face. It is the worst of all the
sicknesses that live in human beings: shamelessness. But you did well to
come. I will lighten my heart by telling you exactly how bad you are,
and you will hurt, listening.

I will start at the beginning. I saved your life. Every Greek who
boarded the Argo with you knows it. You had been sent to yoke the
fire-breathing bulls and to sow the field of death. And the serpent that
coiled round the golden fleece and guarded it, sleepless, in its many
folds: I killed it, and held up the light that saved you. I betrayed my
own father and my own house and came with you to Iolcus under Pelion,
more eager than clever. And I killed Pelias, in the most painful way
there is to die, at the hands of his own children, and I wiped out his
whole house. That is what I did for you, and you, worst of men, betrayed
me and took a new bed, when we had children. If you had still been
childless, I could have forgiven you for wanting this marriage.

The trust in oaths is gone. I cannot make out whether you think the gods
of those days no longer rule, or that new rules have been laid down for
human beings now, because you know perfectly well you have not kept your
oath to me. Ah, this right hand, which you took hold of so often, and
these knees. How uselessly we were touched by a bad man, and how we
missed what we hoped for.

Come, then. I will talk to you as if you were a friend, though what good
do I expect to get from you? Still, I will do it. The questions will
show you up as even more shameful. Where am I to turn now? To my
father's house, which I betrayed for you, along with my country, to come
here? To the poor daughters of Pelias? They would give me a fine welcome
into their house, when I killed their father. That is how it stands. To
the ones I loved at home I have become an enemy; and the ones I had no
reason to harm, I have made my enemies, to do you a favour. And in
return for all that, you have made me so blessed in the eyes of many a
Greek woman. What a wonderful husband I have in you, and how faithful,
poor me, if I am thrown out of the country and go into exile, without
one friend, alone with my children, alone. A fine reproach to the new
bridegroom: his children wandering as beggars, and the woman who saved
his life. Zeus, why did you give people clear tests for gold, to tell
the counterfeit from the true, when there is no mark stamped on a man's
body to tell the bad one by?
