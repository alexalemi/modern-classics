Heracles
(Part 3 of 3)

Chorus.
	The murder that the rock of Argos remembers,
	the daughters of Danaus killing their husbands in one night,
	was once the most famous crime in Greece, the hardest to believe.
	This has gone past it. It has outrun
	those old horrors, and it fell on the wretched son of Zeus.
	I could tell of Procne, who had one child
	and killed him, and made a sacrifice of him to the Muses.
	But you, you butcher, fathered three,
	and a raging fate worked beside you and finished them all.
	Aiai. What groan, what wail,
	what song for the dead,
	what dance of Hades am I to sound?
	Pheu, pheu.
	Look. The bolts are drawn, the doors are parting,
	the doors of the high-gated house.
	Io moi.
	Look at the children lying in front of their father,
	pitiful, in front of that wretched man,
	who sleeps a terrible sleep after murdering his sons.
	And round him the bonds, the ropes with all their knots,
	propping up the body of Heracles,
	lashed to the stone pillars of the house.

(The doors of the house stand open. Heracles lies asleep, roped to a
broken pillar, with the bodies of his wife and children round him.
Amphitryon comes slowly towards him.)

Chorus.
And here is the old man, like a bird crying over a nest of young that
never flew. He comes after his son on slow old feet, down a bitter
road. Here he is.

Amphitryon.
	Old men of Thebes, quiet. Quiet.
	Will you not let him lie there, gone limp with sleep,
	and forget his evils?

Chorus.
	I weep for you, old man, and I groan,
	and for the children, and for that head that won every victory.

Amphitryon.
	Go further off. Do not
	stamp, do not shout, do not
	wake him. He is sleeping easy,
	sunk deep in sleep, on his bed.

Chorus.
	Oh.
	All this blood, that he...

Amphitryon.
	Ah. Ah.
	You will be the death of me.

Chorus.
	...spilled, is rising up against him.

Amphitryon.
	Can you not keep your dirge quiet,
	old men, when you cry Aiai?
	Or he will wake and burst his bonds and destroy the city,
	and tear his father apart, and bring the house down.

Chorus.
	I cannot. I cannot.

Amphitryon.
	Quiet. Let me learn how he is breathing.
	Here. Let me put my ear to him.

Chorus.
	Is he asleep?

Amphitryon.
	Yes. He is asleep,
	a sleep that is no sleep, a deadly sleep, the man who killed his wife,
	who killed his children, shot them down to the twang of his bow.

Chorus.
	Groan, then...

Amphitryon.
	I am groaning.

Chorus.
	...for the children's destruction...

Amphitryon.
	Omoi.

Chorus.
	...and for your son's.

Amphitryon.
	Aiai.

Chorus.
	Old man...

Amphitryon.
	Quiet. Quiet.
	He is turning over, he is waking, he is turning round. Here,
	let me hide myself out of sight, under the roof.

Chorus.
	Courage. Night still holds your son's eyes.

Amphitryon.
	Look. Look. To leave the light
	after evils like these, I do not
	shrink from it, wretched as I am. But if he kills me, his father,
	he will pile evil on evil,
	and on top of the Furies he has already, he will carry a father's blood.

Chorus.
	You should have died back then, when you went out
	to take payment for your wife's murdered brothers
	and sacked the Taphians' city, the city ringed by the sea.

Amphitryon.
	Run, run, old men, away from the house,
	get away. Run from a raving
	man as he wakes.
	Or soon he will throw fresh murder on the old murder
	and run wild through the city of the Thebans again.

Chorus.
Zeus, why did you hate your own son so much, with such rage? Why did
you lead him into this sea of evils?

Heracles.
Ah. I am breathing. And I can see what I ought to see: the sky, and the
earth, and these arrows of the Sun. But I have fallen into some kind of
surge, some terrible churning of the mind, and the breath I breathe
comes up hot from my lungs, in gasps, and will not hold steady. Look at
this. Why am I sitting here, moored with ropes like a ship, my strong
young chest and my arm tied fast to a half-smashed block of cut stone,
with corpses for neighbours? And here are winged shafts, and my bow,
scattered over the floor, the ones that used to stand beside my arms
like shield-bearers, and kept my ribs safe, and were kept safe by me.
Surely I have not gone down to Hades again? Run Eurystheus's double
course a second time? To Hades? How? No. I do not see the rock of
Sisyphus, or Pluto, or the sceptre of Persephone, Demeter's daughter. I
am stunned. Where on earth am I, so helpless? Hey. Is any of my friends
near, or far off, who can cure me of this not knowing? Because I know
nothing clearly. Nothing of the things I used to know.

Amphitryon.
Old men, shall I go closer to my own evils?

Chorus.
And I will go with you. I will not desert you in your trouble.

Heracles.
Father, why are you crying, and covering your eyes, standing so far
away from the son you love best?

Amphitryon.
My child. You are mine, even when things go badly for you.

Heracles.
And what is going badly for me, that you are pouring out tears?

Amphitryon.
Something even a god would groan over, if he learned of it.

Heracles.
Big words. But you still have not said what has happened.

Amphitryon.
You can see it for yourself, if you are in your right mind by now.

Heracles.
Speak, if you are sketching some new turn in my life.

Amphitryon.
If you have stopped raving, like one of Hades' own madmen, I will tell
you.

Heracles.
Papai. There it is again, that riddle. It makes me suspicious.

Amphitryon.
And I am still watching to see whether you are steadily in your senses.

Heracles.
I do not remember going out of my mind, if that is what you mean.

Amphitryon.
Old men, shall I untie my son's bonds? Or what should I do?

Heracles.
Yes, and tell me who tied me. It shames me.

Amphitryon.
Know this much of your evils, and let the rest go.

Heracles.
Is silence enough to teach me what I want to know?

Amphitryon.
Zeus, do you see this, done from Hera's throne?

Heracles.
Has something hostile come at us from there?

Amphitryon.
Leave the goddess alone and look after your own evils.

Heracles.
Then I am finished. You are going to tell me of some disaster.

Amphitryon.
Look. Look at these. Your children, fallen.

Heracles.
Oh. What sight is this I am looking at? What is this?

Amphitryon.
My boy, you rushed into a war on your children that was no war.

Heracles.
War? What do you mean, war? Who killed these?

Amphitryon.
You, and your bow, and whichever of the gods is to blame.

Heracles.
What are you saying? What did I do? Father, what evil news you bring.

Amphitryon.
You were mad. It is a wretched explanation you are asking for.

Heracles.
Am I my wife's murderer too?

Amphitryon.
All of this is the work of one hand. Yours.

Heracles.
Aiai. A cloud of groaning is closing round me.

Amphitryon.
That is why I groan for what has happened to you.

Heracles.
Did I smash my house as well, in my madness?

Amphitryon.
I know one thing only. Everything of yours is ruined.

Heracles.
Where did the madness take hold of me? Where did it destroy me?

Amphitryon.
When you were purifying your hands with fire at the altar.

Heracles.
Oh. Then why am I sparing my own life, when I have become the murderer
of the children I loved most? Why do I not go and jump from some sheer
rock, or drive a sword into my liver and be the judge who avenges my
children's blood, or burn this flesh that she drove mad, and push away
from my life the disgrace that is waiting for me? But here is Theseus,
coming to stand in the way of my plans for dying, my kinsman and my
friend. I will be seen. The pollution of a child-killer will come before
the eyes of the dearest of my friends. Oh, what am I going to do? Where
can I find a place empty of evils? Grow wings, or go under the ground?
Here, let me throw some darkness over my head. I am ashamed of the
evils I have done, and I have taken this blood on me that cries out for
vengeance, and I do not want to harm anyone who is innocent.

(He covers his head with his robe. Theseus comes in.)

Theseus.
I have come with others, armed young men of the land of Athens, who are
waiting by the streams of the Asopus. I am bringing your son an allied
spear, old man. Word came to the city of Erechtheus's people that Lycus
had snatched the sceptre of this country and set himself against you in
war and open battle. So I came, to pay something back for what Heracles
did for me first, when he saved me from the world below, in case you
need anything, old man, from my hand or my allies. Ah. Why is the floor
so thick with these dead? Surely I have not been left behind, arrived
too late for some newer evil? Who killed these children? Whose wife is
this I am looking at? Boys do not stand anywhere near the spear. No,
this is some other, some strange new evil I have found here.

Amphitryon.
King, whose home is the hill where the olive grows.

Theseus.
Why do you call on me with such a pitiful opening?

Amphitryon.
We have suffered. Wretched sufferings, from the gods.

Theseus.
These children. Whose are they, that you are crying over them?

Amphitryon.
	My son fathered them, my wretched son,
	and having fathered them he killed them. He had the heart for that blood.

Theseus.
	Do not say such things. Speak well.

Amphitryon.
	You are telling people who wish they could.

Theseus.
	You have said a terrible thing.

Amphitryon.
	We are gone, gone, flown away.

Theseus.
	What are you saying? What did he do?

Amphitryon.
	He went astray in a stroke of madness,
	with arrows dipped in the hundred-headed hydra.

Theseus.
	This is Hera's fight. But who is this among the dead, old man?

Amphitryon.
	Mine. He is mine, this son of mine, who has laboured so much,
	who went with the gods to the plain of Phlegra
	with his shield, to the spear-fight that killed the giants.

Theseus.
	Pheu, pheu. Was any man ever born so hated by his god?

Amphitryon.
	You could not find another
	among mortals who has laboured more,
	or been driven further.

Theseus.
	Why is he hiding his wretched head in his robe?

Amphitryon.
	He is ashamed to meet your eye,
	ashamed before a kinsman's love,
	and the blood of his children.

Theseus.
	But I came to share his pain. Uncover him.

Amphitryon.
	My child, take the robe away from your eyes,
	throw it off, show your face to the sun.
	A grief as heavy as yours is wrestling with your tears.
	I beg you, at your beard, at your knee,
	at your hand, falling before you, letting fall
	an old man's grey tears. Io, my boy,
	hold down the wild lion's temper in you.
	You are being driven to a roar of unholy bloodshed,
	wanting to join evils to evils, child.

Theseus.
Well then. You, sitting there in that wretched posture. I am calling to
you: show your face to your friends. There is no darkness with a cloud
black enough to hide the disaster of your evils. Why are you waving
your hand at me, signalling murder? So that the pollution of speaking
to you will not fall on me? It is nothing to me to share in your bad
fortune. I had good fortune with you once. I have to carry my mind back
to that, to the time you brought me safe up from the dead into the
light. I hate a friend's gratitude that grows old. I hate the man who
wants to enjoy his friends' good things and will not sail in the same
ship with them when they are in trouble. Stand up. Uncover your
wretched head. Look at us. A man who is well born bears what the gods
throw down on him, and does not refuse it.

Heracles.
Theseus, did you see this struggle? My children?

Theseus.
I heard of it. And now you are showing evils to a man who can see.

Heracles.
Then why have you uncovered my head to the sun?

Theseus.
Why not? You are mortal. You cannot pollute what belongs to the gods.

Heracles.
Run, you poor man, from my unholy pollution.

Theseus.
No avenging spirit passes from friend to friend.

Heracles.
Thank you for that. And I do not regret the good I did you.

Theseus.
And I, who had good done to me then, pity you now.

Heracles.
I am to be pitied. I killed my own children.

Theseus.
I weep for you, now that your fortune has turned to this.

Heracles.
Have you ever found another man in greater evils?

Theseus.
Your misfortune reaches from the earth up to the sky.

Heracles.
Then I am ready. I mean to die.

Theseus.
Do you think the gods care anything for your threats?

Heracles.
The god is stubborn. So I will be stubborn with the gods.

Theseus.
Hold your tongue. Big talk brings bigger suffering.

Heracles.
I am full of evils already. There is no room left to stow another.

Theseus.
What will you do, then? Where is this anger carrying you?

Heracles.
I will die, and go back under the earth, where I came from.

Theseus.
That is the talk of any ordinary man.

Heracles.
And you are standing outside the disaster, lecturing me.

Theseus.
Is this Heracles talking, who has endured so much?

Heracles.
Not this much. Not if suffering is measured by any scale.

Theseus.
The benefactor of mankind? Their great friend?

Heracles.
Mankind is no help to me. Hera is winning.

Theseus.
Greece would never stand by and let you die of your own folly.

Heracles.
Listen to me, then, so I can compete in argument against your lectures.
I will unfold it for you: my life was unlivable, now and before. First,
I was born from this man, who killed my mother's old father and had
that blood on him when he married Alcmene, who bore me. When the
foundation of a family is not laid straight, the descendants are bound
to come to grief. And Zeus, whoever Zeus is, begot me as an enemy to
Hera. Do not be hurt, old man. I count you my father, not Zeus. While I
was still at the breast, the woman who shares Zeus's bed slipped snakes
with Gorgon eyes into my swaddling clothes, to kill me. And when I got
the covering of flesh a grown man has, the labours I endured, what need
to tell them? What lions, what three-bodied Typhons, what Giants, what
war against the four-legged crowd of Centaurs did I not finish? And the
hydra, the dog with heads all round that grew back as I cut them, I
killed it, and I went through herds of countless other labours and came
to the dead, to bring Hades' gatekeeper, the three-headed dog, up to
the light, at Eurystheus's orders. And this last labour I endured,
wretched man: I killed my children, and set the coping stone of evils
on my house.

I have come to this by necessity. It is not holy for me to live in
Thebes, which I love. And if I do stay, what temple or gathering of
friends can I go to? The curses I carry do not invite greetings. Shall
I go to Argos? How, when I am in exile from my country? Well then,
shall I rush off to some other city? And then be looked at sideways as
a marked man, and held in check by the bitter goads of tongues: "Isn't
this the son of Zeus, who once killed his children and his wife? Won't
he get himself out of this land and perish?" For a man who was once
called happy, changes are painful. The man who is always badly off
feels nothing; he was born to be wretched. And I think I will come to
this point of misfortune: the earth will send out a voice forbidding me
to touch the ground, and the sea and the springs of the rivers will
forbid me to cross, and I will be an Ixion, driven round in chains on
his wheel. And so this is best: that none of the Greeks see me, the
people I lived among when I was happy and fortune was good. Why should
I live? What gain is there in owning a useless, unholy life? Let the
famous wife of Zeus dance, then, and stamp her shoe on the floor of
Olympian Zeus. She has done what she wanted. She has turned the first
man in Greece upside down, foundations and all. Who would pray to a
goddess like that? A goddess who, because of a woman, out of jealousy
over Zeus's bed, destroyed the benefactors of Greece, who were guilty
of nothing.

Chorus.
This fight is no other god's than the wife of Zeus's. You have judged
that rightly.

Theseus.
I would rather advise you than watch you suffer. No mortal is untouched
by fortune, and no god either, if what the poets sing is not false.
Have they not gone to bed with each other in ways no law allows? Have
they not put their fathers in chains and disgraced them, to get a
throne? And yet they live on Olympus all the same, and they have put up
with their own wrongdoing. And what will you say, then, if you, born a
mortal, take your fortunes too hard, and the gods do not? Leave Thebes,
then, for the law's sake, and come along with us to the city of Pallas.
There I will purify your hands of the pollution, and I will give you a
house and a share of what I own. And the gifts I have from the
citizens for saving the fourteen children, when I killed the bull of
Cnossos, I will give to you. Everywhere in the country I have been
allotted precincts of land, and from now on people will call them by
your name while you live. And when you die and go down to Hades, the
whole city of the Athenians will raise you up in honour with sacrifices
and monuments of stone. It is a fine crown for citizens to win from the
Greeks, a good name, for helping a good man. And I will give you this
in return for saving me. Because now you need friends. When the gods
honour a man, he has no need of friends. The god's help is enough, when
he chooses to give it.

Heracles.
Oh. All this is beside the point of my evils. I do not believe the gods
love beds that are not lawful. I never thought it worth believing that
they fasten chains on one another's hands, and I will not be persuaded
of it now, or that one god is born the master of another. A god, if he
is truly a god, needs nothing. These are the wretched tales of poets.
But I have considered, even in my evils, whether I would be convicted
of cowardice if I left the light. Whoever cannot stand up under
disasters could not stand up to a man's weapon either. I will hold out.
I will endure life. I will go to your city, and I owe you thanks for
countless gifts.

Well. I have tasted countless labours, and I refused none of them, and
I never let a spring run from my eyes. I never thought I would come to
this, to let tears fall. But now, it seems, I must be a slave to
fortune. So be it. Old man, you see me going into exile. You see that I
am the murderer of my own children. Give them to the tomb and lay them
out, and honour them with your tears, since the law does not let me. Lay
them against their mother's breast and put them in her arms, a wretched
partnership, which I in my wretchedness destroyed without meaning to.
And when you have hidden the dead in the earth, live on in this city,
wretchedly, but all the same force your soul to carry my evils with me.
Children, the father who begot you and fathered you has destroyed you,
and you got no good from my fine deeds, from all the labour and the
force I spent to lay up a good name for you, a fine thing to have from
a father. And you, poor woman, I destroyed you too, and not as you
deserved, you who kept my bed safe and faithful, and bore the long
watch of keeping my house. Oh, my wife and my children. Oh, myself, how
wretchedly I have done, and how I am unyoked from my children and my
wife. Bitter, the sweetness of these kisses. Bitter, the partnership of
these weapons. I do not know whether to keep them or let them go. They
will knock against my side and say this to me: "With us you took your
children and your wife. You are keeping us. Your children's killers."
Then am I to carry them on my arm? Saying what? But strip myself of the
weapons I did the finest things in Greece with, throw myself under my
enemies' feet, and die in shame? I must not leave them. Wretchedly, I
must keep them. In one thing, Theseus, share the labour with me: come
with me to Argos and help me settle the business of the wretched dog I
have to deliver there, so that grief for my children does not make me
do something, if I am alone. Land of Cadmus, and all the people of
Thebes, cut your hair, mourn with me, come to my children's burial. You
will mourn all of us together, in one word: the dead, and me. We have
all perished, all of us wretched, struck down by one blow of Hera's.

Theseus.
Stand up, you poor man. Enough of tears.

Heracles.
I cannot. My joints have frozen.

Theseus.
Yes. Misfortune brings down the strong as well.

Heracles.
Pheu. I wish I could turn to stone right here, and forget my evils.

Theseus.
Stop that. Give your hand to a friend who wants to help.

Heracles.
No. I will wipe the blood off on your clothes.

Theseus.
Wipe it off. Do not spare them. I do not refuse you.

Heracles.
I have lost my sons, and I have you for a son.

Theseus.
Put your arm round my neck, and I will lead you.

Heracles.
A loving pair we make. And one of us is broken. Old man, this is the
kind of man to have for a friend.

Amphitryon.
The country that bore him is blessed in her sons.

Heracles.
Theseus, turn me round again, so I can see my children.

Theseus.
What for? Do you think that is a charm that will make you feel better?

Heracles.
I long for it. And I want to hold my father to my chest.

Amphitryon.
Here I am, my boy. What you want is what I want.

Theseus.
Have you no memory left of your labours, that you carry on like this?

Heracles.
Everything I endured then was less than this.

Theseus.
If anyone sees you acting like a woman, they will not admire it.

Heracles.
Am I so low in your eyes, alive? I don't think I was before.

Theseus.
Far too low. Sick like this, you are not the famous Heracles.

Heracles.
And what were you like, down below, in your evils?

Theseus.
When it came to nerve, I was the least of men.

Heracles.
Then how can you still say that I have been shrunk by my evils?

Theseus.
Walk on.

Heracles.
Goodbye, old man.

Amphitryon.
And goodbye to you, my child.

Heracles.
Bury the children as I told you.

Amphitryon.
And who will bury me, my child?

Heracles.
I will.

Amphitryon.
When will you come back for me?

Heracles.
When you have buried the children.

Amphitryon.
How will you manage that?

Heracles.
	I will send for you, from Thebes to Athens.
	But carry the children in. The earth will find them a heavy load.
	And I, who have wasted my house in shame,
	will follow Theseus like a little boat towed behind, a total wreck.
	Whoever wants wealth or strength
	more than good friends has not thought it through.

(Heracles goes out, leaning on Theseus. Amphitryon and the Chorus turn
to the bodies.)

Chorus.
	We go, pitiful, with many tears,
	having lost the greatest of our friends.
