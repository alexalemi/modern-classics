Helen
(Part 1 of 4)

(Egypt, on the island of Pharos, in front of the royal palace. The tomb
of Proteus stands before the doors. Helen is sitting on it.)

Helen.
This is the Nile, the river of the lovely nymphs. There is no rain
from heaven here; when the white snow melts, it is the Nile that
waters the plain of Egypt and its fields. Proteus, while he lived, was
king of this land; he lived on the island of Pharos and was lord of
Egypt, and he married one of the girls of the sea, Psamathe, after she
had left the bed of Aeacus. She bore him two children in this house: a
son, Theoclymenus, so called because he honoured the gods all his
life, and a noble daughter, her mother's pride, who was called Eido
when she was a baby. When she grew up and reached the age for
marriage, they called her Theonoe, because she knew everything the
gods know, everything that is and everything that is to come. That was
an honour handed down to her from her ancestor Nereus.

As for me, my own country is not a nameless one: it is Sparta, and my
father is Tyndareus. Though there is a story that Zeus flew to my
mother Leda in the shape of a swan, pretending to run from an eagle,
and got into her bed by that trick. If the story is true. My name is
Helen. And what I have suffered, I will tell you.

Three goddesses came to a hollow on Mount Ida, to Paris, over the
question of beauty: Hera, and Aphrodite, and the virgin daughter of
Zeus. They wanted a judgement on their looks, and they wanted it
settled. Aphrodite held out my beauty as the prize, if a thing that
brings such misery can be called beauty: Paris was to have me for his
wife. And she won. So Paris of Ida left his cattle stalls and came to
Sparta, to take me to bed. But Hera was angry at losing to the other
two, and she turned my marriage to Paris into a puff of wind. She gave
the son of King Priam not me, but a breathing image, put together out
of the sky and made to look like me. He thinks he has me. It is an
empty thought. He has nothing.

And on top of these troubles Zeus had plans of his own. He brought a
war on the land of the Greeks and on the poor Phrygians, to lighten
mother earth of the swarming mass of mortals on her, and to make the
strongest man in Greece famous. So I was set up as the prize the
Greeks fought for, to test the courage of the Phrygians. Not me. My
name. Hermes took me up into the folds of the air and wrapped me in a
cloud, because Zeus had not forgotten me, and set me down here in the
house of Proteus, whom he had picked out of all mankind as the most
self-controlled man alive, so that I could keep my bed untouched for
Menelaus. So here I am. And my poor husband gathered an army and went
off to the towers of Troy, hunting for the wife who had been snatched
from him. Many lives have been lost because of me on the banks of the
Scamander. And I, who have endured all of it, am cursed. Everyone
thinks I betrayed my husband and started a great war for the Greeks.

Then why am I still alive? Because I heard the god Hermes say that I
would live again on the famous plain of Sparta, with my husband, since
he knew I never went to Troy, and that I would never lie in another
man's bed. Well, as long as Proteus was alive and saw this sunlight, I
was safe from marriage. But now he is hidden in the dark of the earth,
and the dead man's son is hunting me for his wife. So, for the sake of
the husband I married long ago, I have thrown myself down here at
Proteus's tomb as a suppliant, so that it will keep my bed safe for my
husband. If I carry a filthy name all over Greece, at least my body
will not bring me disgrace here.

(Teucer comes in, a bow in his hand. He stares at the palace, then
sees Helen.)

Teucer.
Who holds the power in this fortress of a house? You could set it
beside the house of Wealth himself: the royal walls, the fine cornices
on the buildings. Ah. Oh gods, what am I looking at? I see the
murderous likeness of the woman I hate most in the world, the woman
who destroyed me and every Achaean with me. May the gods spit you out,
for how much you look like Helen. If I were not standing on foreign
soil, this well-aimed arrow would have killed you by now, as your
reward for looking like the daughter of Zeus.

Helen.
Poor man. Whoever you are, why turn away from me like that, and hate
me for the disasters that woman caused?

Teucer.
I was wrong. I gave in to anger more than I should have. All Greece
hates the daughter of Zeus. Forgive me for what I said, lady.

Helen.
Who are you? Where have you come from, to set foot in this country?

Teucer.
One of the Achaeans, lady. One of the unlucky ones.

Helen.
Then it is no wonder you hate Helen. But who are you, and where from?
Whose son should I call you?

Teucer.
My name is Teucer. The father who begot me is Telamon, and Salamis is
the land that raised me.

Helen.
Then why are you visiting these fields of the Nile?

Teucer.
I am an exile. I have been driven out of my father's country.

Helen.
That is hard on you. Who threw you out of your homeland?

Teucer.
Telamon, my own father. Who could be closer to you than that?

Helen.
But why? There is some disaster in this somewhere.

Teucer.
My brother Ajax died at Troy, and that destroyed me.

Helen.
How? You did not take his life with your own sword, surely?

Teucer.
He threw himself on his own sword. That is what killed him.

Helen.
Was he mad? Who in his right mind would have the nerve to do that?

Teucer.
You know of a certain Achilles, the son of Peleus?

Helen.
Yes. He came to Sparta once as a suitor for Helen, so we hear.

Teucer.
When he died, he left his allies a quarrel over his armour.

Helen.
And how did that turn into harm for Ajax?

Teucer.
Somebody else got the armour, and he left his life.

Helen.
So you are sick with his sufferings?

Teucer.
Yes. Because I did not die along with him.

Helen.
So you went to the famous city of Troy, stranger?

Teucer.
I did, and I helped to sack it, and I was destroyed in return.

Helen.
Has it been set on fire already, and burned to the ground?

Teucer.
So that not a trace of the walls can be seen.

Helen.
Poor Helen. Because of you the Phrygians are dead.

Teucer.
And the Achaeans too. Great evils have been done.

Helen.
How long is it since the city fell?

Teucer.
Nearly seven years. Seven rounds of harvests.

Helen.
And how long were you at Troy before that?

Teucer.
Many moons. Ten years' worth of them.

Helen.
And did you take the Spartan woman?

Teucer.
Menelaus took her. He dragged her off by the hair.

Helen.
Did you see the wretched woman yourself? Or is it something you heard?

Teucer.
With my own eyes. As plainly as I see you now, no less.

Helen.
Consider whether what you saw was an illusion sent by the gods.

Teucer.
Talk about something else. Not that woman. Not any more.

Helen.
You are that sure your illusion was solid?

Teucer.
I saw it with my own eyes. And the mind has eyes too.

Helen.
And Menelaus is home already, with his wife?

Teucer.
No. He is not in Argos, and not by the streams of the Eurotas.

Helen.
Aiai. That is bad news, for anyone it is bad news for.

Teucer.
The story is that he vanished, and his wife with him.

Helen.
Did all the Argives not sail home the same way?

Teucer.
They did. But a storm scattered them, each one somewhere different.

Helen.
On what stretch of the salt sea?

Teucer.
Crossing the open Aegean, midway over.

Helen.
And since then nobody has seen Menelaus come to land?

Teucer.
Nobody. All over Greece they say he is dead.

Helen.
I am finished. And the daughter of Thestius, is she alive?

Teucer.
Leda, you mean? She is dead and gone.

Helen.
It was not Helen's shameful name that killed her, surely?

Teucer.
They say it was. She tied a noose round her noble neck.

Helen.
And the sons of Tyndareus, are they alive or not?

Teucer.
Dead, and not dead. There are two stories.

Helen.
Which is the stronger? Oh, what a wretched heap of evils I have.

Teucer.
They say the two of them have become gods, in the shape of stars.

Helen.
That is good to hear. And the other story?

Teucer.
That they cut their own throats because of their sister. But enough
talk. I do not need to grieve twice over. As for why I came to this
royal house: I want to see the prophetess Theonoe. Be my sponsor here
and introduce me, so I can get an oracle from her about which way to
steer my ship's sail on a fair wind to Cyprus, the island in the sea
where Apollo has told me I am to live, and to give the place the
island name of Salamis, in memory of the homeland I left behind.

Helen.
The voyage will show you the way itself, stranger. But leave this
country, get out, before the son of Proteus sees you. He rules this
land. He is away just now, out with his hounds, killing wild animals
and trusting them to do it. He kills every Greek stranger he catches.
Why he does it, do not try to find out, and I will not say. What good
would it do you?

Teucer.
Well said, lady. May the gods pay you back with good things for your
kindness. You have Helen's body, but you do not have her heart. Yours
is a very different one. May she die badly, and never reach the
streams of the Eurotas. And may you have good luck always, lady.

(Teucer goes.)

Helen.
	Oh. As I lay the foundations of a great lament for great griefs,
	what kind of cry shall I struggle to raise? What Muse shall I go to
	with tears, or dirges, or mourning? Aiai.

Helen.
	Winged girls,
	virgin daughters of the Earth,
	Sirens, come to my crying,
	and bring the Libyan
	flute, or the pipes, or
	the lyre, and tears to match
	my wailing griefs.
	Pain for pain, song for song,
	let Persephone send
	a choir of the dead
	to sing along with my mourning,
	so that under her dark roof
	she may have from me, along with my tears,
	a hymn of thanks
	for the dead who are gone.

Chorus.
	Beside the dark blue water,
	on the curling grass,
	I was spreading purple cloaks
	in the sun's golden rays
	to dry among the young reeds,
	and I heard my mistress
	cry out in misery from where she sat:
	a wail, a song with no lyre in it,
	a shriek of grief, as she groaned
	her cries of Aiai, the way a nymph,
	a Naiad, up in the mountains,
	running, throws out a wailing note,
	and under the hollows of the rocks
	screams that Pan
	has caught her and forced her.

Helen.
	Io, io.
	Girls of Greece, the catch of a barbarian oar,
	a sailor came, an Achaean sailor,
	he came, he came, bringing me tears on top of tears.
	Troy is torn down,
	and it belongs to the enemy's fire,
	because of me, the killer of so many,
	because of my name, the cause of so much pain.
	And Leda died in a noose,
	from the agony of my disgrace.
	And my husband, after all his wandering on the sea,
	is dead and gone,
	and Castor and his brother,
	the twin glory of their homeland,
	have vanished, vanished, and left the plains
	that rang under their horses, and the wrestling grounds
	along the reedy Eurotas,
	where the young men trained.

Chorus.
	Aiai, aiai.
	Lady, what a fate you were dealt, what a destiny of groans.
	A life that is no life
	fell to you, fell to you, when Zeus begot you on your mother,
	flashing through the sky
	on the snow-white wing of a swan.
	What evil have you been spared?
	What life have you not endured?
	Your mother is gone,
	the twin sons of Zeus, your dear brothers,
	have no happiness,
	you cannot see your own country,
	and through the cities runs
	a rumour that hands you over,
	my lady, to a barbarian's bed.
	Your husband has left his life
	in the sea and the waves, and never again
	will you make your father's halls glad,
	or Athena of the Bronze House.

Helen.
	Pheu, pheu. Who was it, a Phrygian
	or someone from the land of Greece,
	who cut the pine
	that brought tears to Troy?
	From it the son of Priam
	built his deadly boat, and rowed
	with barbarian oars
	to my hearth,
	after my beauty, the most unlucky
	beauty there ever was, to take me
	for his bride. And with him came the trickster,
	the killer of so many, Aphrodite,
	bringing death to the sons of Danaus and the sons of Priam.
	Oh, I am wretched in my misfortune.
	And Hera, holy Hera, whom Zeus holds in his arms
	on her golden throne,
	sent the swift-footed son of Maia,
	and he found me gathering fresh rose petals
	into the fold of my dress,
	on my way to Athena of the Bronze House,
	and snatched me up through the sky
	to this luckless land, and made me
	a quarrel, a wretched quarrel
	between Greece and the sons of Priam.
	And my name
	by the streams of the Simois
	carries a story that is a lie.

Chorus.
You have pain, I know. But it is best to bear the things life forces
on us as lightly as we can.

Helen.
Dear friends, what a fate I am yoked to. Did my mother give birth to
me as a freak, for people to stare at? No woman, Greek or barbarian,
lays a white egg with chicks in it, and they say that is what Leda
bore me in, to Zeus. My whole life, everything about me, is a freak,
partly because of Hera, and partly because my beauty is to blame. If
only I could be wiped out like a painting and drawn again, uglier this
time, instead of beautiful, and the Greeks would forget the evil luck
I have now and remember what is not evil in me, the way they now
remember only the evil.

When someone fixes his eyes on a single misfortune and is ill-treated
by the gods, it is heavy, but it can be borne. But I lie under a whole
heap of troubles. First: I have done nothing wrong, and my name is
filth. And that is worse than the truth would be, to be saddled with
evils that are not your own. Second: the gods have taken me out of my
own country and set me down among barbarian ways, and stripped of
everyone I owe, I have become a slave, though I was born of free
people, because among barbarians everyone is a slave except one man.
And the one anchor that held my fortunes steady, the hope that my
husband would come one day and free me from my troubles: he is dead.
He does not exist any more. My mother is dead, and I am her murderer.
Unjustly, but the injustice is mine to carry. The child who was born
to be the glory of the house and of me, my daughter, is going grey as
an unmarried virgin. And the two they call the Dioscuri, the sons of
Zeus, are no more. With everything I have gone wrong, I am dead as far
as my circumstances go, though not in fact. And this is the last and
worst of it: if I ever did get home, they would bar the doors against
me, thinking I was that Helen from Troy, coming home at Menelaus's
side. If my husband were alive, we could have recognised each other
by falling back on the tokens only the two of us know. But now that is
gone, and he will never be saved.

Then why am I still alive? What fortune is left for me? Shall I choose
marriage as a way out of my troubles, and live with a barbarian man,
sitting at his rich table? But when a woman lives with a husband she
hates, her own body becomes hateful to her too. Death is best. And how
could I die well? Hanging, high in the air, is ugly; even slaves think
it is beneath them. The knife has something noble and fine about it,
and the moment when the flesh is set free from life is a short one.
That is how deep into evil I have come. Other women are lucky because
of their beauty. Mine is the very thing that has destroyed me.

Chorus.
Helen, whoever that stranger was who came here, do not assume that
everything he said was true.

Helen.
And yet he said quite clearly that my husband is dead.

Chorus.
A great many words can be said that turn out to be lies.

Helen.
And the opposite of a lie is plain, and true.

Chorus.
You are being carried towards the disaster, instead of the good.

Helen.
Yes. Fear has wrapped its arms round me and is leading me straight to
the thing I am afraid of.

Chorus.
How much goodwill do you have in this house?

Helen.
Everyone is my friend, except the one who is hunting me for his bed.

Chorus.
Then do you know what you should do? Leave your seat on this tomb and

Helen.
And what advice are you creeping towards?

Chorus.
Go into the house and ask Theonoe, who knows everything, the daughter
of the sea nymph, the child of Nereus's line, about your husband:
whether he is still alive or has left the light of day. And when you
know for certain, then rejoice or mourn, whichever fits your luck.
Before you know anything for sure, what good does it do you to grieve?
Listen to me. Leave this tomb and go and join the girl. You have
someone in this house who can tell you the whole truth; why look any
further? And I want to go in with you, and hear the girl's prophecies
with you. Women should share each other's troubles.

Helen.
	Friends, I take your advice.
	Go in, go into the house,
	and inside these walls
	learn how my struggle ends.

Chorus.
	You are calling one who is more than willing.

Helen.
	Io. What a wretched day.
	What word, what tearful word
	am I going to hear, wretched as I am?

Chorus.
	Do not go prophesying pain,
	dear friend, and mourn before the time.

Helen.
	What has my poor husband suffered?
	Does he see the light,
	and the sun's four-horse chariot, and the paths of the stars,
	or down among the dead under the earth
	does he have his lasting fate?

Chorus.
	Put the best face on what is coming,
	whatever it turns out to be.

Helen.
	I call on you, I swear by you,
	Eurotas, green with your watery reeds:
	if this report is true
	that my husband is dead,
	and what was hard to understand in it?
	I will stretch a deadly noose
	round my neck, or drive a sword
	to the kill, a thrust through my throat
	gushing blood, iron forced into flesh,
	a race to the end, a sacrifice
	to the three goddesses and to the son of Priam
	who used to sit in the hollows of Ida
	beside his cattle stalls.

Chorus.
	May the evil be turned away somewhere else,
	and may your luck be good.

Helen.
	Io, poor Troy.
	For things that were never done, you are destroyed,
	and you have suffered pitiably. The gift Aphrodite gave me
	brought forth blood, so much blood,
	and so many tears. Grief on grief,
	tears on tears it took, and pain:
	mothers lost their sons,
	and girls cut off their hair
	for their dead brothers, by the swollen
	Phrygian water of the Scamander.
	And Greece cried out, cried out,
	and broke into wailing,
	and laid her hands on her head,
	and with her nails she drenched her soft cheeks
	in bloody blows.
	Oh, Callisto, girl of Arcadia, blessed once,
	who climbed into the bed of Zeus on four paws,
	how much better your lot was than my mother's:
	you took the shape of a shaggy-limbed beast,
	the form of a lioness with a fierce eye,
	and traded your load of grief for it.
	And the one Artemis once drove out of her dancing,
	the Titan girl, the daughter of Merops,
	as a deer with golden horns, because of her beauty:
	she was blessed too. But my body
	has destroyed, destroyed the towers of Dardanus's city
	and the Achaeans, who are gone.

(Helen and the Chorus go into the house. Menelaus comes in alone, in
rags.)

Menelaus.
Pelops, you who raced Oenomaus long ago in the four-horse chariot race
at Pisa: if only, on the day they talked you into giving the gods a
banquet, you had left your life then and there, among the gods, before
you ever fathered my father Atreus, who begot Agamemnon and me,
Menelaus, from his marriage to Aerope. A famous pair. I believe, and I
say it without boasting, that I carried the largest army that ever
crossed the sea by oar to Troy, and I was no tyrant driving troops by
force; the young men of Greece came because they wanted to, and I led
them. Some of them can be counted among the dead now. Others were glad
to escape the sea, and brought home the names of the dead. But I have
been wandering wretchedly over the swell of the grey salt sea ever
since I sacked the towers of Troy, and though I long to reach my own
country, the gods do not think I deserve to get there. I have sailed
to every desolate, unfriendly landing place in Libya, and whenever I
get near my homeland the wind shoves me back again, and never once has
a fair breeze filled my sail and let me come home.

And now I am thrown up on this coast, a wretched shipwrecked man who
has lost his friends. My ship is smashed against the rocks into more
pieces of wreckage than I can count. Out of all its clever joinery
only the keel was left, and on it I was saved, barely, by a luck I
never hoped for. I, and Helen, whom I tore away from Troy and have
with me. What this country is called and what people live here, I do
not know. I was ashamed to be seen by a crowd and ask, so I hid these
rags of mine, out of shame for my bad luck. When a man of high
position does badly, the strangeness of it hits him harder than it
hits a man who has been unlucky all along. Need is grinding me down.
There is no food, and there are no clothes on my body; you can judge
for yourself, what I have on is what the ship threw up. The fine robes
and bright cloaks and luxuries I used to wear, the sea has snatched
all of them. I have hidden the woman who started every one of my
troubles deep in a cave, and I have made the friends I have left guard
my wife, and I have come here alone, looking for what those friends of
mine over there need, if I can find it by searching. When I saw this
house with its cornice running round it, and the grand gates of some
prosperous man, I came up to it. Sailors can hope to get something
from a wealthy house. From people with no living of their own, they
could not help us even if they wanted to.

Hey! Is there a doorkeeper who could come out of the house and take
word of my troubles in to the people inside?

(An Old Woman opens the door and stands in it.)

Old Woman.
Who is at the door? Get away from the house. Do not stand there at the
courtyard gate making a nuisance of yourself to my masters. Or you
will die, because you were born a Greek, and Greeks have no business
here.

Menelaus.
Old woman, you could say all of that in a different tone. I will do
what you tell me. Just stop shouting.
