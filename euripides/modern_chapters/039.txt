The Phoenician Women
(Part 1 of 4)

(Thebes, in front of the royal house. Jocasta, an old woman dressed in
black, comes out of it alone.)

Jocasta.
Sun, cutting your road through the stars of the sky, standing in your
chariot inlaid with gold, whirling your flame along behind your fast
horses: what an unlucky beam you sent down on Thebes the day Cadmus came
to this country, leaving Phoenicia and its sea-coast behind him. He
married Harmonia, Aphrodite's daughter, and fathered Polydorus, and from
Polydorus, they say, came Labdacus, and from Labdacus, Laius. I am known
as the daughter of Menoeceus, and Creon is my brother, born of the same
mother. They call me Jocasta. That is the name my father gave me. Laius
married me. And when he had had me in his bed for years and there were
still no children in the house, he went and asked Apollo, and begged him
at the same time for sons, for the two of us to share. And the god said,
"King of Thebes, city of fine horses, do not sow that furrow for
children against the will of the gods. If you father a son, that son
will kill you, and your whole house will wade through blood." But he
gave in to pleasure one night when the wine had him, and sowed a child
in me. And once he had done it, and knew his mistake and what the god
had said, he gave the baby to herdsmen to leave out to die in Hera's
meadow, on the rocks of Cithaeron, after driving iron spikes through the
middle of his ankles. That is why Greece named him Oedipus, the boy with
the swollen feet. Polybus's horse-herders found him and carried him home
and put him in their mistress's arms. And she put the child I had
laboured to bear to her own breast, and persuaded her husband that she
had given birth to him.

When my son was a grown man, with a red-gold beard on his cheeks, either
he guessed it or somebody told him, and he set out for Apollo's temple,
wanting to find out who his parents were. And Laius, my husband, set out
too, to ask whether the child he had left out to die was dead by now.
And the two of them came together at the same spot, where the road
splits in Phocis. Laius's driver called out to him, "Stranger, out of
the road for the king." He walked on without a word, proud man. And the
colts' hooves bloodied the tendons of his feet. And then, but why should
I tell what lies outside our troubles? The son killed the father, and
took the chariot and gave it to Polybus, who had raised him.

Then the Sphinx was crushing the city with her raids, and my husband was
gone, and my brother Creon put my marriage up as a prize: whoever could
solve the clever girl's riddle would have me. And somehow it happened
that my son Oedipus understood the Sphinx's song. So he was made king of
this land, and took the sceptre of this country as his prize. He married
his own mother, poor man, and did not know it, and his mother did not
know she was sleeping with her son. I bore my son children: two boys,
Eteocles and Polyneices, the strong and the famous, and two girls; one
of them her father named Ismene, and the other, the elder, I named
Antigone. When Oedipus, who had endured everything, learned that his
marriage was a marriage to his mother, he did a terrible slaughter on
his own eyes, bloodying the pupils with pins of beaten gold. And when my
sons' cheeks darkened with their beards, they hid their father behind
locked doors, so that what had happened to him, which took a great deal
of cleverness to hide, might be forgotten. He is alive, in the house.
And sick with what fate has done to him, he calls down the most unholy
curses on his sons: that they should divide this house between them with
sharpened iron.

The two of them were afraid the gods would bring those prayers to
fulfilment if they went on living together, so they made an agreement:
Polyneices, the younger, would leave this country first, an exile of his
own free will, and Eteocles would stay and hold the sceptre, and they
would change places every year. But once Eteocles was seated on the
bench of power he would not get off the throne, and he drove Polyneices
out of this land as an exile. Polyneices went to Argos, married into
Adrastus's family, gathered a great mass of Argive shields, and is
leading them here. He has come against these very walls, the walls with
the seven gates, and he is demanding his father's sceptre and his share
of the land. And I, to undo their quarrel, have persuaded one son to
come to the other under truce, before either of them touches a spear.
The messenger I sent says he will come.

Zeus, you who live in the bright folds of the sky, save us. Give my sons
an agreement. If you are wise, as they say you are, you must not let the
same mortal go on being unlucky for ever.

(Jocasta goes back into the house. The Tutor, an old slave, comes out
onto the flat roof above the door, and Antigone climbs after him.)

Tutor.
Antigone, glorious child of your father's house. Your mother let you
leave the girls' rooms and come up to the top storey of the house
because you begged her, to look at the Argive army. But wait a moment,
so I can look over the path first and see whether any of the townspeople
is in sight on the road. Blame would be a small thing to me, a slave. To
you, a princess, it would not. When I have looked at everything, I will
tell you what I saw and heard from the Argives, when I carried the terms
of the truce from here to your brother over there, and back again from
them to here. No. There is nobody from the town anywhere near this
house. Come up the old cedar ladder. Look out over the plain, along the
streams of Ismenus and the spring of Dirce, and see how big the enemy
army is.

Antigone.
	Reach me your hand now, reach it, an old hand to a young one,
	down from the ladder,
	and lift my foot up the last step.

Tutor.
There. Take hold of it, girl. You have come at the right moment. The
Argive army is just on the move, and they are separating the companies
from each other.

Antigone.
	Oh, Lady Hecate, daughter of Leto,
	the whole plain is one sheet of bronze,
	and it flashes like lightning.

Tutor.
Polyneices has not come back to this country in any small way. Many
horses, weapons by the thousand, and the roar of all of them.

Antigone.
	Are the gates barred? Are the bolts with their bronze bands
	fitted into the stone of the walls Amphion built?

Tutor.
Don't worry. Inside, the city is safe. But look at the first one, if you
want to know who he is.

Antigone.
	Who is that one with the white plume,
	who marches out in front of the army
	with a shield of solid bronze
	carried light on his arm?

Tutor.
	A captain, mistress.

Antigone.
	Who is he? Where is he from?
	Say it, old man. What is his name?

Tutor.
He calls himself a Mycenaean by birth, and he lives by the streams of
Lerna. Lord Hippomedon.

Antigone.
	E, e. How proud he is, how frightening to look at,
	like one of the giants born from the earth
	in the paintings, with stars across his shield,
	nothing like the race of men, who live for a day.

Tutor.
	And the one crossing the water of Dirce. Do you see him?

Antigone.
	Different. This one's armour is quite different.
	Who is he?

Tutor.
	He is the son of Oeneus, Tydeus,
	and he carries the war-god of Aetolia in his chest.

Antigone.
	Is this the one, old man, who married
	the sister of Polyneices' wife?
	How foreign his armour looks. Half barbarian.

Tutor.
All the Aetolians carry the big shield, child, and there are no surer
hands with a throwing spear.

Antigone.
And you, old man, how do you know all this so exactly?

Tutor.
I saw the devices on their shields then, when I went to your brother
carrying the terms of the truce, and I recognised them. I looked at them
closely, so I know the men inside the armour.

Antigone.
	And who is this one going past the tomb of Zethus,
	the young one with the long curls
	and the fierce look in his eyes,
	a captain, since a crowd in full armour
	follows at his heels?

Tutor.
	That is Parthenopaeus, Atalanta's son.

Antigone.
	Then may Artemis, who runs the mountains with his mother,
	bring him down with her arrows and destroy him,
	for coming against my city to sack it.

Tutor.
May it be so, child. But they have come to this land with justice on
their side, and that is what I am afraid of: that the gods will see it
the right way.

Antigone.
	And where is the one who was born
	from the same mother as me, to a life of pain?
	Dearest old friend, tell me. Where is Polyneices?

Tutor.
He is over there, near the tomb of Niobe's seven unmarried daughters,
standing close beside Adrastus. Do you see him?

Antigone.
	I see him. Not clearly. But I see something,
	the shape of a body, the outline of a chest.
	If only I could run the road a cloud runs before the wind,
	on my own feet, through the air,
	to my brother, my own blood, and throw my arms
	round his dear neck at last,
	the poor exile. How he stands out
	in his golden armour, old man,
	blazing like the first rays of the morning sun.

Tutor.
	He will come to this house under truce,
	and fill you with joy.

Antigone.
	And that one, old man. Who is he,
	standing in a chariot, driving white horses?

Tutor.
That is the prophet Amphiaraus, mistress. And the animals for the
sacrifice are with him, the streams of blood the earth loves to drink.

Antigone.
	Moon, daughter of the Sun with the shining belt,
	circle of golden light,
	how quietly he drives, how steady the goad,
	shifting it from one horse to the other, keeping them straight.
	And where is the one who shouts those terrible insults at this city?

Tutor.
Capaneus? That is him, working out the ways up the towers, measuring our
walls from top to bottom.

Antigone.
	Io.
	Nemesis, and deep-roaring thunders of Zeus,
	and smoking light of the lightning,
	it is you who put boasts too big for a man to sleep.
	This is the man who says he will give the women of Thebes
	to the women of Mycenae, prisoners of his spear,
	to Lerna's trident and the waters of Amymone
	that Poseidon loves,
	once he has thrown slavery round them.
	Never, never, Lady,
	golden-haired child of Zeus,
	Artemis, let me endure that slavery.

Tutor.
Child, go inside the house and stay in your own rooms under the roof,
now that you have had what you longed for and seen what you wanted to
see. There is a crowd of women coming towards the royal house, now that
confusion has got into the city. And women are a fault-finding lot by
nature. Give them the smallest opening for talk and they bring in more
of it, and there is a kind of pleasure women take in saying nothing good
about each other.

(Antigone and the Tutor go down from the roof. The Chorus come in, young
women of Phoenicia on their way to Apollo's temple at Delphi, held up at
Thebes by the war.)

Chorus.
	I left the swell of the Tyrian sea and came,
	the choicest of offerings for Apollo,
	from the island of Phoenicia,
	to be a slave in Apollo's house,
	where he has settled under the snowy ridges
	of Parnassus. Over the Ionian sea
	I sailed, the oars driving me through the water that rings us round,
	over the plains nobody harvests
	off Sicily, while the west wind
	rode the sky, the loveliest
	music in the heavens.

Chorus.
	Chosen out of my city,
	the finest thing it had to give Apollo,
	I came to the land of Cadmus,
	sent here to the towers of Laius,
	the home of the famous sons of Agenor,
	who are my own blood.
	I have become Apollo's servant,
	like his statues of worked gold.
	But the water of Castalia
	is still waiting for me,
	to drench the girlish glory of my hair
	for the service of Apollo.

Chorus.
	Oh, rock that shines with fire,
	the double-peaked blaze above the heights
	where the women of Dionysus dance,
	and vine that every day
	drips the rich cluster,
	letting the grape-flower loose,
	and holy cave of the serpent,
	and watchtowers of the gods in the hills,
	and sacred mountain under its snow.
	If only I could be whirling in the deathless god's dance
	with nothing to fear,
	in Apollo's valley at the navel of the world,
	with Dirce left behind me.

Chorus.
	But now Ares has come in his fury
	to the front of the walls,
	and he is kindling the blood of war
	against this city. May it never happen.
	The pain of friends is shared,
	and shared too, if this land of seven towers
	suffers anything,
	with the country of Phoenicia. Pheu, pheu.
	One blood, one family of children,
	born from Io, the girl who wore the horns:
	their troubles are mine as well.

Chorus.
	And round the city a thick cloud
	of shields is blazing,
	the shape of bloody battle,
	and Ares will soon know it,
	when he brings down on the sons of Oedipus
	the ruin the Furies have planned.
	Argos, Pelasgian Argos,
	I am afraid of your strength,
	and of what comes from the gods. Because the son
	who comes in arms to claim his house
	is not rushing into an unjust fight.

(Polyneices comes in through the gate, his sword drawn, looking about
him.)

Polyneices.
The gatekeepers' bolts let me in easily enough, inside the walls. Which
is exactly what I am afraid of: that they have me in the net now, and
will not let me out again without blood on my skin. So I have to keep my
eyes moving everywhere, this way and that, in case there is some trick.
With this sword in my hand I can give myself the confidence that comes
from daring. Hey. Who is that? Or is it just a noise I am afraid of?
Everything looks dangerous to a man who is daring something, once his
feet are moving through an enemy's country. I trust my mother, and at
the same time I do not trust her, the woman who persuaded me to come
here under truce. Still, help is close. The altar hearths are right
beside me, and the house is not empty. Come on. Let me put the sword
back into its dark sheath and ask these women standing at the house who
they are.

Ladies, strangers, tell me: what country do you come from, to be at the
door of a Greek house?

Chorus.
Phoenicia is my country, the land that raised me. The descendants of
Agenor sent me here to Apollo, as the pick of the spoils their spears
had won. And the famous son of Oedipus was about to send me on to
Apollo's holy oracle and his altars when the Argives marched against the
city. Now you answer me in return. Who are you, who have come to the
fortress of the Theban land, with its seven gates?

Polyneices.
My father is Oedipus, the son of Laius. My mother is Jocasta, the
daughter of Menoeceus. And the people of Thebes call me Polyneices.

Chorus.
Kinsman of the children of Agenor, my kings, the ones who sent me here.

Chorus.
I go down on my knees before you, my lord, honouring the custom of my
home. You have come at last to your father's land. Io. Io. Lady, come
out to the front of the house. Throw the gates open. Do you hear,
mother, you who gave birth to this man? Why are you slow to leave the
rooms under your roof and put your arms round your son?

(Jocasta comes out of the house, her white hair cut short.)

Jocasta.
	I heard your Phoenician voices, girls,
	and I am dragging my old foot out here,
	one shaking step at a time. Oh, my child.
	At last, after so many thousand days,
	I see your face. Put your arms round me,
	hold your mother to your chest,
	reach me your cheeks, let the dark curls
	of your hair fall over my neck and shade it.
	Io, io. You have barely appeared,
	beyond all hope, beyond all expecting, in your mother's arms.
	What can I say to you? How can I take it all,
	with my hands and with my words,
	the pleasure that winds round and round,
	dancing this way and that way,
	the delight I had in the old days, the old joy?
	Oh, my child,
	you left your father's house empty
	when you were sent away into exile by your brother's outrage.
	How your friends missed you.
	How Thebes missed you.
	That is why I cut off my white hair
	and let it fall, in tears, for grief,
	and I wear no white clothes now, my child,
	but these dark rags instead,
	black round me, in the dark.
	And the old man in the house, his eyes gone,
	always holding on to his longing, all tears,
	for the pair of you, the matched team
	that was unyoked and driven from the house,
	he rushed at the sword to cut his own throat,
	at the rope over the roof-beams,
	groaning his curses on his sons,
	and now with cry after cry of pain
	he hides himself in the dark.
	And you, my child. I hear you are yoked in marriage,
	and have the pleasure of making children
	in a stranger's house,
	and are tending a stranger's kinship,
	a thing your mother cannot bear,
	that Laius, your ancestor, cannot bear,
	the ruin a marriage brought in from outside.
	I never lit the fire for you,
	the torch a wedding calls for,
	the way a happy mother should.
	Ismenus was never asked to the wedding,
	never asked to bring the water for the bath, the luxury of it,
	and through the city of Thebes there was silence
	when your bride came in.
	Curse them all. Whether iron is to blame,
	or strife, or your father,
	or the god who came dancing drunk
	into the house of Oedipus,
	the pain of all these evils has come to me.

Chorus.
Giving birth, with all its pains, is a powerful thing for women. The
whole female race is somehow in love with its children.

Polyneices.
Mother, whether it was sense or folly, I have come among enemies. But
everyone has to love his country; there is no choice about it, and a man
who says otherwise enjoys the words, but his mind is somewhere else. I
was so frightened, so full of fear that some trick of my brother's would
kill me, that I came through the city with my sword in my hand, turning
my face in every direction. One thing helps me: the truce, and your
promise, which brought me inside my father's walls. And I came in tears.
It has been so long since I saw the house and the altars of the gods and
the training grounds where I was brought up and the water of Dirce, and
I was driven away from them unjustly to live in a foreign city, with a
stream of tears running from my eyes. But now, pain on top of pain, I
look at you with your head shaved and your black dress. Oh, my troubles.
How terrible hatred is, mother, between people of one family, and how
hard it makes the making of peace. What is my old father doing in the
house, staring at darkness? And what of my two sisters? They must be
grieving over my exile, poor things.

Jocasta.
Some god is destroying the house of Oedipus out of malice. That is how
it began: I gave birth against every law, your father made an evil
marriage, and you were born. But what is the use of this? What the gods
send has to be borne. I am afraid to ask you what I want to ask, in case
I bite into your heart. But I long to.

Polyneices.
No. Ask. Leave nothing out. What you want, mother, is what I want.

Jocasta.
Then here is the first thing I want to know. What is it, to be robbed of
your country? Is it a great evil?

Polyneices.
The greatest. And it is worse in the living than in the telling.

Jocasta.
What is it like? What is the hard part, for exiles?

Polyneices.
One thing above all. You cannot say what you think.

Jocasta.
That is a slave's condition you are describing. Not saying what you
think.

Polyneices.
You have to put up with the stupidity of the people in power.

Jocasta.
That is painful too, to play the fool alongside fools.

Polyneices.
But for profit you have to be a slave, against your nature.

Jocasta.
Hopes feed exiles, they say.

Polyneices.
They look at you with lovely eyes. But they keep you waiting.

Jocasta.
Doesn't time show them up for the empty things they are?

Polyneices.
They have a sweetness of their own for a man in trouble, like love does.

Jocasta.
And where did you get your living, before your marriage found you one?

Polyneices.
Some days I had enough for the day. Some days I had nothing.

Jocasta.
Your father's friends, his guest-friends, did they not help you?

Polyneices.
Be prosperous. Friends are nothing once you are down.

Jocasta.
Did your good birth not lift you up high?

Polyneices.
Having nothing is the evil. My birth did not feed me.

Jocasta.
So a man's country, it seems, is the dearest thing he has.

Polyneices.
You could not even put into words how dear it is.

Jocasta.
How did you come to Argos? What was your idea?

Polyneices.
Apollo gave Adrastus an oracle.

Jocasta.
What oracle? What are you telling me? I do not follow.

Polyneices.
To marry his daughters to a boar and a lion.

Jocasta.
And what did you have to do with the names of animals, my child?

Polyneices.
I do not know. The god called me to my fate.

Jocasta.
Yes, the god is wise. But how did you win your wife?

Polyneices.
It was night. I came to Adrastus's porch.

Jocasta.
Looking for a bed, wandering, an exile?

Polyneices.
That was it. And then another exile came there after me.

Jocasta.
Who was that? He was miserable too, then.

Polyneices.
Tydeus. They say Oeneus is his father.

Jocasta.
And why did Adrastus compare the two of you to wild animals?

Polyneices.
Because we came to blows over the bed.

Jocasta.
That was when the son of Talaus understood the oracle?

Polyneices.
Yes. And he gave the two of us his two daughters.

Jocasta.
So are you lucky in your marriage, or unlucky?

Polyneices.
I have no complaint against it, so far.

Jocasta.
And how did you persuade an army to follow you here?

Polyneices.
Adrastus swore this to his two sons-in-law, Tydeus and me, since he is
married to my wife's sister: that he would bring us both back home, and
me first. Many of the leading men of Argos and of Mycenae are here with
me, doing me a favour I hate and cannot do without, because it is my own
city I am marching against. I have sworn to the gods that I lifted the
spear against the people I love most against my will, when they were
willing enough. But the undoing of these evils comes down to you,
mother: reconcile the people of one blood, and put an end to the trouble
for me and for you and for the whole city. It has been sung a long time,
but I will say it anyway: money is what people value most, and of
everything in human life it has the most power. That is what I have come
here for, with ten thousand spears at my back. A man of good family who
is poor is nothing.

Chorus.
And here is Eteocles coming to the reconciliation. It is your job,
Jocasta, their mother, to say the kind of words that will bring your
sons together.

(Eteocles comes in from the city, armed.)

Eteocles.
Mother, I am here. I came to do you this favour. What do I have to do?
Let somebody start the talking. I broke off posting the citizens in
double companies round the walls to come and hear this arbitration of
yours between us, the one you persuaded me to admit this man inside the
walls under truce for.

Jocasta.
Wait. Speed does not carry justice with it. Slow words get the most
wisdom done. Loosen that terrible look on your face and the breath of
your anger. You are not looking at the severed head of the Gorgon. You
are looking at your brother, who has come. And you, Polyneices, turn
your face towards your brother. If you look at the same point he is
looking at, you will speak better and take in what he says better. I
want to give you both one piece of good advice. When a man is angry with
a friend and meets him face to face and looks him in the eye, he should
consider only what he has come about, and keep no memory of the old
evils. Polyneices, my child, your speech comes first, because you have
come leading an army of Argives, and you say you have been wronged. May
some god be the judge, and reconcile these evils.
