Ion
(Part 2 of 3)

Ion.
So I have escaped being a slave's child.

Xuthus.
Then take your father, my boy.

Ion.
Well, it makes no sense to doubt the god.

Xuthus.
Now you are thinking straight.

Ion.
And what more could I want—

Xuthus.
Now you are seeing what you ought to see.

Ion.
—than to be the son of a son of Zeus?

Xuthus.
Which is exactly what you are.

Ion.
Then may I really touch the man who fathered me?

Xuthus.
Yes. Trust the god.

Ion.
Father. I greet you—

Xuthus.
That is a word I am glad to hear.

Ion.
—and this day—

Xuthus.
—has made me a happy man.

Ion.
Mother. My dear mother, when will I see your face as well? I long to
look at you now more than ever, whoever you are. But perhaps you are
dead, and there is nothing at all we can do.

Chorus.
The good luck of the house is ours to share. All the same, I wish my
mistress had been lucky in children too, and the house of Erechtheus
with her.

Xuthus.
My son, the god has brought his oracle out true as far as finding you
goes. He has joined you to me, and you have found the thing you love
most without ever knowing it before. And the thing you rushed at just
now, quite rightly, is the thing I long for too: that you should find
your mother, boy, and that I should find out what woman gave you to
me. Give it time and perhaps we will find that out as well. But now
leave the god's floor and your wandering life and come to Athens, of
one mind with your father. Your father's sceptre is waiting for you
there, and it is a fortunate one, and great wealth with it. You will
not be sick with one of the two diseases, low birth and poverty, and
called both names at once. You will be a nobleman, and rich. You are
silent? Why do you keep your eyes fixed on the ground? You have gone
off into your thoughts, and by swinging back like this from joy you
are frightening your father.

Ion.
Things do not look the same when they are far off and when you see
them close up. I welcome what has happened. I have found a father in
you. But listen, father, to what I am working out.

They say that the famous Athenians are born from their own soil, not
a race brought in from somewhere else. So I will land among them
carrying two diseases: a father who is an import, and myself a
bastard. With that reproach on me, while I am still weak, I will be
called a nobody, and a nobody's son. And if I push my way toward the
front bench of the city and try to be somebody, the people with no
power will hate me, because a man above you is galling. And the good
men, the men who could be clever if they chose, but who keep quiet and
do not rush into public business: among them I will be a laugh, and a
fool, for not keeping my head down in a city full of fear. And as for
the speakers, the men who use the city and make their careers in it,
the higher I climb in rank the more they will guard against me with
their votes. That is how it goes, father. The men who hold the cities
and the offices are deadliest of all to anyone who competes with them.

Then there is the house. I come into another man's house as an
outsider, and to a childless woman, who shared your troubles with you
before, and who now has lost her share and will bear her fate alone
and bitterly. How can she help hating me, and fairly, when I stand
there at your elbow, and she, with no child, looks at the thing you
love with bitter eyes? And then you must either betray me and look to
your wife, or honour me and throw your whole house into confusion. How
many killings, how many deadly poisons, women have found to destroy
their husbands with. And besides, I pity your wife, father, growing
old without a child. She comes from noble fathers, and she does not
deserve to sicken with childlessness.

And kingship, which everybody praises for no reason: its face is
sweet, but inside the house it is misery. Who is blessed, who is
happy, who spins out his life afraid, glancing sideways? I would rather
live as a happy commoner than as a tyrant, who takes pleasure in having
bad men for friends and hates the good ones because he is afraid of
being killed. You will tell me gold outweighs all that, and that being
rich is pleasant. I do not like hearing noises in the night while I
clutch my fortune in both hands, and I do not like the work of it
either. Let me have a moderate amount, and no grief.

Now listen, father, to the good things I had here. First, leisure, the
dearest thing a man can have, and not much of a crowd. No villain ever
shoved me off the road, and that is the one unbearable thing, to give
way and step aside for men worse than yourself. I was either at my
prayers to the gods or in talk with people, and the people I served
were happy people, not people in mourning. I was always seeing one set
of strangers off as the next set arrived, so I was always welcome, a
new face among new faces. And the thing people pray for even when they
do not want it, to be honest: custom and my own nature together made
me honest in the god's sight. When I add all that up, father, I think
things are better here than there. Let me live here. The pleasure is
the same either way: to enjoy great things, or to have small ones and
like them.

Chorus.
Well said, so long as the people I love find their happiness in what
you say.

Xuthus.
Stop this talk and learn how to be happy. I want to begin right here,
my boy, where I found you: a shared table, a feast we sit down to
together, and the birth-sacrifice for you that I never made before.
For now I will bring you to my hearth as if you were a guest, and give
you a good dinner, and take you to the land of Athens as a visitor,
supposedly, and not as my own son. I do not want to hurt my wife with
my own good luck while she has no child. In time, when the moment is
right, I will bring her round to letting you hold my sceptre over the
land. And I name you Ion, to fit what happened: you were the one coming
toward me, the first to meet my step, as I came out of the god's
shrine. Now gather the whole company of your friends, and over the
pleasure of a roasted ox say your goodbyes, since you are about to
leave the city of Delphi.

And you slave women, I am telling you: keep silent. Say one word of
this to my wife and it is death.

Ion.
I will go. But one piece of my luck is still missing. If I do not find
the woman who bore me, father, my life is not worth living. And if I
may pray for it, let the mother who bore me be a woman of Athens, so
that I get the right to speak my mind from my mother's side. When a
stranger lands in a city that keeps its blood pure, he may be a citizen
in name, but his mouth is a slave's mouth, and he cannot speak freely.

(Xuthus and Ion go out together. The Chorus are left alone.)

Chorus.
	I see tears, and cries of mourning,
	and the first breaking-out of groans,
	when my queen learns that her husband
	has a fine son,
	and she herself is childless, left without children.
	What song was that you prophesied,
	prophetic son of Leto?
	Where did this boy come from, this foster-child
	raised around your temple? What woman's son is he?
	The oracle does not win me over. There may be a trick in it.
	I am afraid of what is coming,
	wherever it is going to land.
	A strange god is giving me strange things,
	and what he says still sounds fair,
	but the boy raised from another's blood
	is a trick and a stroke of luck together.
	Who will not agree with me?

Chorus.
	Friends, shall we say it plainly,
	straight into our mistress's ear?
	Her husband, in whom she had everything,
	whose hopes she shared, poor woman—
	and now she is wrecked by misfortune, and he is prospering,
	she is sliding into grey old age, and her husband
	has no honour for the people he owes.
	A wretch, who came into the house from outside,
	into great wealth, and did not hold to her fortune,
	who tricked my lady. Let him die. Let him die.
	And may the gods never accept from him
	the fair-flamed cake he burns
	on their fire. He will find out what I am,
	how much I was born a friend
	to my queen's royal house.
	Now they are near the feast already,
	father and son, the new man and the new.

Chorus.
	Io, ridges of Parnassus,
	you rock that holds the peak and the seat in the sky,
	where Bacchus lifts his two flaring pine-torches
	and leaps, light-footed, with the Bacchants who roam the night:
	may that boy never come to my city.
	Let him die and leave his young day behind him.
	Our city would have every reason to groan
	at a foreigner pushing in,
	the city that lord Erechtheus,
	our first ruler, gathered into one.

(Creusa comes in, leading the Old Man who was her father's servant
slowly up the steep path.)

Creusa.
Old man, you were tutor to my father Erechtheus once, when he was
still in the light. Climb up to the god's oracle with me, so that you
can share my joy, if lord Apollo has given any word about the birth
of children. It is sweet to do well when your friends are with you,
and if something bad turns up, which I pray it does not, it is sweet
to look into the eyes of someone who wishes you well. You looked
after my father once. I am your mistress, but all the same I look
after you now, in his place.

Old Man.
Daughter, you keep the ways your parents kept, and they were worthy
of you and you of them. You have never shamed your ancestors, the
children of this soil. Pull, pull me toward the temple, carry me up.
The oracle is a steep place. Work with my old age. Be the doctor for
my leg.

Creusa.
Follow me, then. Watch where you put your feet.

Old Man.
There. The foot is slow. The mind is quick.

Creusa.
Lean on your stick. The path winds round.

Old Man.
The stick is blind too, when I see so little.

Creusa.
True. But do not give up because you are tired.

Old Man.
Not willingly. But I am not master of what I no longer have.

Creusa.
Women, faithful slaves of my loom and my shuttle, what fortune did my
husband take away with him about the children we came here for? Tell
me. If you bring me good news, you will not be throwing your favour to
masters who forget it.

Chorus.
Io. Our fate.

Old Man.
That is not a lucky opening to a speech.

Chorus.
Io. Poor lady.

Old Man.
Is there something in the masters' oracles that hurts me too?

Chorus.
Well then. What do we do? There is death hanging over this.

Creusa.
What is this tune? And what is the fear about?

Chorus.
Do we speak or keep silent? What are we going to do?

Creusa.
Speak. You have some misfortune for me. I can see it.

Chorus.
Then it will be said, even if I have to die twice for it. Mistress,
you will never take a child in your arms, or hold one to your breast.

Creusa.
Oh. Let me die.

Old Man.
Daughter.

Creusa.
	Poor me, and my misfortune.
	I have taken it, I have suffered it, a pain
	no one could live with, friends.
	We are finished.

Old Man.
	Child.

Creusa.
	Aiai. Aiai.
	The pain has gone straight through me,
	deep inside my lungs.

Old Man.
Do not groan yet—

Creusa.
But the grief is here already.

Old Man.
—until we learn—

Creusa.
Learn what? What news is there for me?

Old Man.
—whether the master is in the same case and shares the misfortune
with you, or whether you are unlucky alone.

Chorus.
To him, old man, Apollo has given a son. He is lucky on his own
account, apart from her.

Creusa.
This on top of that. The very peak of evil you have cried out, a pain
for me to groan over.

Old Man.
This boy you speak of: is he still to be born, from some woman, or
did the god prophesy one already born?

Chorus.
Already born, and fully grown, a young man. Apollo gives him to him.
I was there.

Creusa.
What are you saying? Unspeakable. Unspeakable. A word that should
never be spoken, and you cry it at me.

Old Man.
And at me. But how is the oracle being carried out? Tell me more
clearly. And who is the boy?

Chorus.
Whoever your husband met first as he came hurrying out from the god,
that man the god gave him for a son.

Creusa.
Ottotottotoi. Then it is a childless life, childless, that he has
cried out for me? And I will live in a lonely house, with no one to
come after me.

Old Man.
Then who was the oracle about? Whose step did the poor woman's husband
fall in with? How, and where, did he see him?

Chorus.
Dear mistress, you know the young man who was sweeping this temple?
He is the son.

Creusa.
Let me fly up through the wet air, far from the land of Greece, out to
the evening stars. Such pain, such pain I have suffered, friends.

Old Man.
And what name does his father call him? Do you know, or is that still
unsettled, and unsaid?

Chorus.
Ion, because he was the first to meet his father coming out.

Old Man.
And what mother is he—

Chorus.
I cannot tell you. But so that you know everything I know, old man,
her husband has gone off to the sacred tents, without telling her, to
make the guest-offerings and the birth-offerings for the boy, and to
sit down to a feast with his new son.

Old Man.
Mistress, we are betrayed. I say we, because I am sick with the same
sickness as you. Betrayed by your husband. It was planned. We are
being outraged and thrown out of the house of Erechtheus. And I do
not say this because I hate your husband. I say it because I love you
more than him. He came into this city as a stranger, married you, took
over your house and your whole inheritance, and now it turns out he
has been secretly harvesting children from another woman. How
secretly? I will tell you. When he saw you were childless, he was not
content to be like you and carry an equal share of your fortune. He
took a slave to his bed, married her in secret, and fathered the boy,
and then he sent him out of the way and gave him to some Delphian to
raise. And the boy was let loose in the god's house, so nobody would
notice, and brought up there. Then when he heard the boy had grown to
be a young man, he persuaded you to come here about your childlessness.
So the god did not lie. He lied. He had been raising the boy all these
years, and he wove his plots like this: if he was caught, he would put
it all down to the god; if he got away with it, he would come home,
and take his time about it, and wrap the kingship of the land round the
boy. And the new name, shaped to fit after all this time: Ion, because
supposedly he met him coming.

Chorus.
Oh, how I have always hated wicked men, who put an injustice together
and then dress it up with schemes. I would rather have a plain honest
man for a friend than a bad one who was cleverer.

Old Man.
And of all these evils you will suffer the last and worst: to bring
into your house as its master a nobody with no mother and no name,
some slave woman's child. The evil would have been a simple one if he
had talked you round, pleading your childlessness, and settled a
nobly born mother's son in the house. And if that was too bitter for
you, he should have gone and asked for a marriage among the daughters
of Aeolus. So now you must do something, and do it the way a woman
does. Take a sword, or use some trick, or poison, and kill your
husband and the boy before death comes to you from them. If you hold
back from this, you will lose your life. When two enemies come under
one roof, one of them has to go down, or the other. I am willing to
work at this with you, and to share in killing the boy. I will go into
the house where he is laying on his feast, and pay my masters back for
my keep, and either die or live to look at the light. There is one
thing, and one only, that brings shame on a slave, and that is the
name. In everything else a slave is no worse than a free man, if he is
a good one.

Chorus.
And I too, dear mistress, want to share in this fortune with you, and
either die or live with honour.

Creusa.
	My soul, how can I keep silent?
	But how can I bring that dark bed into the light
	and be left with no shame at all?
	What is there still standing in my way?
	Who is it I am competing with, to be the better woman?
	Has my husband not turned traitor to me?
	I am robbed of my house, robbed of children,
	and the hopes are gone, the hopes I wanted
	to settle well and could not,
	keeping silent about that coupling,
	silent about that birth, with all its tears.
	No. By the starry seat of Zeus,
	and by the goddess who stands on my cliffs,
	and by the holy shore of the Tritonian lake,
	I will hide that bed no longer.
	Once I have taken it off my chest I will breathe easier.
	My eyes are streaming with tears,
	my soul is in pain from the evil that was planned against me
	by men and by immortals both,
	and I will show them up for what they are,
	traitors to the bed, and ungrateful.
	You who sing to the voice
	of the seven-stringed lyre,
	who make the Muses' hymns ring sweet
	on horns from the fields, on dead horn,
	son of Leto, it is you I will accuse,
	out loud, here in this daylight.
	You came to me with your hair shining gold,
	when I was gathering saffron petals
	into the folds of my dress,
	flowers that flashed the gold back at the sun.
	You gripped my white wrists,
	you dragged me to a bed in the cave
	while I screamed "Mother! Mother!",
	you, a god, to lie with me,
	with no shame in you,
	doing Aphrodite a favour.
	And I bore you a son, poor wretch that I am,
	and in terror of my mother I threw him
	onto that bed of yours,
	where you had yoked me, miserable,
	in a miserable coupling.
	Oimoi. And now he is gone,
	snatched up as a feast for birds,
	my boy, and yours, poor thing.
	And you play your lyre,
	and sing your songs of healing.
	Ohe.
	You, son of Leto, I am calling you,
	you who deal out your voice from your golden throne,
	your seat at the centre of the earth:
	into your ear I will shout it.
	Io. You were a bad lover.
	You settle a son in my husband's house
	when he has done nothing to earn it,
	and my child, and yours, the poor ignorant thing,
	is gone, torn up by birds,
	stripped of the swaddling clothes his mother wrapped him in.
	Delos hates you now, and the young laurel
	beside the palm with its soft hair,
	where Leto bore you in her holy labour,
	the fruit of Zeus.

Chorus.
Oh, what a great storehouse of evils has been opened. Anyone would
weep at it.

Old Man.
Daughter, I cannot get my fill of looking at your face, and my mind
has gone out from under me. I had just bailed one wave of evils out
of my thoughts, and another lifts me from astern, from your words. You
have flung off the troubles in front of us and gone down some other
evil road, into other griefs. What are you saying? What charge are you
bringing against Apollo? What child do you say you bore? Where in the
city did you lay him down, a tomb for wild beasts to enjoy? Go back
over it for me.

Creusa.
I am ashamed in front of you, old man. But I will say it all the same.

Old Man.
I know how to grieve with the people I love, and grieve properly.

Creusa.
Then listen. You know the cave on the north side of Cecrops's rock,
the cliffs we call the Long Rocks?

Old Man.
I know it. The shrine of Pan is there, and the altars near it.

Creusa.
That is where I fought a terrible fight.

Old Man.
What fight? My tears are coming out already to meet your words.

Creusa.
Apollo forced me. I lay with him against my will.

Old Man.
Daughter, was this the thing I noticed at the time?

Creusa.
I do not know. If you are telling the truth, I would say yes.

Old Man.
When you were groaning in secret over some hidden sickness?

Creusa.
That was the time. That was the evil I am showing you plainly now.

Old Man.
And then how did you hide what Apollo had done?

Creusa.
I gave birth. Bear with me, old man, while you hear this.

Old Man.
Where? Who delivered you? Or did you labour through it alone?

Creusa.
Alone, in the cave where I was yoked to him.

Old Man.
And the child? Where is he, so that you need not be childless any
longer?

Creusa.
He is dead, old man. I put him out for the wild beasts.

Old Man.
Dead? And Apollo, the bad god, gave no help at all?

Creusa.
None. My boy is being raised in the house of Hades.

Old Man.
Who put him out, then? Surely not you.

Creusa.
I did. In the dark, after I had wrapped him in my robes.

Old Man.
And nobody at all knew you had left the baby out?

Creusa.
Only my misfortune, and the darkness.

Old Man.
How did you have the heart to leave your own child in that cave?

Creusa.
How? I said a great many pitiful things over him with this mouth—

Old Man.
Pheu. You had a hard heart, and the god's was harder than yours.

Creusa.
If you had seen the baby stretching out his hands to me.

Old Man.
Looking for the breast? Or to fall into your arms?

Creusa.
For the place he never got to, where I did him such a wrong.

Old Man.
And what put it into your head to throw your child away?

Creusa.
I thought the god would save his own son.

Old Man.
Oh, what a storm is battering the good fortune of your house.

Creusa.
Why have you covered your head, old man? Why are you weeping?

Old Man.
Because I see you and your father brought low.

Creusa.
That is what it is to be mortal. Nothing stays in the same place.

Old Man.
Then let us hold on to our grieving no longer, daughter.

Creusa.
What am I supposed to do? To be unlucky is to have no way out.

Old Man.
Pay back the god who wronged you first.

Creusa.
And how am I, a mortal, going to outrun the powers above me?

Old Man.
Set fire to Apollo's holy oracle.

Creusa.
I am afraid. I have grief enough already.

Old Man.
Then dare what you can. Kill your husband.

Creusa.
I respect the bed we shared in the days when he was good.

Old Man.
Then at least the boy who has appeared to take your place.

Creusa.
How? If only it were possible. How I would love to.

Old Man.
Arm your attendants with swords.

Creusa.
I would go and do it. But where is it to happen?

Old Man.
In the sacred tents, where he is feasting his friends.

Creusa.
The murder would be seen by everyone, and slaves are weak.

Old Man.
Oh, now you are losing your nerve. Come on then, you think of
something.

Creusa.
As it happens I do have something. It is a trick, and it works.

Old Man.
Then I am your servant for both, the trick and the work.

Creusa.
Then listen. You know the battle of the earth-born giants?

Old Man.
I know it. At Phlegra, the Giants stood up against the gods.

Creusa.
That is where Earth gave birth to the Gorgon, a terrible monster.

Old Man.
To fight beside her own children, and make trouble for the gods?

Creusa.
Yes. And the goddess Pallas, the daughter of Zeus, killed it.

Old Man.
What shape did it have, this wild thing?

Creusa.
A breastplate armoured with coils of vipers.

Old Man.
Is this the story I heard long ago?

Creusa.
That Athena wears its skin over her chest.

Old Man.
The thing they call the aegis, Pallas's armour?

Creusa.
That is the name it got the day she rushed into the gods' battle.

Old Man.
But daughter, how does this hurt your enemies?

Creusa.
You know Erichthonius, or— But of course you do, old man.

Old Man.
The first of your ancestors, the one Earth sent up?

Creusa.
When he was newborn, Pallas gave him—

Old Man.
Gave him what? You are slow bringing this word out.

Creusa.
Two drops of blood from the Gorgon.

Old Man.
And what power do they have over a human body?

Creusa.
One kills. The other cures diseases.

Old Man.
What did she fasten them in, to hang them round the baby's body?

Creusa.
Gold bands. And he gave the thing to my father.

Old Man.
And when he died, it came to you?

Creusa.
Yes. I wear it here, on my wrist.

Old Man.
So how does the goddess's double gift work?

Creusa.
The drop that ran from the hollow vein when she killed it—

Old Man.
What is that one used for? What power does it carry?

Creusa.
It keeps off disease, and it feeds life.

Old Man.
And the second one you spoke of, what does that do?

Creusa.
It kills. It is the venom of the Gorgon's snakes.

Old Man.
And do you carry them mixed into one, or separate?

Creusa.
Separate. Good does not mix with evil.

Old Man.
My dearest child, you have everything you need.

Creusa.
The boy will die by this. And you will be the one who kills him.

Old Man.
Where, and how? Yours is to say it, mine is to dare it.

Creusa.
In Athens, when he comes to my house.

Old Man.
That is not well said. You found fault with my plan, and I find fault
with yours.

Creusa.
How? Do you suspect the same thing that is coming into my own mind?

Old Man.
You will get the blame for the boy's death even if you do not kill
him.

Creusa.
True. They say stepmothers are jealous of their husbands' children.

Old Man.
Then kill him here, where you can deny the murder.

Creusa.
And I get the pleasure of it sooner, too.

Old Man.
And you will keep your husband in the dark, in exactly the way he is
trying to keep you.

Creusa.
Then you know what to do? Take this from my hand, this gold thing of
Athena's, the old work, and go to where my husband is secretly
sacrificing his oxen. When they have finished dinner and are about to
pour their libations to the gods, have this ready in the folds of your
clothes, and drop it into the young man's cup. His alone, not
everyone's. Keep a separate drink for the man who means to be master
of my house. And once it is down his throat, he will never reach
famous Athens. He will die here, and here he will stay.

Old Man.
Now you go inside, to the house of our hosts. I will finish the work I
have been set. Come on, old foot, be young in what you do, even if
time will not allow you to be young in years. Go with your masters
against their enemy, and help to kill him, and help to clear him out
of the house. Piety is a fine thing to honour when you are
prospering. But when a man wants to hurt his enemies, there is no law
that stands in the way.

(Creusa goes into the house of her Delphian hosts. The Old Man goes
off toward the feast.)

Chorus.
	Goddess of the crossroads, daughter of Demeter,
	who rule the attacks that walk by night
	and the attacks that walk by day,
	guide the filling of the death-cups
	to where my queen, my queen, is sending them:
	the drops from the cut throat
	of the Gorgon, earth's child,
	for the man who is laying hands
	on the house of Erechtheus's sons.
	Let no one from any other house
	ever rule my city
	except the nobly born sons of Erechtheus.

Chorus.
	And if the death miscarries, and my mistress's efforts,
	and the moment for daring passes,
	the moment where her hope showed itself just now,
	then either a sharpened sword, or a noose
	that fate will knot around her neck.
	She will finish one suffering with another
	and go down to another shape of life.
	Because she would never, while she lived,
	with her eyes open in the bright light,
	put up with strangers from another land
	ruling her house,
	she who was born of the noble houses.

Chorus.
	I am ashamed before the god of the many hymns,
	if this boy is to stand beside the fair-dancing springs
	and watch the torches on the twentieth night,
	sleepless, awake till morning,
	when the starry sky of Zeus
	takes up the dance too,
	and the moon dances,
	and the fifty daughters of Nereus,
	who dance across the sea
	and in the eddies of the ever-flowing rivers,
	in honour of Persephone with her crown of gold
	and her holy mother.
	That is where he hopes to be king,
	dropping onto other men's labour,
	Apollo's vagabond.

Chorus.
	Look at us, all you poets who go
	against us with your ugly music,
	singing of our beds, and our loves,
	Aphrodite's lawless, unholy loves.
	See how far we outdo in decency
	the unjust sowing that men do.
	Let the song turn round.
	Let the ugly music about beds
	go against men now.
	Because this man from the sons of Zeus
	shows how forgetful he is:
	he never fathered on his house
	a child his mistress could share,
	he paid his court to another love instead,
	and got himself a bastard son.

(A Servant of Creusa runs in.)

Servant.
Noble women, where can I find my mistress, the daughter of Erechtheus?
I have searched every corner of the town for her and I cannot lay
hands on her.

Chorus.
What is it, fellow slave? Why the hurry in your feet? What news are
you carrying?

Servant.
We are being hunted. The rulers of this land are looking for her, to
put her to death by stoning.

Chorus.
Oh. What are you going to tell us? Surely we have not been caught
plotting the secret murder of the boy?

Servant.
You have guessed it. And you will not be the last to share in the
trouble.

Chorus.
How were the hidden schemes seen?

Servant.
The god found them out. Injustice lost to justice. He would not have
his temple polluted.

Chorus.
How? I beg you, I go down on my knees to you, tell us. If we have to
die anyway, we would die more gladly knowing, or go on looking at the
light.

Servant.
When Creusa's husband left the god's oracle, he took his new son off
to the dinner and the sacrifices he was preparing for the gods. Then
Xuthus himself went up to where the god's Bacchic fire leaps, to wet
the two rocks of Dionysus with blood, as a thank-offering for the
sight of his son. He said, "You stay here, my boy, and have the
builders put up the tents, walled in on every side. If I take a long
time over the sacrifice to the gods of birth, let the friends who are
here begin the feast without me." Then he took the calves and went.

The young man set about the enclosure of the tents, and he did it with
ceremony: no walls, upright posts, and he watched the angle of the sun
carefully, so that the tent faced neither the burning rays of midday
nor the rays of the end of the day. He measured out a square a hundred
feet on each side, ten thousand square feet inside it, as the experts
put it, as though he were inviting the whole people of Delphi to the
feast. Then he took sacred weavings from the treasuries and roofed it
with them, and they were a wonder for men to look at.

First he spread over the roof a great wing of woven cloth, the gift of
the son of Zeus, the spoils Heracles took from the Amazons and brought
to the god. There were pictures woven into it, like this. Heaven,
gathering the stars into the circle of the sky. The Sun driving his
horses toward the last of the flame, drawing the bright light of the
Evening Star behind him. Night in her black robe, driving her chariot
with a single pair of horses and no outrunners, and the stars keeping
the goddess company. The Pleiades going through the middle of the
sky, and Orion with his sword, and above them the Bear, wheeling her
golden tail round the pole. The full round moon that cuts the month
in half, shooting her light upward, and the Hyades, the clearest sign
a sailor has, and Dawn, the light-bringer, chasing the stars away.

On the walls he hung other weavings, barbarian work: ships with fine
oars facing Greek ships, and men who were half beast, and huntsmen on
horseback chasing deer, and hunts of wild lions. At the entrances,
Cecrops, coiling in his serpent spirals, with his daughters beside
him, the gift of some Athenian. And in the middle of the dining space
he set up gold mixing-bowls.

A herald stood up on tiptoe and announced that any man of the country
who wished could come to the feast. When the tent was full, they put
on their garlands and filled themselves with good rich food. And when
they had had their fill of the pleasure of it, the old man came forward
and took his stand in the middle of the floor, and he gave the diners
a great deal to laugh at with his busyness. He poured water for their
hands from the pitchers, he burned the sweat of the myrrh tree for
incense, he put himself in charge of the gold cups, all a job he had
assigned to himself. And when they came to the flutes and the shared
bowl, the old man said, "Away with these little wine-cups. Bring the
big ones, so these gentlemen can get to the pleasures of the mind a
little faster." Then there was hard work carrying round the cups of
beaten silver and of gold. And he picked out one cup in particular,
as though he were doing a favour to his new master, and handed it
full, and into the wine he had dropped what they say the mistress
gave him, a poison that does its work, so that the new son would leave
the light. Nobody knew any of this.

The son that had just been revealed was holding his libation in his
hands with the others when one of the household slaves let slip a word
of bad omen. And the boy, raised in a temple among good seers, took
it as a sign. He ordered a fresh bowl to be filled, and the first
libation to the god he gave to the ground, and he told everyone else
to pour theirs out. Silence fell. We filled the sacred bowls again
with water and with wine from Byblos. And while we were at it, a
winged revel of doves came tumbling into the tent, for in Apollo's
house they live without fear. When the men had poured out their wine,
the doves, thirsty, dipped their beaks into it and drew it up into
their fine-feathered throats. For all the others the god's libation
was harmless. But the one that settled where the new son had poured
his, and tasted the drink: at once she shook her feathered body and
went into a frenzy, and screamed out a cry that meant nothing, a
wailing, and the whole crowd of diners stared in astonishment at the
bird's agony. She died gasping, and her red claws went slack.

Then the son the oracle had given flung his arms bare out of his robes
across the table and shouted, "Which of you men was going to kill me?
Tell me, old man. The eagerness was all yours. It was from your hand I
took the cup." And at once he grabbed the old arm and searched him, to
catch the old man red-handed, with the thing on him. He was found out,
and forced to it, and slowly, unwillingly, he confessed Creusa's
daring and the trick of the cup. Straight away the young man Apollo's
oracle had given ran out, taking the diners with him, and stood before
the lords of Delphi and said, "Holy Earth, the daughter of Erechtheus,
a woman from another country, is killing me with poison." And the lords
of Delphi voted, not one vote against, that my mistress should die by
being thrown from the rock, for trying to kill the god's own boy, and
for planning a murder inside the sanctuary. Now the whole city is
hunting her, the wretched woman who rushed so wretchedly down that
road. She came to Apollo wanting children, and along with the children
she has lost her own life.
