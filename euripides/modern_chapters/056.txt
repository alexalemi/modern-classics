Rhesus
(Part 2 of 2)

Chorus.
	Whose watch is it? Who takes over from mine?
	The first stars are going down,
	and the seven Pleiades are climbing the sky.
	The Eagle is flying in the middle of heaven.
	Wake up. What are you waiting for?
	Up out of your blankets and on watch.
	Do you not see the moonlight?
	Dawn is close. Dawn is coming,
	and that star there is one of the ones
	that run out in front of it.
	Who was called for the first watch?
	Coroebus, they say. Mygdon's son.
	And who after him?
	The Paeonians woke the Cilicians,
	and the Mysians woke us.
	Then is it not time to go and wake the Lycians
	for the fifth watch, the way the lot fell?

Chorus.
	Listen. I hear it.
	Down by the Simois, sitting on her bloody nest,
	the nightingale who killed her own child
	sings her grief out with a voice of many strings.
	Already they are grazing the flocks on Ida.
	I can hear the pipe going in the dark.
	Sleep is charming my eyes shut.
	It comes sweetest on the lids toward morning.
	Why has the scout not come back,
	the one Hector sent out to spy on the ships?
	I am afraid. He has been gone a long time.
	Has he blundered into a hidden ambush
	and been killed?
	He may have. It frightens me.
	I say we go and wake the Lycians
	for the fifth watch, the way the lot fell.

(The sentries move off into the dark. Odysseus and Diomedes come in
from the other side, with their swords drawn.)

Odysseus.
Diomedes. Did you not hear something? Or is it an empty noise
dripping through my ears? Some clash of armour?

Diomedes.
No. It is the harness on the chariot rails, the iron rattling. Fear
went through me too, until I made out that it was the horses' chains
knocking.

Odysseus.
Watch you do not walk into the sentries in the dark.

Diomedes.
I will watch. I am setting my feet down carefully, even in the black.

Odysseus.
And if you do wake them, do you know the army's password?

Diomedes.
The word is Apollo. I know it; I heard Dolon give it.

Odysseus.
Ah. These beds here are empty. I am looking at the enemy's beds and
there is nobody in them.

Diomedes.
And yet Dolon told us this was where Hector sleeps. He is the man
this sword is drawn for.

Odysseus.
What can it mean? Has his company gone off somewhere?

Diomedes.
To set up some trick against us, perhaps.

Odysseus.
Hector is bold now that he is winning. Bold.

Diomedes.
Then what do we do, Odysseus? We have not found the man in his bed.
We have missed what we came for.

Odysseus.
We get back to the ships as fast as we can. Whichever of the gods it
is that keeps him lucky, that god is keeping him alive. We must not
force our luck.

Diomedes.
Then should we not go for Aeneas, or for Paris, the most hateful of
all the Phrygians, and take his head off with the sword?

Odysseus.
And how, in the dark, in the middle of an enemy army, will you hunt
those two down and kill them without any risk?

Diomedes.
Still, it is a shameful thing to go back to the Argive ships without
having done the enemy any fresh harm.

Odysseus.
What do you mean, none? Did we not kill Dolon, who was spying on the
moorings? Have we not got his gear here, safe? Or were you expecting
to sack the whole camp?

Diomedes.
You win. We go back. And may the luck hold.

(Athena appears in the dark above them.)

Athena.
Where are you going, leaving the Trojan lines, with grief biting at
your hearts because a god will not give you Hector or Paris to kill?
Have you not heard that a man has come to help Troy, and in no small
way? Rhesus. If he lives through this night into tomorrow, neither
Achilles nor the spear of Ajax could stop him from destroying the
whole Argive fleet — tearing down the palisade and driving a wide
charge with his spear right through the gates. Kill him and you have
everything. Leave Hector's bed alone, and the cutting of his throat.
His death will come from another hand.

Odysseus.
Lady Athena. I knew that voice; I know the sound of it well. You are
always there in my troubles, at my side, fighting for me. Tell us
where the man is asleep. Where in the barbarian army has he been
posted?

Athena.
He is lying close by here, and he is not drawn up with the rest of
the army. Hector has bedded him down outside the ranks until night
gives way to daylight. Near him his white horses are tethered to the
Thracian chariot, easy to pick out in the dark. They shine like the
wing of a river swan. Kill their master and take them home; they are
the finest spoils there are. There is no place on earth that hides a
team like that.

Odysseus.
Diomedes. Either you kill the Thracians, or hand that to me and take
the horses yourself.

Diomedes.
I will do the killing and you will handle the horses. You are
practised at the clever business and you have the quick mind for it.
A man should be put where he will do the most good.

Athena.
Look. I see Paris coming this way, toward us. He must have heard from
one of the guards some vague story that enemies have got in.

Diomedes.
Is he with others, or coming alone?

Athena.
Alone. He seems to be making for Hector's quarters, to report that
there are spies in the camp.

Diomedes.
Then should he not be the first one dead?

Athena.
You cannot get more than what is fated. It is not allowed that he
should die by your hand. Go, and be quick about the killing you did
come here to do. And I will pass myself off as Aphrodite, his ally,
standing beside him to help him in his trouble, and I will answer my
enemy with rotten words. That is what I mean to do. But the man it is
going to happen to knows nothing of it and has heard nothing, though
he is standing close enough to the words to touch them.

(Odysseus and Diomedes go off into the dark. Paris comes in from the
other side.)

Paris.
You there. I am talking to you, my general and my brother. Hector,
are you asleep? Should you not be up? Somebody hostile is edging in on
our army. Thieves, or spies of some kind.

Athena.
Courage. It is Aphrodite here, keeping watch over you, and kindly. I
take your war as my own business. I have not forgotten the honour you
did me, and I am grateful to you for the good turn. And now, with the
Trojan army winning, I have come bringing you a great friend: the
Thracian son of the goddess who makes the songs, the Muse. His father
is the river; they call him Strymon.

Paris.
You have always been good to this city, and to me. When I judged that
quarrel and gave you the prize, I laid up the greatest treasure of my
life for this city, and I say so. I came because I heard something —
not clearly, but a rumour has got in among the guards — that Achaean
spies are here. One man tells it who never saw them; another who did
see them come cannot say what they were. That is why I came to
Hector's quarters.

Athena.
Do not be afraid of anything. There is nothing new in the army.
Hector has gone off to bed the Thracian troops down.

Paris.
You convince me. I trust what you say, and I will go back to my post
and stand guard with no fear in me.

Athena.
Go. Take it that everything of yours is my concern; I like to see my
allies come off well. And you will find out for yourself how much I
want it. Now I am calling to you two over there, the pair of you with
far too much fight in you. Son of Laertes, put your sharpened swords
to sleep. The Thracian commander is down, the horses are taken, and
the enemy have noticed and are coming after you. Run for the ships'
moorings as fast as you can. Why are you waiting, with the storm
breaking over you, to save your lives?

(Athena goes. The sentries come running in out of the dark, and
Odysseus and Diomedes are among them.)

Chorus.
	Ah. Ah.
	Hit him, hit him, hit him, hit him.
	Strike. Strike.
	Who is that man? Look. That is the one I mean.
	Thieves. Those are the men stirring up this army in the dark.
	Here. Over here, all of you.
	I have them. I have them in my hands.
	What company is this? Where have you come from? Where are you from?

Odysseus.
That is not for you to know. You die today for the harm you have
done.

Chorus.
Give the password, before the spear goes through your chest.

Odysseus.
Halt. Steady.

Chorus.
Get in close. Everyone, strike.

Odysseus.
Was it you who killed Rhesus?

Chorus.
No. It was you, who came to kill us—

Odysseus.
Everyone, hold.

Chorus.
We will not.

Odysseus.
Ah. Do not strike a friend.

Chorus.
Then what is the sign?

Odysseus.
The word is Apollo.

Chorus.
Right. Everyone, hold your spear. Do you know where the men went?

Odysseus.
Somewhere over this way. We caught sight of them.

Chorus.
After them, all of you, on their tracks. Or should we raise the
alarm? No. It is an ugly thing to throw our own allies into a panic
in the night.

Chorus.
	Which of the men was it who got through?
	Who is it, so very bold,
	who will boast he slipped out of my hands?
	Where am I to find him?
	Who shall I put him next to,
	a man who came through the dark on a foot that never flinched,
	through the ranks and past the guard posts?
	A Thessalian?
	Someone who lives in one of the seaside towns of Locris?
	Or does he pick up a living out among the scattered islands?
	Who was he? Where from? What country?
	Which god does he call the highest of them all?
	Is this Odysseus's work, or somebody else's?
	If we are to judge by what he has done before, what else?
	You think so?
	Why would I not?
	He is bold enough against us.
	What strength? Who is it you are praising?
	Odysseus.
	Do not praise a thief's smooth-talking spear.

Chorus.
	He came into the city once before,
	with a running, bleary eye,
	wrapped in a rag of a coat,
	a sword on him, hidden in the folds.
	He crawled about begging his food like some hired vagrant,
	his head dusty and filthy.
	And he said a great deal of foul stuff
	about the royal house of the sons of Atreus,
	pretending he hated the generals.
	I wish he had died, died as he deserved,
	before he ever set a footprint on Phrygian ground.
	Odysseus or not, I am afraid.
	Hector will blame us, the sentries.
	Saying what?
	Suspecting us.
	Of doing what? What are you afraid of?
	That they got past us—
	Which men?
	The ones who came into the Phrygian army tonight.

(A man comes in out of the dark, holding his side.)

Charioteer.
	Io, io. The luck a god deals is heavy. Pheu, pheu.

Chorus.
Ah. Ah. Quiet, all of you, get down low. Somebody may be walking into
the net.

Charioteer.
Io, io. A heavy disaster for the Thracians.

Chorus.
That is one of the allies, groaning.

Charioteer.
	Io, io.
	I am destroyed. And you, lord of the Thracians,
	you who ever laid eyes on Troy, the hatefullest sight of your life,
	what an ending has taken hold of you.

Chorus.
Who are you? One of the allies? In the night my eyes are dull and I
cannot make you out clearly.

Charioteer.
	Where can I find one of the Trojan lords?
	Where is Hector sleeping,
	bedded down under his shield?
	Which of the officers of this army do I tell
	what has been done to us,
	what somebody did to us and then went off unseen,
	after winding up a grief for the Thracians
	that anybody can see?

Chorus.
Something bad seems to have come on the Thracian army, from what I
can make out of this man.

Charioteer.
	The army is gone. The king is down,
	killed by a treacherous blow.
	Ah. Ah. Ah. Ah.
	What pain grinds at me, deep inside
	the bloody hole of the wound. How can I die?
	Was this what Rhesus and I had to die for,
	with no glory, after putting in at Troy to help?

Chorus.
There are no riddles in the trouble he is telling. He says plainly
that our allies are dead.

Charioteer.
It has gone badly, and on top of badly it has gone shamefully, and
that makes the evil twice the size. To die well, if a man has to die,
is still bitter for the one dying — how could it not be? — but for the
living it is weight and a good name for the house. We died stupidly
and with no glory at all.

When Hector's own hand had bedded us down and given us the password,
we slept, beaten flat by the march. No guard was set over the army, no
watches through the night. Our weapons were not laid out in their
places, and no goads were fitted on the horses' yokes, because our
king had been told that you were on top and lying in wait by the
sterns of their ships. So we simply dropped where we were and slept.

I woke out of sleep with my mind on my work, and I was measuring out
the horses' feed with a generous hand, expecting to yoke them at dawn
for the fighting. And through the thick dark I see two men going
round and round our camp. When I stirred, they ducked down and drew
off again. I shouted at them not to come near the army. I thought
they were thieves out of our own allies. They said nothing. And I
said nothing more either. I went back to my bed and slept again.

And a sort of vision came to me in my sleep. The horses I had reared
and used to drive, standing beside Rhesus, I saw them — thinking I
was dreaming — with wolves up on their backs, sitting there as if
they belonged. The wolves were lashing the horses' hide with their
tails and driving them on, and the mares were snorting through their
nostrils, breathing rage, tossing their manes in terror. I sprang up
to beat the beasts off my horses, because the fear of the night had
me on my feet.

And as I lifted my head I heard the choking of dying men. A hot
spring of new blood hit me, from my master, from his cut throat, out
of his hard dying. I come up straight, with nothing in my hands, no
spear. And while I am peering about, hunting for a weapon, a man in
his prime steps up beside me and drives a sword into my lower ribs. I
felt the blade go in and take a deep furrow of a wound out of me. I
went down on my face. And they took the horses and the chariot and ran
with them.

Ah. Ah.

The pain grinds at me. I cannot stand up straight any more.

I know what has happened, because I saw it. But how the dead men died
I cannot tell you, or by whose hand. Though I can guess this much:
what we suffered came from friends.

Chorus.
Charioteer of the Thracian who has come off so badly, do not suspect
us. The enemy did this. And here is Hector himself, coming. He has
heard what happened to you, and he shares the pain of it, as he
should.

Hector.
You. You who have done us this enormous damage. How did the enemy's
spies come in on you without your knowing, to your shame? How was the
army butchered, and you never pushed them out, going in or coming
out? Who is going to pay the penalty for this if not you? You, I say.
You were the guard set over the army. And they are gone, without a
scratch, laughing themselves sick at Phrygian cowardice and at me,
their general. Now understand this, and I swear it by Zeus my father:
either the whip is waiting for you, or the block and the axe, for
doing a thing like this. Or else you can count Hector as nothing, and
a coward.

Chorus.
	Io, io.
	It was to you, yes, to you,
	the strength that holds this city up,
	that I came running,
	when I brought you the news that there were fires
	burning by the ships.
	Through the whole night I never put my sleepless eye
	to bed, I never dozed.
	By the springs of the Simois I swear it.
	Do not set your anger on me, my lord.
	I am guilty of none of this.
	And if in time you find out that I said
	or did one thing out of place,
	send me under the ground alive.
	I will not ask you for anything.

Charioteer.
Why are you threatening these men? Why are you working on my
barbarian judgement with your woven words — you, a barbarian
yourself? You did this. Nobody else would be accepted for it, not by
the dead and not by us who are wounded. You will need a long, clever
speech before you talk me out of believing you killed your friends.
You fell in love with the horses, and for their sake you murder your
own allies, after all the pressing you did to make them come. They
came. They are dead. Paris shamed a host's table more decently than
you have, killing your allies.

And do not tell me some Argive came and destroyed us. Who could have
got over the Trojan lines and reached us, and not been seen? You were
camped in front of us, you and the Phrygian army. Who was wounded,
who was killed among your allies, when these enemies you talk of came
through? We were the ones wounded, out on the edge, and the ones who
got worse than that are not looking at the sunlight any more. To put
it simply: we blame no Achaean. What enemy could have come through
the dark and found where Rhesus lay on the bare ground, unless some
god pointed the way for the killers? They did not even know he had
arrived. No. You engineered this.

Hector.
I have had allies for as long as the Achaean army has been in this
land, and I have never heard a wrong word said about me by any of
them. You would be the first. Never let a longing for horses take
hold of me like that, so that I would kill my friends. And this is
Odysseus. Who else of the Argives would ever have done such a thing,
or planned it? I am afraid of the man, and something in my mind is
troubled: that he may have run into Dolon as well and killed him. He
has been gone a long time now and there is no sign of him.

Charioteer.
I do not know these Odysseuses of yours. It was no enemy's hand that
struck us.

Hector.
Then believe that, if that is how it looks to you.

Charioteer.
Land of my fathers, if only I could die in you.

Hector.
Do not die. There is a big enough crowd of dead already.

Charioteer.
Where am I to turn, with my master gone?

Hector.
My house will take you in and heal you.

Charioteer.
And how will the hands of the murderers nurse me?

Hector.
This man will never stop telling the same story.

Charioteer.
Curse the man who did it. My tongue is not pointing at you, whatever
you boast. But Justice knows how it stands.

Hector.
Take hold of him. Get him into my house, and see to it he has nothing
to complain of. And you men, go to the people on the walls, to Priam
and the elders, and tell them to bury the dead at the turnings off
the highway.

Chorus.
	Why is some other god bringing Troy
	back out of her great good luck
	into grief again? What is he planting?
	Ah. Ah. Oh. Oh.
	What goddess is that above our heads, my king,
	carrying the newly killed body
	in her two arms?
	I am afraid, looking at this pain.

(The Muse appears above, holding her son's body.)

Muse.
You may look, Trojans. I am the Muse, one of the sisters, the one who
is honoured among clever men, and I am here because I have seen my
son, my darling, killed pitifully by his enemies. And the man who
killed him, Odysseus the trickster, will in time pay the penalty he
owes.

Muse.
	With a dirge out of my own country,
	child, I am mourning you,
	you, your mother's pain.
	What a road you put in on, coming to Troy,
	an unlucky road, a miserable one.
	You went against me, and I told you not to go;
	you forced your way past your father, who begged you.
	Oh, my grief for you.
	Dear head, dear head, my child. Oh.

Chorus.
As far as anyone can who has no share of your blood, I pity your son
and I grieve for him.

Muse.
	Curse Diomedes, who came out of Oeneus's house,
	curse Odysseus, the son of Laertes.
	They have left me childless
	of the finest child a mother ever bore.
	And curse the woman who left a Greek house
	and sailed to a Phrygian bed under Ilion.
	She killed you, dearest, for Troy's sake,
	and she emptied a thousand cities
	of good men.

Muse.
Thamyris, son of Philammon, there was much you did to my heart while
you lived, and much when you had gone down to Hades. It was your
outrage that tripped you, and your quarrel with the Muses, and it is
what made me the mother of this unlucky child. For I was crossing the
running water and I came too near to Strymon's fertile bed, that time
we Muses came to the crag of Pangaeus with the gold in its soil, all
our instruments with us, for the greatest contest of song there ever
was, against that clever Thracian. And we blinded him. Thamyris, who
had said so much contempt about our craft.

And when I gave birth to you, I was ashamed in front of my sisters,
and ashamed of the virginity I had lost, and I put you out into the
eddies of your father, the beautiful water. And Strymon did not hand
you over to mortal hands to be raised, but to the girls of the
springs. There you were brought up as beautifully as anyone could
be, by the nymphs, and you ruled Thrace, the first of men, my child.
While you were arming yourself for the bloody fighting round your own
country I never feared you would die. But I told you never to put in
at the city of Troy, because I knew what was fated for you. And
Hector's embassies and those endless councils of elders talked you
into coming and helping your friends.

And you, Athena, are the cause of every part of this death. Odysseus
did nothing, and the son of Tydeus did nothing, for all that they did
it. Do not imagine it has escaped me. And yet we the sister Muses
honour your city above all others and haunt your land. It was Orpheus
who showed men the torches of the mysteries that must not be spoken
of, and Orpheus is this dead man's own cousin, the man you are
killing. And Musaeus, your own holy citizen, who went further in
learning than any one man ever went, was trained by Apollo and by us,
his sisters. And here is my wages for it. I am holding my child in my
arms and singing over him. I will not be bringing you another
teacher.

Chorus.
So the Thracian driver was wrong to abuse us, Hector, for planning
this man's murder.

Hector.
I knew that. It needed no prophets to tell me the man had died by
Odysseus's arts. And when I saw the Greek army camped in my land, of
course I was going to send heralds to my friends and ask them to come
and help this country. I sent. He came, as he owed it to me, to share
the work. But I take no pleasure at all in his being dead. And now I
am ready to build him a tomb and burn a great luxury of robes on his
fire. He came here as a friend, and he goes away out of bad luck.

Muse.
He will not go down into the black floor of the earth. This much I
will ask of the bride below, the daughter of Demeter who makes the
crops grow: to let his soul go free. She owes me, and she should show
that she honours the friends of Orpheus. For me, from now on, he will
be as one dead, one who does not look at the light. He will never
come to me again, and he will never see his mother's body. He will
lie hidden in the caves of that silver-veined ground, a man who is
also a god, and looking at daylight, the way the prophet of Dionysus
came to live under the rock of Pangaeus, a holy god to the people who
know about him.

The sea goddess's grief I will carry more easily now, because her son
has to die as well. First we sisters will sing you in our dirges, and
after you Achilles, on the day Thetis mourns. Pallas Athena, who
killed you, will not save him. That is the kind of arrow Apollo keeps
in his quiver for him.

Oh, the disasters that come of having children. The trouble human
beings carry. Whoever adds it up honestly will go through life
without children, and will not bury the children he had.

Chorus.
This man is his mother's business now, hers to bury. But you, Hector,
if you mean to do anything about what is in front of you, now is the
time. This is daylight.

Hector.
Move. Tell the allies to arm at once and to fill the yokes with the
horses' necks. They are to hold their torches and wait for the
Etruscan trumpet's call. I am confident that I will get over their
army and over their wall and throw fire into the Achaean ships, and
carry this coming ray of the sun to the Trojans as the day they are
made free.

Chorus.
	Do as the king says.
	Let us go and put our armour on
	and pass the orders to the allies.
	Perhaps the god who is on our side
	will give us the victory.

(They go out. The sun is up.)
