Heracles
(Part 2 of 3)

(Heracles comes in along the road, carrying his bow and his club. Megara
sees him first.)

Megara.
Ah. Old man, am I looking at the people I love most? Or what am I to
say?

Amphitryon.
I do not know, daughter. I cannot find my voice either.

Megara.
This is the man they told us was under the earth. Unless we are seeing
some dream in broad daylight. What am I saying? What kind of dreams am I
seeing, in my fear? This is nobody else, old man. This is your son. Come
here, children, hang on to your father's clothes. Go on, quickly, and do
not let go. He is every bit as good to you as Zeus the Saviour himself.

Heracles.
Greetings, my house, and the gates of my hearth. How glad I am to see
you, coming back into the light. Ah. What is this? I see my children in
front of the house with their heads wreathed in the dress of the dead,
and my wife in a crowd of men, and my father in tears. What has
happened? Let me go closer to them and find out. Wife, what new trouble
has come to the house?

Megara.
Dearest of men.

Amphitryon.
Light, come back to your father.

Megara.
You are here? You are safe? You have come in time for the people you
love?

Heracles.
What are you saying? What is this confusion I have walked into, father?

Megara.
We are being destroyed. Old man, forgive me if I have snatched first
what was yours to tell him. A woman is somehow quicker to cry than a
man, and it was my children who were dying, and I was dying with them.

Heracles.
Apollo. What an opening to your story.

Megara.
My brothers are dead, and my old father.

Heracles.
What? What did he do? Whose spear did he meet?

Megara.
Lycus killed them. The new master of the country.

Heracles.
In open battle, or was the land sick?

Megara.
Civil war. He holds the power now, over Cadmus's city of the seven
gates.

Heracles.
Then why did the fear come to you and the old man?

Megara.
He was going to kill your father, and me, and the children.

Heracles.
What? What did he have to fear from my fatherless children?

Megara.
That one day they would make him pay for Creon's death.

Heracles.
And this dress the children are wearing, fit for the dead?

Megara.
We have already put on the wrappings of death.

Heracles.
And you were being put to death by force? Oh, this is hard to bear.

Megara.
With no friends left. And we heard that you were dead.

Heracles.
Where did that despair come from? Who told you?

Megara.
The heralds of Eurystheus kept announcing it.

Heracles.
And why did you leave my house and my hearth?

Megara.
By force. Your father was thrown out of his bed.

Heracles.
And he had no shame? He could treat an old man like that?

Megara.
Shame? He lives a long way from that goddess.

Heracles.
Were we so short of friends while I was away?

Megara.
What friends does a man have when his luck turns?

Heracles.
The battles I fought against the Minyans. They spat on those?

Megara.
Bad luck has no friends. I will say it to you again.

Heracles.
Throw those wrappings of Hades off your heads, and look up at the light.
Instead of the dark down there, your eyes are getting a welcome
exchange. And I, since now this is work for my own hand, will first go
and tear down the house of the new king, cut off his unholy head and
throw it to the dogs to drag about. And every Theban I find who turned
bad after I did him good, I will bring down with this club that has won
so many victories. The rest I will scatter with feathered arrows and
fill the whole Ismenus with the corpses of the dead, and the white
stream of Dirce will run with blood. Who should I defend, if not my wife
and my children and my old father? Goodbye to my labours. I did them for
nothing, if I did them instead of this. And I must die defending them,
if they were going to die for their father. Or what will we call fine in
going to fight a hydra and a lion on Eurystheus's orders, if I do not
work to save my own children from death? Then I will not be called
Heracles the glorious victor any more, the way I was before.

Chorus.
It is right that children help the people who made them, and an old
father, and the woman who shares a man's marriage.

Amphitryon.
It is like you, my boy, to be a friend to your friends and to hate what
is hateful. But do not be in too much of a hurry.

Heracles.
And what am I doing faster than I should, father?

Amphitryon.
The king has a lot of allies, poor men who pass for rich by the way they
talk. They started the civil war and wrecked the city so they could
plunder their neighbours, because what they had at home was gone, spent,
run through by idleness. You were seen coming into the city. And since
you were seen, watch out that you do not gather your enemies together
and fall when you never expected it.

Heracles.
I do not care if the whole city saw me. But I saw a bird sitting in an
unlucky quarter of the sky, and I knew some trouble had fallen on the
house. So I came into the country secretly, on purpose.

Amphitryon.
Good. Now go in and greet your hearth, and let your father's house see
your face. The king is coming here himself, to drag out your wife and
children and butcher them, and to cut my throat over them. If you stay
right here, everything will fall into your hands, and you gain safety
into the bargain. And do not stir up your city before you have settled
this well, child.

Heracles.
I will do that. You are right. I will go into the house. I have come
back at last from the sunless corners of Hades and of Persephone, down
below, and I will not fail to greet the gods under my own roof first.

Amphitryon.
Did you really go to the house of Hades, child?

Heracles.
Yes, and I brought the three-headed beast up into the light.

Amphitryon.
Did you beat him in a fight, or was he a gift from the goddess?

Heracles.
A fight. And I was lucky: I had already seen the rites of the mysteries,
the ones the initiated see at Eleusis.

Amphitryon.
And is the beast in Eurystheus's house now?

Heracles.
The grove of Demeter of the Underworld has him, and the town of
Hermione.

Amphitryon.
And Eurystheus does not know you have come back up onto the earth?

Heracles.
He does not. I came here first, to find out how things stood.

Amphitryon.
And why were you so long under the earth?

Heracles.
I stayed to bring Theseus back out of Hades, father.

Amphitryon.
And where is he? Has he gone off to his own country?

Heracles.
He has gone to Athens, glad to have escaped from down below. But come,
children, walk into the house with your father. Your going in is going
to be a better thing than your coming out was. Take heart, and stop
letting the streams run from your eyes. And you, wife, gather your
courage, stop trembling, and let go of my clothes. I have no wings, and
I have no wish to run away from the people I love. Ah. They will not let
go. They cling to my clothes all the harder. Were you standing so close
to the edge of the razor? Then I will take these little boats by the
hand and tow them along, the way a ship tows its dinghies. I do not
refuse to take care of my children. Everything human is the same. The
best of men love their children, and so do men who are nothing at all.
Money makes them different, some have it and some do not, but the whole
race loves its children.

(Heracles goes into the house, leading the children by the hand. Megara
and Amphitryon follow him.)

Chorus.
	Youth is what I love, always.
	Old age is a weight on my head,
	heavier than the crags of Etna,
	and it lays a dark veil
	over the light of my eyes.
	Never let me have the wealth
	of an Asian king's throne,
	never a house piled with gold,
	if the price is my youth.
	Youth is the loveliest thing there is when you are rich,
	and the loveliest when you are poor.
	But old age, grim and deadly,
	I hate it. Let it sink under the waves.
	It should never have come
	to the houses and cities of men.
	It should be out over the sky forever,
	carried along on wings.

Chorus.
	If the gods had any understanding,
	any wisdom, as men reckon such things,
	they would have given a second youth,
	a visible mark of goodness,
	to whoever had goodness in him, and when they died
	they would have run back up into the sunlight
	on a second lap of the course,
	while a bad nature had to make do
	with a single stretch of life.
	That way you could tell
	the bad from the good,
	the way sailors can count the stars
	through a gap in the clouds.
	As it is, the gods have set no clear line
	between good men and bad.
	Time turns and turns,
	and only piles up wealth.

Chorus.
	I will not stop mixing the Graces
	in with the Muses,
	the sweetest pairing there is.
	Let me not live without music.
	Let me always wear a garland.
	The old singer still sings
	of Memory, the mother of the Muses.
	I still sing the glorious victory
	of Heracles,
	beside Dionysus the giver of wine,
	beside the song of the seven-stringed shell
	and the Libyan pipe.
	Not yet will I put down the Muses,
	who set me dancing.

Chorus.
	The girls of Delos sing their hymn
	around the temple gates
	for Leto's lovely son,
	whirling in their beautiful dance.
	And I, an old singer, like a swan,
	will sing my hymns at your doors
	out of a grey-bearded throat.
	There is good matter here for a song:
	he is the son of Zeus.
	But his goodness rises
	higher than his birth. He laboured,
	and he made life calm for men,
	and killed the beasts they were afraid of.

(Lycus comes in with his men. Amphitryon comes out of the house to meet
him.)

Lycus.
Amphitryon. You are coming out of the house just in time. It is a long
while now that you have been dressing your bodies in the clothes and
ornaments of the dead. Come on. Tell the children and the wife of
Heracles to show themselves outside this house, on the terms you
offered, of your own free will, to die.

Amphitryon.
My lord, you hound me when I am already broken, and you heap outrage on
me on top of my dead. You should be more measured in your eagerness,
even if you do hold the power. But since you lay the necessity of dying
on us, we have to accept it. What you decide has to be done.

Lycus.
Where is Megara, then? Where are the children of Alcmene's son?

Amphitryon.
I think she, as far as I can guess from out here.

Lycus.
What is this guess of yours? What proof have you got?

Amphitryon.
Is sitting as a suppliant at the holy steps of the hearth.

Lycus.
Begging, and getting nothing for it. It will not save her life.

Amphitryon.
And calling on her dead husband. For nothing.

Lycus.
Who is not here, and who will never come.

Amphitryon.
No. Unless some god should raise him up.

Lycus.
Go to her and bring her out of the house.

Amphitryon.
If I did that I would be a partner in the murder.

Lycus.
Then since you have that on your conscience, we will do it, we who are
past being afraid. We will bring the children out, and their mother with
them. Follow me, men. Let us cut this delay short and get the work done,
and gladly.

(Lycus goes into the house with his men.)

Amphitryon.
Go on, then. Go where you have to go. The rest, perhaps, someone else
will see to. You have done evil; expect some evil to come to you. Old
men, he is walking in beautifully. He is going to be tangled in the
nooses of a net with swords in it, when he thought he was going to kill
his neighbours, the vilest man alive. I am going in to watch him fall
dead. There is pleasure in an enemy dying and paying the penalty for
what he has done.

(Amphitryon goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	The evil has changed sides. The great king of before
	turns his life back round, up out of Hades.
	Io.
	Justice, and the tide of the gods, turning back.
	You have come at last to where you will pay for it with your death,
	for the outrages you did to your betters.
	Joy has made my tears break out.
	He has come back,
	the thing I never once, in my heart, hoped
	to live to see: the king of this land.
	But come, old friends, let us watch what is going on inside the house too,
	and see whether a certain man is faring the way I want him to.

Lycus.
	Io moi moi.

Chorus.
	This is the song, beginning now, that I love
	to hear inside the house. Death is not far off.
	The king cries out,
	groaning the first notes of his murder.

Lycus.
	Oh, all the land of Cadmus, I am being killed by a trick.

Chorus.
	Yes, because you killed. Endure it, as you pay it back,
	pay the penalty for what you did.
	Who was he, a mortal man, who stained the gods with his lawlessness,
	who threw a mindless slander
	at the blessed ones in heaven, that the gods, after all,
	have no strength?
	Old men, the godless man is no more.
	The house is silent. Let us turn to the dance.
	The friends I want to prosper are prospering.

Chorus.
	Dances, dances
	and feasting are what matter now
	all through the holy town of Thebes.
	A change from tears,
	a change of fortune,
	has given birth to new songs.
	The new king is gone, and the old one
	rules, back from the harbour of Acheron.
	Hope has come
	beyond anything I counted on.

Chorus.
	The gods, the gods
	take care to notice the unjust
	and the holy.
	Gold and good luck
	drag men out of their right minds
	and haul unjust power along after them.
	No one dared to look at what Time brings round again.
	He left the law behind, he gave his favour to lawlessness,
	and he smashed
	the black chariot of his prosperity.

Chorus.
	Ismenus, put on your garlands.
	Polished streets of the seven-gated city,
	break out into dancing,
	and Dirce with your lovely stream,
	and you, the daughters of Asopus,
	leave your father's water and come and sing with us,
	nymphs, of the glorious victory
	Heracles has won.
	Wooded rock of Pythian Apollo,
	and you houses of the Muses on Helicon,
	oh, come with glad shouting
	to my city, to my walls,
	where the Sown Men sprang out of the ground,
	the crop that grew from the dragon's teeth Cadmus planted,
	a troop with bronze shields, who hand this land
	down to their children's children,
	a holy light for Thebes.

Chorus.
	Oh, the two beds that shared one marriage,
	a mortal's and Zeus's, who came to the bed
	of the bride descended from Perseus.
	How I believe now, Zeus, in that old story
	of your bed; it turned out true beyond my hope,
	and time has shown bright and clear
	the strength of Heracles,
	who came up out of the earth's chambers,
	leaving the house of Pluto
	down below.
	You are a better king to me
	than that low-born lord,
	as anyone can see now
	from this contest of drawn swords, which showed
	whether justice still pleases
	the gods.

(Iris and Madness appear above the house.)

Chorus.
Ah. Ah. Have we come into the same fit of fear again, old men? What is
that thing I see over the house? Run, run. Lift your slow legs and get
out of the way. Lord Healer, turn this disaster away from me.

Iris.
Courage, old men. The one you are looking at is Madness, the daughter of
Night, and I am Iris, the servant of the gods. We have not come to do
the city any harm. Our campaign is against one man's body, the man they
say was born from Zeus and Alcmene. Until he had finished his bitter
labours, what had to be kept him safe, and his father Zeus never let me
or Hera hurt him. But now that he has come through the tasks Eurystheus
set him, Hera wants to fasten fresh blood on him: he is to kill his own
children. And I want it too. Come, then, daughter of black Night, who
never married, take your heart and make it hard. Drive madness into this
man, a child-killing confusion of the mind, and set his feet leaping.
Drive him, shake him, let out the murderous sail. When he has ferried
his fine garland of children across the strait of Acheron, killed by his
own hand, then let him know what Hera's anger against him is like, and
learn what mine is. Otherwise the gods are nowhere, and mortal men will
be great, if he does not pay.

Madness.
I come from a noble father and a noble mother. I am of the blood of
Night and of Heaven. And the office I hold is not one to turn against
friends in anger, and I take no joy in visiting the houses of men. I
want to give Hera some advice, before I see her stumble, and you too, if
you will listen to what I say. This man, whose house you are sending me
into, is no nobody, on earth or among the gods. He tamed the trackless
country and the wild sea, and by himself he set the honours of the gods
back on their feet when they were falling, brought down by unholy men.
So I advise you: do not wish great evils on him.

Iris.
Do not lecture us about Hera's plans and mine.

Madness.
I am setting your foot on the better path instead of the evil one.

Iris.
It was not to keep yourself in hand that the wife of Zeus sent you here.

Madness.
I call the Sun to witness that I am doing what I do not want to do. But
if I really must serve Hera and you, and follow you at full speed, in
full cry, like hounds after the huntsman, then I will go. Not the sea,
groaning so wildly with its waves, not the earthquake, not the sting of
the thunderbolt breathing out its pangs, will be anything like the
sprint I am going to make into the chest of Heracles. I will smash the
roof and bring the house down on top of him, once I have killed his
children first. And the killer will not know that he is cutting down the
children he fathered, until my madness lets him go. Look, there. Already
he is tossing his head at the starting line, and rolling his eyes,
twisted, glaring like a Gorgon, without a word. He cannot keep his
breathing in hand; he is like a bull about to charge, terrible, and he
bellows, calling up the Fates out of Tartarus. Soon I will make you
dance harder still, and pipe terror into your ears. Go up to Olympus,
Iris, lift your noble foot. I am going down into the house of Heracles,
where no one will see me.

(Iris goes. Madness goes down into the house.)

Chorus.
	Ototototoi. Groan. He is being cut down,
	the flower of your city, the son of Zeus.
	Poor Greece, you are going to throw away
	the man who did you good, and destroy him, dancing
	to the mad pipes of Madness.
	She is off in her chariot, the lady of many groans,
	she puts the goad to her horses
	as if to outrage them,
	the Gorgon daughter of Night, with the hissing
	of a hundred snake heads, Madness with the flashing eyes.
	Quickly the god has turned his good luck over,
	quickly the children will breathe out their lives at their father's hand.

Amphitryon.
	Io moi. Wretched man that I am.

Chorus.
	Io, Zeus. Your child will have no children now;
	the mad, unjust Avengers who eat raw flesh
	are about to lay him out flat in misery.

Amphitryon.
	Io, roof of my house.

Chorus.
	The dance begins, without the drums,
	without the wand Dionysus loves,

Amphitryon.
	Io, my house.

Chorus.
	for blood, and not for the pouring
	of Dionysus's wine, the juice of the grape.

Amphitryon.
	Run, children. Get out of there.

Chorus.
	Deadly, this
	deadly tune the pipe is playing.
	He is hunting his children down like game. Never in vain
	will Madness run wild through a house.

Amphitryon.
	Aiai. The evil of it.

Chorus.
	Aiai indeed. How I groan for the old man,
	the father, and for the mother who raised the children,
	who bore them for nothing.
	Look. Look.
	A storm is shaking the house. The roof is falling in.

Heracles.
	E, e. What are you doing, daughter of Zeus, to my house?
	You are sending the turmoil of Tartarus into my house, Pallas,
	the way you did once against Enceladus.

(The Messenger comes out of the house.)

Messenger.
	Old men, bodies white with age.

Chorus.
	What is this cry you are calling me
	with?

Messenger.
	Unspeakable things in the house.

Chorus.
	I need no other
	prophet to tell me that.

Messenger.
	The children are dead.

Chorus.
	Aiai.

Messenger.
	Groan. There is reason to groan.

Chorus.
	Deadly murders,
	deadly hands of a parent. Oh.

Messenger.
	No one could say more than what we have suffered.

Chorus.
	How can you show us this ruin, this ruin of a father
	over his children, that we must groan for?
	Tell us how these evils rushed down, sent from a god,
	onto the house,
	and how the children met what they suffered.

Messenger.
The sacrifice was set out in front of the altar of Zeus, to purify the
house, because Heracles had killed the king of the land and thrown him
out of this house. The children stood there, a beautiful group, with his
father and Megara. The basket had already gone round the altar, and we
were keeping the holy silence. Alcmene's son was about to take up the
torch in his right hand, to dip it in the water of purification, when he
stood still without a word. And as their father went on standing there,
the children looked up at him. He was not himself any more. He was gone,
ruined, in the rolling of his eyes; the roots of his eyes stood out
bloodshot, and foam ran down into his thick beard. And he spoke, with a
laugh that had gone wrong. "Father, why am I sacrificing and lighting
the purifying fire before I have killed Eurystheus? Why have the work
twice over? It is a job for one hand of mine to settle all this. When I
bring the head of Eurystheus back here, I will wash my hands clean for
the men who have just died, and for him together. Pour out the water.
Throw the baskets out of your hands. Who is giving me my bow? Who is
giving me the weapon for my hand? I am going to Mycenae. I need crowbars
and pickaxes, to smash back down with bent iron the foundations the
Cyclopes fitted together with their red plumb-line and their hammers."

Then he set off. He had no chariot, but he said he had one, and he
stepped up onto the rail of a chariot that was not there and struck with
his hand, as if he were holding a goad. The servants felt two things at
once, laughter and fear together. And one man looked at another and
said, "Is the master playing with us, or is he mad?" And he went pacing
up and down through the house, and burst into the middle of the men's
hall and said he had reached the city of Nisus, Megara, on the road
south. Then, going further inside, he lay down on the floor, just as he
was, and set about preparing himself a feast. And after he had passed a
short while there, he said he was coming to the wooded flats of the
Isthmus. And there he stripped his body of its clothes and their
fastenings and wrestled with nobody, and had himself proclaimed the
glorious victor over no one, by himself, to himself, after calling for
silence from a crowd that was not there. Then, roaring terrible threats
at Eurystheus, he was in Mycenae, by his own account. But his father
took hold of his strong hand and said this to him. "My son, what is the
matter with you? What is this strange journey you are on? It is not the
blood of the men you have just killed that has driven you into this
frenzy?" But he thought it was the father of Eurystheus, frightened,
touching his hand to beg for mercy, and he pushed him away, and got his
quiver ready, and his bow, against his own children, thinking he was
killing the sons of Eurystheus. And they were terrified, and bolted, one
this way and one that: one into the dress of his poor mother, one into
the shadow of a pillar, and the other crouched under the altar like a
bird. And their mother screamed, "You, their father, what are you doing?
Are you killing your children?" And the old man screamed, and the whole
crowd of slaves.

But he chased the boy round and round the pillar, a terrible circling of
feet, and came face to face with him and shot him in the liver. He fell
on his back, and soaked the stone pillars with his blood as he breathed
out his life. And Heracles shouted in triumph and boasted. "There. One
of Eurystheus's chicks is dead, fallen at my feet, paying for his
father's hatred." Then he aimed the bow at another, who had crouched
down at the base of the altar, thinking he was hidden. But the poor boy
got to him first. He fell at his father's knees and threw his hand up to
his beard and his neck, and said, "Dearest father, do not kill me. I am
yours. I am your boy. It is not Eurystheus's son you are killing." But
he turned that wild Gorgon eye on him, and since the boy was standing
too close for the deadly bow, he lifted his club over his head, like a
smith striking hot iron, and brought it down on the child's fair head
and broke the bones. With the second boy taken, he goes for the third,
to butcher him on top of the other two. But the poor mother was too
quick for him. She snatched the child away inside the house and shut the
doors. And he, as if he really were at the walls the Cyclopes built, dug
and levered at the doors, tore the doorposts out, and laid his wife and
his child flat with a single arrow. Then he goes galloping off to kill
the old man. But an image came, or so it looked to us who were watching:
Pallas, shaking her spear, the crest of her helmet nodding, and she
threw a rock at Heracles's chest, and it stopped him in the middle of
his frenzy for killing and sent him down into sleep. He fell to the
floor, and hit his back against a pillar that had broken in two when the
roof fell in and was lying across its base. Then we got our feet free of
running away, and with the old man we tied him with bonds of knotted
rope to the pillar, so that when he woke from his sleep he could not add
anything to what he had done. He is sleeping, poor man, a sleep that is
no blessing, after murdering his children and his wife. And I do not
know any mortal more wretched than he is.
