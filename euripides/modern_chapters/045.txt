Orestes
(Part 3 of 4)

Electra.
Women, has poor Orestes gone out of the house? Has the madness the
gods sent got the better of him?

Chorus.
Not at all. He has gone to the people of Argos, to stand the trial
that has been set for his life, the one where you and he must live or
die.

Electra.
Oh. What has he done? Who talked him into it?

Chorus.
Pylades. But here is a messenger, and it looks as if he will soon be
telling us what happened there to your brother.

(An old man comes in from the direction of the town.)

Messenger.
Poor girl. Unhappy daughter of Agamemnon the general, my lady Electra,
listen to the news I have come to bring you. It is bad.

Electra.
Aiai. Then we are finished. Your words make it plain. You have come
with bad news, by the look of you.

Messenger.
The Argives have voted. Your brother is to die, and you too, poor
girl, this very day.

Electra.
Oh. So it has come, the thing I expected. I have been afraid of it for
so long, wasting away with weeping for what was going to happen. But
what was the trial like? What did the Argives say that brought us down
and confirmed our deaths? Tell me, old friend. Is it stones that will
knock the breath out of me, or the knife? Since I share my brother's
misfortunes, whatever they are.

Messenger.
I happened to be coming in from the country, through the gates, wanting
to find out how things stood with you and with Orestes. I was always
well disposed to your father, and your house fed me: a poor man, but a
loyal one where his friends were concerned. And I saw a crowd on the
move, going up to sit on the hill where they say Danaus first gathered
the people into a public assembly, when he was on trial against
Aegyptus. When I saw the crowd I asked one of the townsmen, "What is
happening in Argos? Has some news from the enemy got the city of Danaus
all in a flutter?" And he said, "Don't you see Orestes there, coming
this way, on his way to a race where the prize is his life?" And then I
saw a sight I never expected, and wish I had never seen: Pylades and
your brother coming along together, the one with his head hanging,
slack with sickness, and the other grieving for his friend as if he
were his own brother, nursing him along like a tutor with a child.

When the crowd of Argives was complete, a herald stood up and said,
"Who wishes to speak on the question, whether Orestes should be put to
death or not, for killing his mother?" And at that Talthybius got up,
the man who was with your father when he sacked the Phrygians. He is
always on the side of whoever holds the power, and he spoke to face
both ways. He was full of admiration for your father, and then he had
nothing good to say about your brother, winding fine words and foul
words together: that Orestes was setting up a bad precedent about how
to treat parents. And the whole time his eye kept flicking, bright and
friendly, towards the friends of Aegisthus. That is what the breed is
like. Heralds are always jumping across to whoever is on top. Their
friend is the man who has the power in the city and the office to go
with it.

After him lord Diomedes spoke. He said they should kill neither you nor
your brother, but punish you with exile and keep themselves clean
before the gods. Some shouted that this was well said, and others did
not like it. And then up gets a man with no door on his mouth, a man
whose strength is his cheek. An Argive, but no Argive, a man they had
been forced to take in. He trusts in noise and in the ignorant freedom
of his own tongue, and he is just persuasive enough to get them into
some serious trouble one of these days. Because when a man who talks
pleasantly and thinks badly persuades the crowd, it is a great evil for
the city, whereas the men who give good advice with their brains
switched on may not look useful at the moment, but are useful to the
city later. That is how you should judge a leader, if you have your
eyes open, because the same thing happens to a man who makes speeches
as to a man in office. He said that they should stone you and Orestes
to death. And it was Tyndareus who kept feeding him his lines, the
arguments the man used to get you both killed.

Then another man stood up and said the opposite. Not much to look at,
but a man. He does not often come near the town or the round of the
marketplace. A farmer, one of the men who work their own land, and they
are the only ones who keep this country alive. But he had his wits
about him, and he wanted to close with the arguments and fight them. A
clean man, who had lived a life nobody could point a finger at. He said
they ought to put a crown on Orestes, the son of Agamemnon, for being
willing to avenge his father by killing a wicked, godless woman, one
who would have put a stop to men ever arming themselves and leaving
home to go on campaign, if the men left behind at home were going to
ruin the household and seduce the soldiers' wives. And the decent
people, at least, thought he spoke well.

Nobody spoke after him. Then your brother came forward and said, "You
who hold the land of Inachus, Pelasgians in the old days and Danaids
after, it was in your defence as much as my father's that I killed my
mother. Because if killing men is going to be a holy act for women, you
had better hurry up and die, all of you, or else be the slaves of your
wives. And that is the opposite of what you ought to do. As things
stand, the woman who betrayed my father's bed is dead. But if you kill
me, the law goes slack, and none of you can die too soon, because there
will be no shortage of women with the nerve for it." He seemed to
speak well, but he did not persuade the crowd. The other one won, that
bad man who spoke to the mob, who called for the death of you and your
brother.

Poor Orestes only just talked them out of stoning. He promised that he
would leave life today, by his own hand, and you with him. Pylades is
bringing him home from the assembly now, in tears, and their friends
are walking with them, weeping and pitying them. So he is coming to
you, a bitter thing to look at, a wretched sight. Get the sword ready,
or the noose for your neck, because you have to leave the light. Your
noble blood was no use to you, and neither was Apollo, sitting on his
tripod at Delphi. He destroyed you.

(The old man goes.)

Chorus.
Poor girl. You have hidden your face and bent it to the ground and you
have gone silent, as if you were about to break into groans and
weeping.

Electra.
	I begin the groaning, Pelasgian land.
	I set my white nails to my cheeks,
	bloody ruin,
	and beat my head, the beating that falls to the share
	of the goddess under the earth, Persephone, lovely child of the dead.
	Let the land the Cyclopes built cry out too,
	and set the iron to its head and crop the hair,
	for the ruin of our house.
	Pity, pity: this is what comes
	for those who are about to die,
	who were once the generals of Greece.

Electra.
	It is gone, gone, it has vanished,
	the whole line of the children of Pelops, and the glory
	that once made the house a thing to envy.
	The jealousy of the gods took hold of it, and the hateful
	bloody vote of the citizens.
	Io, io, you tribes of creatures who live for a day,
	full of tears and full of trouble, look how fate
	walks past all your hopes.
	Each of you takes his turn
	of different sorrows, in the long run of time,
	and nothing in the whole life of mortals stands still.

Electra.
	Let me go to the rock
	that hangs stretched out between sky and earth,
	swinging on its chains,
	a lump broken off Olympus, carried round on golden links,
	so that I can shout out my lament
	to the old man, my father's father, Tantalus,
	who fathered, fathered the founders of my house,
	who saw ruin come.
	The winged flight of horses,
	when Pelops in his four-horse chariot
	drove across the sea, and threw the body of murdered Myrtilus
	into the swell of the deep,
	racing his chariot
	along the shore at Geraestus,
	where the white waves
	of the sea come rolling in.
	From that came the curse on my house,
	the curse of many groans,
	when a lamb was born among the flocks, by the doing of Hermes,
	a lamb with a golden fleece,
	a portent of destruction, destruction,
	for Atreus, the breeder of horses.
	From that came Strife, who turned round
	the winged chariot of the sun
	and fitted the westward road of the sky
	to the single horse
	of the Dawn.
	And Zeus turned the seven Pleiades
	off their course onto another road,
	and after that he hands out death for death,
	the feast that carries the name of Thyestes,
	and the bed of Aerope of Crete,
	who was false, false to her marriage. And at the very last
	it has come to me and to my father,
	by the pitiless necessity of our house.

Chorus.
	And look, here comes your brother,
	condemned to death by the vote,
	and with him Pylades, the most faithful man alive,
	as good as a brother, steadying
	the sick limbs of Orestes,
	yoked beside him, and setting his feet with care.

(Orestes comes in, supported by Pylades.)

Electra.
Oh, no. I am groaning because I see you standing at your grave,
brother, in front of the pyre of the dead. Oh, no, again. I have looked
at you for the very last time, and I am out of my mind.

Orestes.
Will you not be quiet, and drop this women's wailing, and put up with
what has been decided? It is pitiful, yes. But all the same you have to
bear the luck we have.

Electra.
And how am I supposed to keep quiet? We are not going to look at this
light of the sun any more, we two poor wretches.

Orestes.
Do not you kill me too. It is enough that the hand of Argos has killed
me already. Leave what we have now alone.

Electra.
Oh, your youth, Orestes, and your fate, and your death before its time.
You ought to have been living, and now you are gone.

Orestes.
By the gods, do not wrap me in cowardice, ferrying me over into tears
by reminding me of everything that has gone wrong.

Electra.
We are going to die. I cannot help groaning about it. Everyone alive
finds it pitiful to lose his own dear life.

Orestes.
This is our appointed day. We have to fasten a noose to hang from, or
sharpen a sword for our hands.

Electra.
Then you kill me, brother. Do not let some Argive kill me and make an
outrage of the child of Agamemnon.

Orestes.
I have enough of my mother's blood on me. I will not kill you. Die by
your own hand, whichever way you like.

Electra.
So be it. I will not be a step behind your sword. But I want to put my
arms round your neck.

Orestes.
Enjoy the empty pleasure, if there is any pleasure in putting your arms
round someone when you are this close to death.

Electra.
My dearest, the one who has the name that is sweetest to your sister
and the one she misses most, and one soul with her.

Orestes.
You will melt me. And I want to give you back the love of my arms. Why
should a wretch like me be ashamed any more?

(He takes her in his arms.)

Oh, my sister's breast, how dear it is to hold you. This is what the
two of us have, poor wretches, instead of children and a marriage bed:
these words of greeting.

Electra.
Ah. If only the same sword could kill us both, if it is allowed, and
one tomb receive us, a coffin made of cedar wood.

Orestes.
That would be the sweetest thing of all. But you can see how short of
friends we are, when we cannot even share a grave.

Electra.
Did he not even speak for you, or make any effort to save your life,
Menelaus, that bad man, the traitor to my father?

Orestes.
He never showed his face. He has his hopes set on the throne, and he
was careful not to save his friends. Well then, let us see that we do
something noble, something worthy of Agamemnon, and die doing it. I
will show the city what my blood is worth, driving the sword into my
liver. And you must match my daring with your own. Pylades, you be the
umpire of our deaths. When we are both dead, lay our bodies out
properly, and carry us to our father's tomb and bury us there together.
And goodbye. I am going to the work, as you see.

Pylades.
Stop. First there is one thing I have to blame you for. Did you think I
would want to go on living when you were dead?

Orestes.
Why? What is it to you to die with me?

Pylades.
You ask? What is life to me without your company?

Orestes.
You did not kill your mother. I did, poor wretch.

Pylades.
With you, together. So I have to suffer the same as you.

Orestes.
Give your body back to your father. Do not die with me. You have a
city. I do not, not any more. You have your father's house, and a great
harbour of wealth. You have lost your marriage to this unlucky sister
of mine, whom I promised to you out of respect for your friendship. So
take another bride and have children with her. The tie between my house
and yours does not exist any more. Goodbye, dearest face of my company.
That word is not for us, but it is still for you. The dead are robbed
of joys.

Pylades.
You are a long way from understanding what I mean. May the fruitful
earth refuse to take my blood, and the bright sky too, if I ever betray
you: if I ever get myself free and leave you behind. I helped with the
killing, I will not deny it, and I planned the whole thing you are now
paying for. So I have to die with you, and with her. Because I count
her as my wife, the woman whose bed I agreed to. What am I going to say
one day, when I come home to Delphi, the citadel of the Phocians? That
I was your friend before your troubles, and now that you are in trouble
I am your friend no longer? It is not possible. No. This is my business
too. But since we are going to die, let us put our heads together and
see how Menelaus can share our bad luck.

Orestes.
Dearest friend. If only I could see that before I die.

Pylades.
Then listen to me, and put off the stroke of the sword.

Orestes.
I will, if there is any way to get back at my enemy.

Pylades.
Quiet, now. I have very little trust in women.

Orestes.
Do not be afraid of these. They are our friends.

Pylades.
Let us kill Helen. A bitter grief for Menelaus.

Orestes.
How? I am ready, if there is a chance of it coming off.

Pylades.
Cut her throat. She is hiding in your house.

Orestes.
She certainly is. And she is already putting her seal on everything in
it.

Pylades.
Not any more, once she has Hades for a bridegroom.

Orestes.
But how? She has barbarian slaves around her.

Pylades.
Who? I would not be afraid of any Phrygian alive.

Orestes.
The kind who are in charge of mirrors and perfume.

Pylades.
So she has come here with her Trojan luxuries?

Orestes.
So much of it that Greece is too small a house for her.

Pylades.
A race of slaves is nothing against men who are not slaves.

Orestes.
If I can do this, I am not afraid to die twice over.

Pylades.
Nor am I, if it is you I am avenging.

Orestes.
Then lay the thing out for me, and go on to the end of it.

Pylades.
We go into the house, as if we were going in to die.

Orestes.
I have that much. The rest I do not.

Pylades.
We pour out our sorrows to her, everything we are suffering.

Orestes.
So that she cries for us, while inside she is delighted.

Pylades.
And we will be doing exactly what she is doing, at the same time.

Orestes.
And then how do we fight the fight?

Pylades.
We will have swords hidden in these cloaks.

Orestes.
But before that, how do we get rid of her attendants?

Pylades.
We shut them out, one here, one there, in different parts of the house.

Orestes.
And any one of them who will not keep quiet, we have to kill.

Pylades.
And then the work itself shows us where to aim.

Orestes.
At Helen. Kill Helen. I understand the signal.

Pylades.
You have it. Now hear how good my plan is. If we drew the sword on a
woman with more decency in her, the killing would be a disgrace. But as
it is, she is going to pay the penalty on behalf of all Greece, for the
fathers she killed and the children she destroyed, and the brides she
made widows of their husbands. There will be shouts of joy. They will
light fires to the gods, and pray for every blessing on your head and
mine, because we took a wicked woman's blood. And when you have killed
her you will not be called the man who killed his mother. You will
leave that name behind you and rise to a better one: the man who killed
Helen, the killer of so many. It is not right, it can never be right,
that Menelaus should do well while your father and you and your sister
die, and your mother, but I let that pass, it is not a fitting thing to
say, and that he should hold your house, when it was Agamemnon's spear
that won him back his bride. Let me not live another day if I do not
draw the sword on her. And if we do not manage to kill Helen, then we
set fire to this house and die. One of two things we will not fail to
get, and either one is glory: to die well, or to be saved well.

Chorus.
The daughter of Tyndareus has earned the hatred of every woman alive.
She has shamed her whole sex.

Orestes.
Ah. There is nothing better than a friend you can be sure of. Not
wealth, not a throne. And the crowd is a stupid thing to trade for one
noble friend. You were the one who worked out what we did to Aegisthus,
and you stood next to me in the danger, and now once again you are
handing me my revenge on my enemies and you are not stepping out of the
way. But I will stop praising you. There is something heavy about it,
being praised too much. Now I am going to breathe out my life whatever
happens, and I want to do something to my enemies before I die, so that
I can pay back the people who betrayed me with ruin of their own, and
so that the ones who made me miserable will have something to groan
about. I am the son of Agamemnon, who was judged worthy to rule Greece,
no tyrant, but a man who had something like a god's strength in him.
I will not shame him by dying a slave's death. I will let go of my life
like a free man, and I will make Menelaus pay. Because if we could get
hold of one thing, we would be lucky men: if rescue fell into our lap
from somewhere, unlooked for, and we turned out the killers and not the
killed. That is my prayer. And what I want is a pleasant thing to say,
it costs nothing, and it cheers the heart with words that fly out of
the mouth.

Electra.
Brother, I think I have it. That very thing. A rescue for you, and for
him, and thirdly for me.

Orestes.
You are talking about the foresight of a god. But where is it? Since I
know the good sense you carry in that soul of yours.

Electra.
Listen to me, then. And you, over here. Pay attention.

Orestes.
Say it. There is a certain pleasure in waiting for good news.

Electra.
You know Helen's daughter? Of course you do. I am asking a man who
knows.

Orestes.
I know her. Hermione. My mother brought her up.

Electra.
She has gone to the tomb of Clytemnestra.

Orestes.
To do what? What hope are you putting in front of me?

Electra.
To pour drink-offerings on our mother's grave.

Orestes.
Well, and how does what you have just said get us to safety?

Electra.
Seize her as a hostage, when she comes back.

Orestes.
What sort of cure is that, for the three of us?

Electra.
Once Helen is dead, if Menelaus tries to do anything to you, or to
Pylades, or to me, because the three of us are one friend now, tell him
you will kill Hermione. You have to draw your sword and hold it right
against the girl's throat. And if Menelaus, when he sees Helen lying in
her blood, wants the girl to live and offers to save you, then let her
father have his daughter's body back. But if he cannot hold his hot
temper in hand and tries to kill you, then you cut the girl's throat.
And I think that if he blusters a great deal at first, he will soften
in time. He was not born bold, and he was not born brave. That is the
rampart I have for our safety. My speech is finished.

Orestes.
You have the mind of a man, and the body that belongs among women. How
much more you deserved to live than to die. Pylades, this is the woman
you will lose, poor man. Or else you will live, and have a blessed
marriage.

Pylades.
If only it could be so, and she could come to the city of the Phocians
with a fine wedding song, and the honour she deserves.

Orestes.
How soon will Hermione get back to the house? Because everything else
you said was perfect, if our luck holds, and we catch the cub of that
unholy father.

Electra.
I think she must be near the house by now. The time it would take
matches exactly.

Orestes.
Good. Then you, Electra, my sister, stay here in front of the house and
wait for the girl to arrive. And keep watch, in case anyone, some ally
of his, or our father's brother himself, gets into the house before the
killing is finished. If so, shout into the house, or bang on the door,
or send word in to us. We are going inside, Pylades, to arm our hands
with swords for the last fight. Because you are sharing this labour
with me.

Father, you who live in the house of the dark night, your son Orestes
is calling you to come and help the people who need you. It is because
of you that I am suffering this, wretched and wrongly. I have been
betrayed by your brother for doing what was right. I want to take his
wife and kill her. Be our partner in it.

Electra.
Father, come. Come, if you can hear under the ground your children
calling, who are dying for your sake.

Pylades.
Agamemnon, kinsman of my father, hear my prayers too. Save your
children.

Orestes.
I killed my mother —

Electra.
And I put my hand to the sword —

Pylades.
And I made the plan, and cut them loose from their fear —

Orestes.
For you, father. To help you.

Electra.
And I did not betray you either.

Pylades.
Will you hear these reproaches and not rescue your children?

Orestes.
I pour you a drink-offering of tears.

Electra.
And I pour you one of laments.

Pylades.
Stop now, and let us get on with the work. If prayers can pierce the
ground like spears, he hears. And you, Zeus, our ancestor, and you,
holy Justice, give good luck to him, and to me, and to her. One fight
for three friends, one judgement: either all of us live, or we pay the
debt of death.

(Orestes and Pylades go into the house.)

Electra.
	Women of Mycenae, my friends,
	first in rank in Argos, where the Pelasgians made their home.

Chorus.
What are you calling out to us, mistress? That name is still yours, in
the city of the Danaids.
