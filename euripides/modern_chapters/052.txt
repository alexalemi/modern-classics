Iphigenia at Aulis
(Part 3 of 3)

Clytemnestra.
In words, you speak well. It is your deeds I do not know how to find
a good name for. Come outside, daughter. You know everything your
father means to do. And bring Orestes with you, your brother, carried
under your dress, child. There. Here she is, doing as you told her.
The rest I will say myself, for her and for me.

Agamemnon.
Child, why are you crying? Why do you not look at me gladly any more?
Why are you staring at the ground, with your dress held up in front of
your eyes?

Clytemnestra.
Pheu. Where do I take hold of my troubles and begin? Any one of them
could come first, or last, or in the middle. They fit anywhere.

Agamemnon.
What is it? All of you have come at me together, with the same
confusion in your faces and the same trouble in your eyes.

Clytemnestra.
Answer what I ask you honestly, husband.

Agamemnon.
You do not need to order me. I am willing to be asked.

Clytemnestra.
Your child, and mine. Are you going to kill her?

Agamemnon.
Ah. That is a cruel thing to say. You are suspecting what you have no
business suspecting.

Clytemnestra.
Keep calm. Just answer the first question again.

Agamemnon.
Ask me a fair question and you will get a fair answer.

Clytemnestra.
I have no other question, and do not give me any other answer.

Agamemnon.
Fate, my lady. Luck. The spirit that has charge of my life.

Clytemnestra.
And of mine, and of hers. One spirit for three people, and unlucky for
all three.

Agamemnon.
What wrong have I done you?

Clytemnestra.
You ask me that? That mind of yours has lost its mind.

Agamemnon.
I am finished. My secret is out.

Clytemnestra.
I know all of it. I have found out what you mean to do to me. The
silence itself is your confession, and so is the groaning. Do not wear
yourself out with a long speech.

Agamemnon.
There. I am silent. Why should I add shamelessness to disaster by
telling lies on top of it?

Clytemnestra.
Then listen. I am going to uncover what I mean, and there will be no
more talking in riddles and hints. First, and I put this first so that
it is the first thing I throw in your face: you married me against my
will. You took me by force, after you had killed Tantalus, the husband
I had before you. And my baby, you tore him from my breast and dashed
him on the ground. Then the two sons of Zeus, my brothers, came riding
against you with the sun flashing off their horses, and my old father
Tyndareus saved you, because you went down on your knees to him and
begged. And so you had my bed after all.

I made my peace with you on those terms, and you will be my witness
that from then on I was a wife nobody could fault, to you and to your
house. I kept myself in hand where Aphrodite was concerned, and I made
your household grow, so that coming in at your door was a joy to you
and going out of it was good fortune. That is a rare catch for a man, a
wife like that. There is no shortage of the worthless kind. And I bore
you three girls, and then this boy, and now, of the girls, you are
taking one away from me. Heartlessly. If anyone asks you why you are
killing her, tell me, what will you say? Or shall I say it for you? So
that Menelaus can have Helen back. A fine bargain. Pay for a bad woman
with your children. We are buying what we hate most with what we love
best.

Come on, then. Suppose you go off to the war and leave me in the house,
and you are away at Troy for years. What do you think my heart will be
like at home? When I look at her chair, and every chair of hers is
empty, and her room is empty, and I sit by myself in tears, singing the
same lament for her over and over: "The father who made you has
destroyed you, child. He killed you himself. Not another man, not
another man's hand. And this is the homecoming he has left waiting for
himself." Because after that it will only take a small excuse for me
and the daughters you have left me to give you the welcome you deserve
to get. By the gods, do not force me to become an evil woman for your
sake. And do not become an evil man yourself.

Well, then. You will sacrifice the child. And what prayers will you say
over her, while you do it? What good thing will you ask for yourself,
with your daughter's throat under the knife? A wretched homecoming, to
match the shameful way you went out? Or is it my job to pray for
something good for you? We would have to think the gods were fools if
we wished murderers well. And when you come home to Argos, will you
throw your arms round your children? You have no right to. Which of
them will so much as look you in the face, once you have called one of
them to you and killed her? Have you even got as far as thinking about
this? Or is your only concern to carry a sceptre about and command an
army?

Here is the fair proposal you should have put to the Argives.
"Achaeans, do you want to sail for the land of the Phrygians? Then draw
lots for whose child has to die." That would have been even-handed.
Instead you pick out your own daughter as the one special victim and
hand her to the Greeks. Or Menelaus could have killed Hermione for her
mother's sake, since it was his business in the first place. As it is,
I, who kept your bed faithfully, am to lose my child, and the woman who
did the wrong will bring her girl home to Sparta safe and grown, and be
happy. Answer me, if anything I have said is wrong. But if I have put
it well, then do not kill her, your child and mine. Then you will be a
man who keeps himself in hand.

Chorus.
Do what she says. Joining together to save your children is a fine
thing, Agamemnon. No one alive will argue with that.

Iphigenia.
If I had the voice of Orpheus, father, so that I could sing a spell and
make the rocks get up and follow me, and charm anyone I liked with
words, I would have gone that way. But the only cleverness I have is
tears, and I will give you those. Tears I can manage. I press my body
against your knees like a suppliant's branch, this body your wife here
bore you. Do not destroy me before my time. It is sweet to look at the
light. Do not force me to see what is under the ground.

I was the first to call you father, and you were the first to call me
your child. I was the first to climb into your lap and give you a
child's kisses and take yours back. And this is what you used to say:
"Shall I see you one day, my girl, happy in a husband's house, living
and flowering, the way a daughter of mine should?" And I would answer,
hanging from your beard, the way I am holding it now with my hand: "And
what will I do for you, father? When you are old, shall I have you in
my house and look after you, and pay you back for all the work it took
to raise me?" I remember every word of it. You have forgotten, and you
want to kill me.

No. By Pelops, and by your father Atreus, and by my mother here, who
went through labour for me once already and is going through this
second labour now. What have I got to do with Paris and Helen and their
marriage? Where did he come from, father, to be my ruin? Look at me.
Give me one look, and a kiss, so that at least I have that to take with
me when I die, something of yours to remember, if you will not do what
I ask.

(She holds the baby up toward him.)

Brother, you are a small helper for the people you love. But cry with
me all the same, and beg our father not to let your sister die. Even
babies have some feeling for evil when it comes. Look, father. He is
begging you without a word. Have some respect for me. Pity my life.
Yes, by your beard, we two who love you are pleading with you, one a
nestling and the other grown. I will cut it all down to one thing and
win the whole argument with it. This light is the sweetest thing human
beings can look at, and what is under the ground is nothing. Anyone who
prays to die is mad. A bad life is better than a fine death.

Chorus.
Miserable Helen. Because of you and your marriages a great struggle has
come on the sons of Atreus and their children.

Agamemnon.
I understand what deserves pity and what does not. And I love my
children. I would be mad if I did not. It is a terrible thing for me to
bring myself to do this, wife, and a terrible thing not to. Because I
have to do it. Look at the size of this army, and the fleet drawn up
round it like a wall. Look at all these lords of Greece in their bronze
armour. There is no sailing to the towers of Troy for them, and no
tearing out the famous foundations of Troy, unless I sacrifice you, as
the prophet Calchas says. Some Aphrodite has driven the Greek army mad
to sail for the barbarians' country as fast as it can and put an end to
the stealing of Greek wives. And they will kill my girls in Argos, and
you, and me, if I break the goddess's oracle. It is not Menelaus who
has made a slave of me, child. I have not come here to do what he
wants. It is Greece. Whether I want it or not, I have to sacrifice you
for her, and against that we are the weaker side. She has to be free,
child, as far as it lies in you and in me to make her free, and Greeks
must not have their wives dragged out of their beds by barbarians.

(Agamemnon goes out toward the camp.)

Clytemnestra.
	Child. Foreign women.
	Oh, I am wretched. Your death.
	Your father is running from you. He has handed you to Hades.

Iphigenia.
	Oh, mother. The same song
	of the same bad luck has fallen on us both,
	and there is no more light for me,
	no more of this blaze of the sun.
	Io. Io.
	Snow-beaten valley of the Phrygians, mountains of Ida,
	where Priam once threw out a soft baby
	he had taken from its mother, to die,
	and the baby was Paris, who was called
	Idaean, Idaean, in the city of the Phrygians.
	If only Priam had never
	brought that cowherd, raised among his cattle,
	Paris, Alexander, to live
	by the white water, where the springs
	of the Nymphs lie,
	and the meadow, green with young shoots,
	and roses
	and hyacinths for goddesses to pick. Because there one day
	came Pallas Athena, and Aphrodite with her scheming heart,
	and Hera, and Hermes, the messenger of Zeus:
	Aphrodite preening in the desire she causes,
	Athena in her spear,
	Hera in the royal bed
	of Zeus the king,
	to a hateful judgement, a contest
	over beauty. And for me it meant death,
	a death that brings the Greeks
	a name, girls, but it is my death
	Artemis took as the first offering on the road to Troy.
	And the man who fathered me, wretched me,
	mother, oh mother,
	is gone. He has betrayed me and left me alone.
	Oh, I am the unluckiest of all. I have seen
	bitter, bitter Helen, Helen my ruin,
	and I am murdered, I am destroyed,
	cut down in an unholy killing by an unholy father.
	I wish this Aulis had never
	taken the bronze-beaked ships
	into these harbours, sterns first,
	the pine fleet bound for Troy,
	and that Zeus had never breathed a wind
	against them on the Euripus. He tempers
	one breeze for one man's sails and another for another's,
	so that some rejoice,
	and some grieve, and some are forced,
	some put out to sea, some make ready,
	some wait.
	Full of trouble, then, full of trouble
	is the race of us who live a day at a time, and what has to be
	is a hard thing for people to find out.
	Io. Io.
	Great suffering, great grief,
	the daughter of Tyndareus has laid on the Greeks.

Chorus.
I pity you. This is bad luck you have met, the kind you should never
have met.

Iphigenia.
Mother, mother who bore me, I can see a crowd of men close by.

Clytemnestra.
It is the goddess's son, child. The one you came here for.

Iphigenia.
Open the tent for me, slaves. Let me hide myself.

Clytemnestra.
Why run away, child?

Iphigenia.
I am ashamed to look at Achilles.

Clytemnestra.
Why?

Iphigenia.
My marriage went so badly. It makes me ashamed.

Clytemnestra.
This is no time to be delicate, with things the way they have fallen.
Stay. There is no room for pride, if it can help us.

Achilles.
Unhappy woman, daughter of Leda—

Clytemnestra.
That is no lie.

Achilles.
There is terrible shouting among the Argives—

Clytemnestra.
What shouting? Tell me.

Achilles.
About your daughter—

Clytemnestra.
That is a bad omen, that word.

Achilles.
That she has to be sacrificed.

Clytemnestra.
And no one speaks against them?

Achilles.
I got into some trouble myself—

Clytemnestra.
What trouble, stranger?

Achilles.
Of being stoned to death.

Clytemnestra.
For trying to save my girl?

Achilles.
Exactly that.

Clytemnestra.
Who would have the nerve to lay a hand on you?

Achilles.
Every Greek there.

Clytemnestra.
Were your Myrmidons not there with you?

Achilles.
They were the first to turn on me.

Clytemnestra.
Then we are finished, child.

Achilles.
They called me the man a marriage had beaten.

Clytemnestra.
And what did you answer?

Achilles.
Not to kill the woman who was going to be my wife—

Clytemnestra.
Quite right.

Achilles.
The wife her father promised me.

Clytemnestra.
And sent for from Argos.

Achilles.
But I was beaten by the shouting.

Clytemnestra.
A mob is a terrible thing.

Achilles.
But I will help you all the same.

Clytemnestra.
And fight so many, one man alone?

Achilles.
Do you see these men, carrying my armour?

Clytemnestra.
Bless you for your good heart.

Achilles.
Then I will be blessed.

Clytemnestra.
So the child is not going to be slaughtered after all?

Achilles.
Not with my consent.

Clytemnestra.
But will someone come to lay hands on the girl?

Achilles.
Thousands of them. And Odysseus will lead them.

Clytemnestra.
The son of Sisyphus?

Achilles.
The very man.

Clytemnestra.
Acting on his own, or under the army's orders?

Achilles.
Chosen, and willing.

Clytemnestra.
A wicked thing to choose. Blood on his hands.

Achilles.
But I will hold him off.

Clytemnestra.
Will he drag her away by force, if she will not go?

Achilles.
Certainly. By her yellow hair.

Clytemnestra.
And what should I do, when it comes to that?

Achilles.
Hold on to your daughter.

Clytemnestra.
If that is all it takes, she will not be slaughtered.

Achilles.
It will come to that. Be sure of it.

Iphigenia.
Mother, listen to me, both of you. I can see you are angry with your
husband, and it is for nothing. It is not easy for us to hold out
against what cannot be helped. The stranger deserves our thanks for
his good will, but you have to see this too: he must not be put in the
wrong with the army, so that we gain nothing and he comes to grief. Now
listen, mother, to what has come into my mind as I thought about it.

I have decided to die. And I want to do it well, with a good name,
putting anything low or mean out of my way. Come and look at it with
me, mother, and see how right I am. The whole of Greece, all of great
Greece, is looking at me now. On me depends the fleet's crossing and
the destruction of the Phrygians, and whether barbarians are ever
again allowed to steal women from happy Greece, if they try anything,
once they have paid for the ruin of Helen, whom Paris carried off. All
of that I will save by dying, and my fame, as the girl who set Greece
free, will be a blessed one.

And after all, I have no right to cling too hard to my life. You bore
me for all the Greeks together, not for yourself alone. Thousands of
men have armed themselves with shields, and thousands have taken up
oars, because their country has been wronged, and they will have the
nerve to go at the enemy and die for Greece. And my one life is to
stand in the way of all that? What kind of justice would that be? Could
I find one word to answer it? And here is the other point. This man
should not have to fight the whole of Argos for a woman's sake, or die
for one. One man is worth more than ten thousand women, when it comes
to seeing the light of day. And if Artemis has decided to take my body,
am I, a mortal, going to stand in a goddess's way? It cannot be done. I
give my body to Greece. Sacrifice it, and go and sack Troy. That will
be my monument for a long time to come. That will be my children, and
my marriage, and my good name. It is right, mother, for Greeks to rule
barbarians, and not for barbarians to rule Greeks. They are slaves, and
we are free.

Chorus.
Your part in this, young woman, is a noble one. It is the part played
by luck and by the goddess that is sick.

Achilles.
Daughter of Agamemnon, some god was going to make me a happy man, if I
had won you for my wife. I envy Greece for having you, and I envy you
for having Greece. What you have said is good, and worthy of your
country. You have given up fighting the gods, who are stronger than
you, and you have worked out what is best and what has to be. And now
that I have looked at your nature and seen what it is, I want you for
my wife more than ever. You are a noble woman. Think about it. I want
to do you good, and take you home to my house, and it hurts me, Thetis
be my witness, if I cannot save you by fighting my way through the
Greeks. Look at it squarely. Death is a terrible thing.

Iphigenia.
I say this with no fear of anyone. The daughter of Tyndareus is already
causing enough fighting and killing among men with her beauty. You,
stranger, do not die for me, and do not kill anyone. Let me save
Greece, if I can.

Achilles.
What a spirit. There is nothing more I can say against that, since this
is what you have decided. It is a noble way to think, and why should
anyone not say so, when it is true? All the same, you may change your
mind about this. So that you know exactly where I stand, I will go and
set this armour down beside the altar, ready to stop you dying, not to
let it happen. You may want to take up my offer soon enough, when you
see the knife close to your throat. I will not let you die through
rashness of your own. I am going to the goddess's temple with these
weapons, and I will be watching for you to arrive.

(Achilles goes out with his men.)

Iphigenia.
Mother, why are you crying without a word, wetting your eyes?

Clytemnestra.
I have reason enough for a heart that hurts. I am wretched.

Iphigenia.
Stop. Do not make a coward of me. Do this one thing I ask.

Clytemnestra.
Say it. You will get no wrong from me, child.

Iphigenia.
Then do not cut off a lock of your hair for me, and do not wrap your
body in black clothes.

Clytemnestra.
What are you saying, child? When I have lost you?

Iphigenia.
You have not. I am saved, and through me you will have a good name.

Clytemnestra.
What do you mean? Am I not to mourn your life?

Iphigenia.
No. Because no grave will be heaped up over me.

Clytemnestra.
What of that? It is the dying we mourn, not the grave.

Iphigenia.
The altar of the goddess, the daughter of Zeus, will be my memorial.

Clytemnestra.
Well, child. I will do as you say. You put it well.

Iphigenia.
Yes. As a woman who is fortunate, and who has done Greece a service.

Clytemnestra.
What message shall I take to your sisters from you?

Iphigenia.
Do not put black clothes on them either.

Clytemnestra.
But is there some loving word I can give the girls from you?

Iphigenia.
Goodbye. And raise Orestes here to be a man, for me.

Clytemnestra.
Hold him close. You are looking at him for the last time.

Iphigenia.
My darling. You helped the people you love as much as you could.

Clytemnestra.
Is there anything I can do in Argos that would please you?

Iphigenia.
Do not hate my father. He is your husband.

Clytemnestra.
He has terrible races to run because of you.

Iphigenia.
He destroyed me against his will, for the land of Greece.

Clytemnestra.
By a trick. Basely. Not the way a son of Atreus should.

Iphigenia.
Who will come and lead me there, before I am dragged by the hair?

Clytemnestra.
I will. I will go with you—

Iphigenia.
Not you. That is not right.

Clytemnestra.
Holding on to your dress.

Iphigenia.
Mother, do as I ask. Stay here. It is better this way, for me and for
you. Let one of these attendants of my father's take me to the meadow
of Artemis, where I am going to be slaughtered.

Clytemnestra.
Child, are you going?

Iphigenia.
Yes. And I will never come back.

Clytemnestra.
Leaving your mother?

Iphigenia.
As you see. And not deserving it.

Clytemnestra.
Wait. Do not leave me.

Iphigenia.
I will not let you shed a tear. And you, young women, sing a hymn of
good omen over what is happening to me, a healing song to the daughter
of Zeus, to Artemis. Let the word go out to the Greeks to keep a holy
silence. Let someone begin the rite with the baskets. Let the fire
blaze up for the barley we scatter to make it clean, and let my father
go round the altar from left to right. Because I am coming to give the
Greeks their safety, and their victory with it.

Iphigenia.
	Lead me away, the girl who takes the cities
	of Troy and the Phrygians.
	Give me wreaths to wind round me, bring them here,
	here is my hair to crown,
	and bring the streams of holy water.
	Dance round the temple,
	round the altar, for Artemis,
	Artemis the queen,
	Artemis the blessed. Because with my blood
	and my sacrifice, if it has to be,
	I am going to wipe the oracle clean.
	Mother, my lady, my lady mother, I will not
	give you my tears.
	They are out of place at the holy rites.
	Io, io, young women,
	sing with me to Artemis,
	whose temple looks across the water at Chalcis,
	where the spears are mad for war
	because of my name, here in the narrow
	harbours of Aulis.
	Io, Pelasgian earth, my mother,
	and Mycenae, where I was brought up—

Chorus.
	Are you calling on the city of Perseus,
	the walls the Cyclopes built with their hands?

Iphigenia.
	You raised me to be a light for Greece.
	I am dying, and I do not say no.

Chorus.
	No. Your fame will never leave you.

Iphigenia.
	Io. Io.
	Day that carries the torch,
	and light of Zeus, another, another
	life and another fate will be my home now.
	Goodbye, dear light.

(Iphigenia goes out toward the altar, and the crowd of men with her.)

Chorus.
	Io. Io.
	Look at her, the girl who takes the cities
	of Troy and the Phrygians,
	walking away, with the wreaths
	to be set on her head, and the streams of holy water,
	to stain the altar of the goddess, that spirit,
	with drops of running blood
	when the lovely neck of her body
	is cut. The dewy streams
	her father pours are waiting for you, and the holy water,
	and the army of the Achaeans, wanting
	to reach the city of Troy.
	But let us call on the daughter of Zeus,
	on Artemis,
	the queen of the gods, as if for a fortunate end.
	Lady, lady, who takes pleasure
	in human sacrifice, send the army of the Greeks
	into the land of the Phrygians,
	to the treacherous seats of Troy,
	and grant that Agamemnon, with his spears,
	sets round his own head the most glorious crown
	Greece ever gave,
	a fame that will never be forgotten.

(The Chorus waits. A Messenger comes running from the direction of the
altar.)

Messenger.
Daughter of Tyndareus, Clytemnestra, come out of the tent and hear
what I have to say.

Clytemnestra.
I heard your voice and I have come. I am wretched, and I am shaking,
struck stupid with fear. Have you come bringing me some new disaster,
on top of the one I have?

Messenger.
No. About your child I want to tell you something wonderful, and
terrible.

Clytemnestra.
Then do not wait. Say it, as fast as you can.

Messenger.
Dear mistress, you will hear all of it plainly. I will tell it from
the beginning, unless my mind stumbles somewhere and trips up my tongue
as I go. When we reached the grove of Artemis, the daughter of Zeus,
and the meadows full of flowers where the Achaean army was assembled,
bringing your daughter with us, the crowd of Argives gathered at once.
And when King Agamemnon saw the girl walking into the grove to be
slaughtered, he groaned aloud and turned his head away and wept, with
his cloak held up in front of his eyes.

But she stood close to the man who fathered her and said this: "Father,
here I am. I give my body, of my own free will, for my country and for
the whole land of Greece, for them to lead to the goddess's altar and
sacrifice, if that is what the oracle demands. And for my part, I wish
you good fortune. May you win the gift of victory and come home to
your fathers' land. And so let none of the Argives lay a hand on me. I
will offer my neck in silence, with a good heart."

That is what she said, and every man who heard it was amazed at the
courage and the goodness of the girl. Then Talthybius, whose business
it was, stood up in the middle of them and called for a holy silence,
with not a word from the army. And Calchas the prophet drew a sharp
knife from its sheath and laid it in a basket of beaten gold, and put
the wreath on the girl's head. Then the son of Peleus took the basket
and the holy water together, and ran in a circle round the goddess's
altar sprinkling it, and said: "Daughter of Zeus, killer of wild
animals, who wheel your bright light through the darkness, accept this
offering, which the army of the Achaeans and King Agamemnon give you
together: the pure blood from a beautiful girl's neck. And grant our
ships a voyage without harm, and grant that our spears tear the towers
of Troy to the ground."

The sons of Atreus and the whole army stood looking at the ground. The
priest took the knife and said his prayer, and studied her throat for
the place to strike. And no small pain went into my heart, and I stood
there with my head down. And then, all at once, there was a wonder to
see. Everyone heard the sound of the blow, clearly. But nobody saw
where in the earth the girl had gone. The priest shouted, and the whole
army shouted back, at the sight of a thing no one had hoped for, sent
by some god, a thing you could not believe even with your eyes on it.
Because there on the ground lay a deer, gasping, huge to look at and
magnificent, and the goddess's altar was soaked from end to end with
its blood.

At that, you can imagine how happily Calchas spoke. "Captains of this
united army of the Achaeans, do you see this sacrifice, the one the
goddess has set before her altar, a deer that runs the mountains? She
welcomes this far more than the girl, so that her altar is not stained
with noble blood. She has accepted it gladly, and she is giving us a
following wind and a landing at Troy. So take heart, every sailor here,
and go to your ships. Because today we must leave the hollow bays of
Aulis and cross the swell of the Aegean."

And when the whole offering had been burned to ash in the fire of
Hephaestus, he prayed the fitting prayers, that the army might have
its homecoming. Agamemnon sends me to tell you this, and to say what
share the gods have given him, and that he has won a fame that will
never die throughout Greece. And I was there, I saw the thing happen,
and I am telling you: your child has plainly flown away to the gods.
So let go of your grief, and let go of your anger at your husband. What
the gods do is not what mortals expect, and the people they love, they
keep safe. This day has seen your daughter dead and alive.

Chorus.
I am so glad to hear this from the messenger. He says your child is
alive and living among the gods.

Clytemnestra.
	Child, which of the gods has stolen you?
	What am I to call you now? How can I help saying
	that this is a story made up to comfort me, and all for nothing,
	so that I would stop my bitter grieving for you?

Chorus.
	And look, here comes King Agamemnon,
	with the same story to tell you himself.

Agamemnon.
Wife, as far as our daughter goes we can count ourselves blessed. She
really is in the company of the gods. Now you must take this young calf
of ours and go home, because the army has its eyes on the sea. And
goodbye. It will be a long time before I greet you again, from Troy.
May it go well with you.

Chorus.
	Go gladly, son of Atreus, to the land of Phrygia,
	and gladly come back,
	with the finest spoils of Troy in your hands, for me.
