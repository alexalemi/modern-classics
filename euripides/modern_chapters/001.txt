Alcestis
(Part 2 of 3)

Child.
	Oh, what has happened to me. Mother has gone
	down below. She is not here any more, father,
	not under the sun.
	She has left me behind, poor thing,
	and made my life an orphan's.
	Look, look at her eyes, and her hands hanging loose.
	Listen to me, hear me, mother, I am begging you.
	It is me, mother, me,
	your own chick, calling you, pressing
	my mouth against your lips.

Admetus.
She cannot hear you, and she cannot see you. You two and I have been
struck a heavy blow.

Child.
	I am so young, father, to be left
	alone like this, without my mother, without her love.
	Oh, it is a terrible thing
	that has been done to me,
	and you, my sister, you have gone
	through it with me. Oh, father,
	you married for nothing, for nothing. You never reached
	the end of old age with her.
	She died first. And with you gone,
	mother, the house is finished.

Chorus.
Admetus, you have to bear this. You are not the first man, and you will
not be the last, to lose a good wife. Remember: every one of us owes a
death.

Admetus.
I know it. This evil did not swoop down on me out of nowhere. I have
known it was coming, and it has been wearing me down for a long time.
But now I am going to carry this body out for burial, so stay here with
me, and while you wait, sing back the hymn to the god below, the god who
cannot be bargained with. And to every Thessalian I rule I say this:
share the mourning for this woman, with your heads shaved and your
clothes black. You who yoke four-horse teams, and you who ride single
horses, cut the manes from their necks with iron. Let there be no sound
of pipes in the city, and no lyre, until twelve full moons have passed.
I will never bury anyone dearer to me than this body, or anyone better
to me. She deserves honour from me. She, and nobody else, died in my
place.

(Admetus and the children go into the house with the body.)

Chorus.
	Daughter of Pelias,
	go with my blessing to live in the house of Hades,
	the home that has no sun.
	Let Hades know it, the god with the black hair, and the old man
	who sits at his oar and his rudder
	ferrying the dead,
	that it is by far, by far the best woman
	he has carried over the lake of Acheron
	in his boat with the two oars.

Chorus.
	The servants of the Muses will sing of you
	many times, to the seven-stringed shell of the mountain tortoise
	and in hymns with no lyre at all, making you famous,
	at Sparta, when the circling year brings round the Carnean
	month, and the moon
	rides high all night long,
	and in shining Athens, the rich city.
	That is the song you left behind when you died,
	for the makers of songs.

Chorus.
	If only it were in my power,
	if only I could send you
	back to the light out of the halls of Hades
	and the streams of Cocytus,
	with an oar on the river of the dead.
	Because you, and you alone, dearest of women,
	you had the nerve
	to buy your husband out of Hades
	with your own life. May the earth
	lie light on you, lady. And if your husband
	ever takes a new bride to his bed, believe me, he will be hated
	by me, and by your children.

Chorus.
	His mother was not willing
	to hide her body in the earth
	for her son, and nor was his old father.
	They brought him into the world, and they would not save him,
	the pair of them, hard-hearted, with their grey hair.
	But you, in the flower
	of your youth, died for him, and are gone.
	May I find a wife like that
	to share my life and love me. It is
	a rare thing in this world. She would live
	beside me all my days
	and never once give me pain.

(Heracles comes in from the road.)

Heracles.
Strangers, you people of this land of Pherae, would I find Admetus at
home?

Chorus.
The son of Pheres is in the house, Heracles. But tell us, what business
sends you to Thessaly, to this town of Pherae?

Heracles.
I am doing a job for Eurystheus of Tiryns.

Chorus.
And where are you going? What wandering are you yoked to this time?

Heracles.
I am after the four-horse chariot team of Diomedes the Thracian.

Chorus.
How are you going to manage that? Have you never met the man who will be
your host?

Heracles.
Never. I have not been to the country of the Bistones yet.

Chorus.
You cannot get hold of those horses without a fight.

Heracles.
But I cannot refuse the labours, either.

Chorus.
Then you will kill him and come back, or die and stay there.

Heracles.
It would not be the first race of that kind I have run.

Chorus.
And if you beat their master, what do you get out of it?

Heracles.
I drive the horses away to the king of Tiryns.

Chorus.
It is not easy to put a bit in those jaws.

Heracles.
Easy enough, unless they breathe fire out of their nostrils.

Chorus.
No, but they tear men to pieces with those quick jaws.

Heracles.
That is fodder for mountain beasts you are talking about, not horses.

Chorus.
You will see their mangers spattered with blood.

Heracles.
And whose son does the man who feeds them boast of being?

Chorus.
The son of Ares. He is lord of the golden shield of Thrace.

Heracles.
This labour too is just the luck I was born with. It is always hard, and
it always goes uphill, if I have to fight the sons Ares fathered, Lycaon
first, then Cycnus, and now this third contest, going in against the
horses and their master. But there is nobody alive who will ever see the
son of Alcmena tremble at an enemy's hand.

Chorus.
And here is Admetus himself, the king of this country, coming out of the
house.

(Admetus comes out, his hair cut short in mourning.)

Admetus.
Welcome, son of Zeus, of the blood of Perseus.

Heracles.
And welcome to you, Admetus, king of Thessaly.

Admetus.
I wish I could be. But I know you mean it kindly.

Heracles.
Why are you showing yourself with your hair cut for mourning?

Admetus.
I have to bury someone today.

Heracles.
May a god keep harm away from your children.

Admetus.
The children I fathered are alive, in the house.

Heracles.
Your father, then. Well, he was ripe for it, if he is gone.

Admetus.
He is alive, Heracles, and so is my mother.

Heracles.
Surely it is not your wife who is dead? Not Alcestis?

Admetus.
There are two things I could tell you about her.

Heracles.
Do you mean she is dead, or still alive?

Admetus.
She is, and she is not. And it hurts me.

Heracles.
I am none the wiser. You are talking in riddles.

Admetus.
Do you not know the fate she has to meet?

Heracles.
I know. She undertook to die in your place.

Admetus.
Then how is she still alive, if she agreed to that?

Heracles.
Ah. Do not mourn your wife ahead of time. Put it off until then.

Admetus.
The one who is about to die is dead. The dead no longer exist.

Heracles.
Being and not being are counted as two different things.

Admetus.
That is how you judge it, Heracles. I judge it the other way.

Heracles.
Then why are you crying? Which of your people has died?

Admetus.
A woman. It was a woman we were speaking of just now.

Heracles.
An outsider, or someone related to you by blood?

Admetus.
An outsider. But she was bound to this house all the same.

Heracles.
Then how did she come to lose her life in your house?

Admetus.
Her father died, and she lived here as an orphan.

Heracles.
Ah. I wish I had found you without grief, Admetus.

Admetus.
What are you planning to do, that you stitch a speech like that
together?

Heracles.
I will go to the hearth of some other host.

Admetus.
That cannot happen, my lord. Never let an evil like that come on me.

Heracles.
A guest is a nuisance to people in grief, if he turns up.

Admetus.
The dead are dead. Come into the house.

Heracles.
It is shameful for guests to feast beside people who are weeping.

Admetus.
The guest rooms are separate. We will take you there.

Heracles.
Let me go, and I will owe you ten thousand thanks.

Admetus.
You are not going to another man's hearth. You there, lead the way for
him. Open the guest rooms that face away from the house, and tell the
stewards to have plenty of food there. And shut the doors to the inner
courtyard, and shut them well. Guests at their dinner should not hear
groaning, or be made unhappy.

(Heracles goes in, a servant leading the way.)

Chorus.
What are you doing? With a disaster like this in front of you, Admetus,
you have the heart to take in guests? What is this foolishness?

Admetus.
And if I had driven him away from my house and my city, when he came to
me as a guest, would you have praised me more? You would not. My
disaster would have been no smaller, and I would have been less of a
host. And on top of my troubles I would have had another trouble: my
house getting the name of a house that hates its guests. And I find
the man himself the best of hosts, whenever I go to the thirsty land
of Argos.

Chorus.
Then why did you hide the misfortune that is on you, if it was a friend
who came, as you say yourself?

Admetus.
He would never have been willing to come into the house if he had known
anything of my troubles. And I suppose there are people who think I am a
fool for doing this, and will not praise me for it. But my house does
not know how to push a guest away, or treat one without honour.

Chorus.
	House that has always had room for guests, house of a free man,
	Pythian Apollo himself, the fine lyre-player,
	thought you worth living in,
	and brought himself to be a shepherd
	under your roof,
	down the slanting hillsides
	piping to your flocks
	the wedding songs of shepherds.

Chorus.
	And the spotted lynxes grazed alongside them, glad of his music,
	and the tawny pack of lions came
	down out of the glen of Othrys,
	and round your lyre, Apollo,
	the fawn with the dappled coat
	danced, stepping out past the tall pines
	on her light hooves,
	happy at the cheerful song.

Chorus.
	And so he lives in a house with more sheep than any other,
	by the lovely waters of Lake Boebe,
	and for his ploughed fields
	and his level plains he sets one border
	in the west, where the sun stables its horses
	in the dark sky of the Molossians,
	and the other at the Aegean, where he rules as far as
	the harbourless coast under Pelion.

Chorus.
	And now he has thrown open his house
	and taken in a guest, with his eyes wet,
	weeping for the body of his dear wife,
	dead in the house this very day. Good birth
	comes out as decency.
	In good men there is every kind of wisdom. I admire it,
	and in my heart the confidence sits firm
	that a man who honours the gods will do well.

Admetus.
Men of Pherae, you are kind to be here. My servants are carrying the
body out now, dressed with everything it needs, high on their shoulders,
to the tomb and the pyre. And you, as custom asks, speak to the dead
woman as she goes out on her last road.

(The body is carried out of the house. Pheres comes in, an old man, with
servants behind him carrying robes and ornaments.)

Chorus.
And I see your father coming, on his old man's feet, and attendants
carrying in their hands finery for your wife, offerings for the dead
below.

Pheres.
I have come to share your pain, my son. You have lost a good wife, and a
sensible one; nobody will deny it. But you have to bear these things,
hard as they are to bear. Take these robes and ornaments, and let them
go under the earth with her. Her body deserves to be honoured. She died
for your life, my son, and she did not leave me childless; she did not
let me lose you and wither away in a mourning old age. She has made life
a more glorious thing for every woman, by daring this noble act. You who
saved this man, who raised us up when we were falling, farewell, and may
it go well with you even in the house of Hades. Marriages like that are
a gain to men, I say. Otherwise marrying is not worth it.

Admetus.
You did not come to this burial because I invited you, and I do not
count your presence as a friend's. She will never wear that finery of
yours. She will be buried needing nothing of yours. You should have
shared my pain then, when I was dying. But you stood out of the way, and
you let somebody else die, somebody young, when you were old, and now
you are going to wail over this body? Then you were never really the
father of this body of mine. And the woman who says she bore me, and is
called my mother, did not give birth to me. I was born of slave's blood
and slipped secretly to your wife's breast. You showed what you are when
you were put to the test, and I do not consider myself your son.

You outdo everyone in cowardice. At your age, at the very end of your
life, you would not die for your own son, you did not have the nerve to.
You left it to this woman, an outsider, and I would be right to count
her, and her alone, as my mother and my father both. And yet what a fine
contest that would have been for you to enter, dying for your own son,
and in any case the time you had left to live was short. And I would
have lived, and so would she, for the rest of our time, and I would not
be left alone, groaning over my troubles.

And you have had everything a happy man should have. You had your youth
as a king. You had a son, me, to inherit this house, so you were not
going to die childless and leave an orphaned house for strangers to
loot. And you cannot say you gave me up to die because I dishonoured
your old age. I was the most respectful son to you that there could be.
And in return for that, this is the thanks you and my mother gave me. So
you had better get on with fathering more children, and lose no time
about it, children who will look after you when you are old, and lay you
out when you are dead, and set out your body for burial. Because I am
not going to bury you, not with this hand. As far as you are concerned,
I am dead. If I found somebody else to save me, and I am looking at the
light, then I say I am that person's child, and the loving supporter of
that person's old age. It is all lies, then, when old men pray to die,
complaining about old age and how long life is. When death comes near,
not one of them wants to die, and old age is not a burden to them any
more.

Chorus.
Stop, both of you. The trouble that is here is enough. My boy, do not
goad your father's temper.

Pheres.
Boy, who do you think you are abusing? Some Lydian, some Phrygian, that
you bought with your silver? Do you not know I am a Thessalian, and my
father was a Thessalian, a free man, legitimately born? You are going
too far. This is outrage. You fling a young man's words at me, and you
are not going to hit me with them and walk off like that. I fathered you
to be the master of this house, and I brought you up. I do not owe you
my death. That is not a law I inherited from my fathers, that fathers
die for their sons, and it is not a Greek one either. You were born for
yourself, lucky or unlucky, and what you had a right to get from me, you
have. You rule many people, and I will leave you broad acres, because I
got them from my father. So how have I wronged you? What am I robbing
you of? Do not die for this man here, and I will not die for you.

You love looking at the light. Do you think your father does not?
Believe me, I count the time down below as long, and life as short, but
sweet all the same. You, at any rate, fought shamelessly not to die, and
you are alive, past the death that was appointed for you, because you
killed her. And then you talk about my cowardice, you, the worst of men,
beaten by a woman, who died for you, her fine young husband? What a
clever scheme you have found for never dying at all: talk whatever wife
you have at the time into dying for you, every time. And then you insult
your own family for not being willing to do it, when you are the coward
yourself? Be quiet. And think about this: if you love your own life,
everyone loves theirs. And if you go on speaking ill of me, you will
hear plenty of evil about yourself, and none of it false.

Chorus.
Too much evil has been said, now and before. Stop, old man, abusing your
son.

Admetus.
Speak. I have had my say. If it hurts you to hear the truth, you should
not have wronged me.

Pheres.
I would have done you more wrong by dying for you.

Admetus.
So it is the same thing for a young man to die and an old one?

Pheres.
We owe one life. Not two.

Admetus.
Then may you live longer than Zeus.

Pheres.
You curse your parents, when they have done you no wrong?

Admetus.
I saw you were in love with a long life.

Pheres.
And this body you are carrying out, is it not in your place?

Admetus.
A sign of your cowardice, you coward.

Pheres.
She did not die at my hands. You cannot say that.

Admetus.
Oh, may you come to need this man some day.

Pheres.
Court plenty of wives, so there will be more of them to die.

Admetus.
That is a reproach to you. You were the one who would not die.

Pheres.
This light of the god is sweet. It is sweet.

Admetus.
That is a mean spirit, and not a man's.

Pheres.
You will not be laughing as you carry out an old man's corpse.

Admetus.
You will die with a bad name, when you do die.

Pheres.
A bad name does not bother me once I am dead.

Admetus.
Oh, oh. How shameless old age is.

Pheres.
She was not shameless. It was a fool you found in her.

Admetus.
Go away, and let me bury this body.

Pheres.
I am going. You bury her, you, her murderer. And you will still pay for
it to her family. Acastus is no longer a man, if he does not make you
answer for his sister's blood.

Admetus.
Get out, you and the woman who lives with you. Grow old childless,
though your son is alive, the way you deserve. You will never again come
under the same roof as this man. If I had to renounce your hearth, my
father's hearth, by heralds' proclamation, I would have renounced it.

(Pheres goes.)

And now, because the trouble in front of us has to be borne, let us go
and lay the body on the pyre.

Chorus.
	Oh. Oh. What nerve you had, and what it cost you.
	Noble woman, best of all by far,
	goodbye. May Hermes of the world below receive you
	gladly, and Hades too. And if there, as well,
	the good have anything more, may you have your share of it
	and sit beside the bride of Hades.

(The funeral procession goes out. The Servant comes out of the house.)

Servant.
I have known many guests already, from every kind of country, come to
the house of Admetus, and I have set dinner in front of them. But I have
never yet taken in a worse one at this hearth than this one. First of
all, he saw the master was in mourning, and he came in anyway, and had
the nerve to walk through the gates. Then, he did not take what
hospitality we could give him with any decency, knowing our disaster.
No. If there was anything we did not bring, he shouted for it. He took
the ivy-wood cup in his hands and drank the black grape's dark juice,
neat and strong, until the flame of the wine wrapped round him and
heated him up, and he put myrtle branches on his head and howled, out of
tune. And there were two kinds of music to hear: he was singing, caring
nothing for the troubles in Admetus's house, and we slaves were crying
for our mistress. But we did not let the guest see our wet eyes, because
Admetus had ordered it so. And now here I am in the house, feeding a
guest, some crook, a thief and a robber for all I know, while she has
gone out of the house, and I did not follow her, or stretch out my hand
to her, mourning my mistress, who was a mother to me and to every slave
in the house. She saved us from ten thousand troubles, softening her
husband's tempers. So am I not right to hate this guest, who came in the
middle of our troubles?

(Heracles comes out of the guest rooms, with a wreath on his head and a
cup in his hand.)

Heracles.
You there. Why the solemn, worried look? A servant should not be
sour-faced with the guests. He should welcome them with a friendly mind.
You see a friend of your master's standing right in front of you, and
you receive him with a sullen face and your brows knitted, because you
are so taken up with a grief that belongs to somebody outside the house.
Come here, so you can learn a little wisdom. Do you know what mortal
life is really like, what its nature is? I do not suppose you do. How
could you? Well, listen to me. Every mortal owes a death, and there is
not a man alive who knows for certain whether he will be alive tomorrow.
Where fortune will go next is hidden. It cannot be taught, and no skill
can catch it. So now you have heard it, and learned it from me: cheer
yourself up, drink, count the life you have today as yours, and the rest
as fortune's. And honour the sweetest of all the gods to mortals,
Aphrodite. She is a kind goddess. Let all the rest go, and take my
advice, if you think I am talking sense, that is. I think I am. So will
you not drop this excessive grief, and drink with us, and put these
troubles behind you, with a garland on your head? I know for a fact that
the splash of wine falling into the cup will carry you out of this
sullen, knotted state of mind and into another harbour. We are mortals,
and we should think mortal thoughts. Because for all the solemn ones
with their knitted brows, if you take my judgement on it, life is not
truly life. It is a disaster.

Servant.
We know all that. But what we are going through right now does not call
for partying and laughter.

Heracles.
The dead woman is an outsider. Do not grieve too much. The masters of
this house are alive.
