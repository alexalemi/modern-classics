Hippolytus
(Part 1 of 3)

(Trozen, in front of the house of Theseus. A statue of Aphrodite
stands on one side of the door and a statue of Artemis on the other.
Aphrodite appears.)

Aphrodite.
I am a great power among mortals, and not a nameless one. My name is
Cypris, the goddess of Cyprus: Aphrodite. In heaven, too. And of all
the people who live between the Black Sea and the bounds of Atlas in
the far west and look at the light of the sun, the ones who respect my
power I put first, and the ones who think themselves too big for me I
trip up. Because gods are like this too: we enjoy being honoured by
human beings. And I will show you how true that is, and soon.

The son of Theseus, the boy the Amazon bore him, Hippolytus, whom holy
Pittheus brought up, is the only person in this land of Trozen who says
I am the vilest of all the gods. He refuses the bed. He will have
nothing to do with marriage. It is Artemis he honours, the sister of
Apollo, the daughter of Zeus. He counts her the greatest of the gods,
and he is always with the virgin in the green woods, clearing the land
of wild animals with his fast hounds, keeping company that is too high
for a mortal. I do not begrudge him that. Why should I? But for the
wrong he has done me, I will punish Hippolytus this very day. I cleared
most of the ground a long time ago. It will not take much work.

Once, he came from Pittheus's house to the land of Pandion, to Athens,
to see the holy mysteries and be initiated, and his father's noble
wife saw him. Phaedra. And her heart was seized with a terrible love.
That was my plan. And before she ever came to this land of Trozen, she
built a temple to Cypris right beside the rock of Pallas, on the
Acropolis, where it looks out over this country, because she was in
love with someone who lived across the water. She named the temple
after Hippolytus, and people will say for ever that the goddess was
set up there for him. Then Theseus left the land of Cecrops, to escape
the pollution of the blood of the sons of Pallas, whom he had killed,
and sailed here to this country with his wife, accepting a year's
exile from home. And here she is, groaning, struck to the heart by the
goads of love, the poor woman, dying, and in silence. Not one of the
household knows what her sickness is.

But this love is not going to end that way. I will reveal the matter
to Theseus, and it will all come out. And the young man who is my
enemy will be killed by his father, with curses. The lord of the sea,
Poseidon, gave Theseus a gift: three prayers to the god that would
never go to waste. As for Phaedra, she keeps her good name, but she
dies all the same. I will not rate her suffering so high that I let my
enemies off paying me a penalty big enough to satisfy me.

But here is the son of Theseus coming now, Hippolytus, back from the
hard work of the hunt, so I will leave this place. A loud crowd of his
men is coming behind him, shouting out, singing hymns in honour of the
goddess Artemis. He does not know that the gates of Hades stand open
for him, and that this light he is looking at is his last.

(Aphrodite goes. Hippolytus comes in with his huntsmen.)

Hippolytus.
	Follow me, singing, follow,
	for Artemis, daughter of Zeus, who lives in heaven,
	whose care we are.

Huntsmen.
	Lady, lady, most holy,
	child of Zeus,
	hail, hail to you, daughter
	of Leto and of Zeus, Artemis,
	by far the loveliest of virgins,
	you who live in the wide sky
	in your noble father's court,
	the golden house of Zeus.
	Hail to you, loveliest, loveliest
	of all the virgins on Olympus,
	Artemis.

Hippolytus.
For you, mistress, I have made this woven crown of flowers from an
untouched meadow, and I bring it to you. No shepherd thinks fit to
graze his flock there. The iron of the scythe has never come there.
Only the bee crosses that meadow in spring, untouched as it is, and
Modesty tends it like a garden, watering it with river dew. Nobody who
had to be taught may pick flowers there. Only those who have it in
their nature, as their portion, to keep themselves in hand in
everything alike. For the bad, it is not allowed. So, dear mistress,
take this band for your golden hair, from a hand that reveres you. I
alone of mortals have this privilege. I am with you, I talk with you
and you answer me, I hear your voice, though I do not see your face.
And may I round the last turn of my life the way I began it.

Servant.
My lord, since only the gods should be called masters, would you take a
piece of good advice from me?

Hippolytus.
Of course. I would look a fool otherwise.

Servant.
Then you know the rule that holds among human beings?

Hippolytus.
No. What rule are you asking me about?

Servant.
To hate what is stiff and proud, and does not make itself a friend to
everyone.

Hippolytus.
Quite right. What stiff and proud man is not a burden?

Servant.
And is there a certain charm in people who are easy to talk to?

Hippolytus.
A great deal of it. And profit, for very little trouble.

Servant.
Do you think the same holds among the gods?

Hippolytus.
Yes, if we mortals take our rules from the gods.

Servant.
Then how is it that you are so stiff and proud yourself, and do not
greet a goddess?

Hippolytus.
Which goddess? Careful. Do not let your tongue slip.

Servant.
This one. Cypris, who stands here at your gate.

Hippolytus.
I greet her from a distance. I am pure.

Servant.
She is a great goddess, all the same, and famous among men.

Hippolytus.
Different gods for different men, and different men for different
gods.

Servant.
I wish you luck, and as much sense as you need.

Hippolytus.
No god that is worshipped at night has any appeal for me.

Servant.
The gods have their honours, boy, and we have to give them.

Hippolytus.
Go on in, men, into the house, and see to the food. A full table is a
pleasure after a hunt. And rub down the horses, so that when I have
eaten my fill I can yoke them to the chariot and give them the
exercise they need. As for your Cypris, I bid her a long goodbye.

(Hippolytus goes into the house with his huntsmen. The Servant stays
behind and turns to the statue of Aphrodite.)

Servant.
As for us, since the young are not to be copied when they think like
that, we will speak the way slaves should speak, and pray to your
image, Lady Cypris. You should forgive him. If somebody with the hot
young blood in his belly talks nonsense about you, pretend you did not
hear it. Gods ought to be wiser than men.

(The Servant goes in. The Chorus, women of Trozen, come in.)

Chorus.
	There is a rock, they say, that drips the water of Ocean,
	pouring a running stream from its cliff face
	where the pitchers are dipped.
	A friend of mine was there,
	soaking purple clothes
	in the river water
	and laying them out on the back
	of a warm rock in the sun.
	It was from her
	that the first word of my mistress came to me:

Chorus.
	that she is worn down on a bed of sickness,
	keeping her body inside the house,
	a thin veil shading her golden head.
	This is the third day, I hear,
	that she has kept her body pure
	of the bread of Demeter,
	her mouth taking nothing,
	wanting, with a grief she hides,
	to run herself aground
	on the wretched shore of death.

Chorus.
	Is a god in you, girl?
	Is it Pan who drives you wandering, or Hecate,
	or the holy Corybantes,
	or the Mother of the mountains?
	Or is it Dictynna, mistress of wild things,
	you have offended? Are you wasting away
	because you owe her offerings you never made?
	She ranges over the lakes
	and over the dry land, and across the sea,
	in the whirlpools of the salt water.

Chorus.
	Or is it your husband, the chief
	of the sons of Erechtheus, the well-born man,
	that some woman in the house is shepherding
	into a bed hidden from yours?
	Or has some sailor come from Crete
	and put in at the harbour
	that is kindest to seamen,
	bringing the queen news,
	so that grief over her troubles
	has tied her soul to her bed?

Chorus.
	A bad helplessness likes to live
	with the awkward temper of women,
	the helplessness of birth pains and of folly.
	That wind blew through my own womb once.
	And I cried out to the heavenly one who eases childbirth,
	Artemis, the mistress of arrows,
	and she comes to me always, and I envy no one,
	with the gods on her side.

Chorus.
	But here is the old nurse at the door,
	bringing her out of the house.
	The hateful cloud on her brows is growing.
	My soul longs to know what it is,
	what has ruined
	the queen's body and changed its colour.

(The Nurse comes out of the house with Phaedra, who is carried on a
couch by her women.)

Nurse.
	Oh, the troubles of mortals, the hateful sicknesses.
	What am I to do for you? What am I not to do?
	Here is the bright light you wanted, here is the open sky.
	Your sickbed is outside the house now.
	Every word you spoke was about coming out here,
	and soon you will be in a hurry to get back to your room.
	You change your mind in a moment. Nothing pleases you.
	What you have does not please you; whatever you have not got
	you think you would like better.
	Better to be sick than to nurse the sick.
	The one is simple. The other joins
	grief in the mind to labour with the hands.
	The whole of human life is painful,
	and there is no rest from the labour.
	If there is anything dearer than living,
	darkness wraps it in clouds and hides it.
	And so we show ourselves badly in love
	with whatever this is that glitters on the earth,
	because we have no experience of another life,
	and no proof of what is under the ground.
	We are carried along by stories, nothing more.

Phaedra.
	Lift my body, hold my head up.
	The joints of my poor limbs have come undone.
	Take my arms, my lovely arms, women.
	The headdress is too heavy for my head.
	Take it off. Let my hair fall over my shoulders.

Nurse.
	Courage, child. Do not throw
	your body about so.
	You will bear the sickness more easily with quiet
	and a noble spirit.
	Mortals have to suffer.

Phaedra.
	Aiai.
	If only I could draw a drink of pure water
	from a dewy spring,
	and lie down under the poplars
	in a long-grassed meadow, and rest.

Nurse.
	Child, what are you saying?
	Do not talk like that in front of a crowd,
	flinging out words with madness riding on them.

Phaedra.
	Send me to the mountain. I will go to the woods,
	to the pine trees, where the hounds that kill
	go treading, hard on the dappled deer.
	By the gods, I long to shout the hounds on,
	to hold a Thessalian spear
	and throw it past my golden hair,
	the barbed point in my hand.

Nurse.
	Child, why are you fretting after these things?
	What do you care about hunting?
	Why do you long for water from a spring?
	There is a dewy slope right by the walls
	where you could have your drink.

Phaedra.
	Artemis, lady of the salt lagoon of Limna
	and of the racing grounds that thunder with hooves,
	if only I were on your level fields,
	breaking in Venetian colts.

Nurse.
	What is this now, another wild word flung out?
	A moment ago you were off to the mountain,
	longing to hunt; now it is colts you long for
	on the sand where there are no waves.
	It would take a great prophet to say
	which god is pulling on your reins
	and knocking your mind off its course, child.

Phaedra.
	I am wretched. What have I done?
	Where have I wandered, away from good sense?
	I went mad. I fell. A god's ruin struck me down.
	Pheu, pheu. Poor me.
	Nurse, cover my head again.
	I am ashamed of what I have said.
	Cover me. Tears run down from my eyes
	and my face is turned towards my shame.
	Getting your mind straight again hurts.
	Madness is bad. But it is better
	to be destroyed without knowing it.

Nurse.
	I cover you. And when will death
	cover my own body?
	A long life has taught me a great deal.
	People ought to mix their friendships
	in moderation, one with another,
	and not to the very marrow of the soul.
	The heart's affections should be easy to untie,
	easy to push away and easy to draw tight.
	For one soul to be in labour over two
	is a hard weight, as I am
	in pain over this one.
	People say that a life held to strict rules
	trips you up more than it delights you,
	and makes war on your health.
	So I have less praise for too much
	than for nothing in excess,
	and the wise will agree with me.

Chorus.
Old woman, faithful nurse of Phaedra our queen, we see her wretched
state, but we cannot tell what the sickness is. We would like to hear
it from you.

Nurse.
I have asked and I know nothing. She will not say.

Chorus.
Not even how these troubles began?

Nurse.
You come to the same answer. She is silent about all of it.

Chorus.
How weak she is. How her body has been worn away.

Nurse.
What do you expect? This is the third day she has eaten nothing.

Chorus.
Is it ruin, some madness struck into her, or is she trying to die?

Nurse.
To die. She is starving herself to be rid of life.

Chorus.
That is astonishing, if her husband is content with it.

Nurse.
She hides the trouble. She says she is not ill.

Chorus.
And he does not read it in her face?

Nurse.
He happens to be out of the country.

Chorus.
And you do not force her? You do not try to find out what her sickness
is, and where her mind has wandered?

Nurse.
I have tried everything, and I have got nowhere. But I will not let
up, even now. You are here; you can be my witness to what I am like to
my master and mistress when things go badly for them.

Come, dear child. Let us both forget what was said before. You be
gentler; unknot that hateful brow, and change the road your mind is
on. And I, where I followed you badly before, will let that go and
find a better line of talk. If your sickness is one of the ones that
cannot be spoken about, here are women to help you set it right. But
if your trouble can be carried out to men, say so, so that it can be
told to doctors.

Well? Why are you silent? You should not be silent, child. Either
correct me, if what I say is wrong, or agree with what is said well.
Say something. Look over here.

Oh, I am wretched. Women, we are wasting our labour. We are as far off
as we were before. Words did not soften her then, and she is not
persuaded now.

But know this, and go on being more stubborn than the sea about it, if
you like. If you die, you betray your children. They will get no share
of their father's house. I swear it by the horse-riding queen, the
Amazon, who bore a master to rule over your children: a bastard with a
legitimate son's ideas of himself. You know him well. I mean
Hippolytus, the one who

Phaedra.
Oh.

Nurse.
That touched you, did it?

Phaedra.
You have destroyed me, nurse. By the gods, I beg you again: about that
man, be silent.

Nurse.
You see? You are in your right mind. And in your right mind, you still
will not help your children and save your own life.

Phaedra.
I love my children. It is another storm I am caught in.

Nurse.
Your hands are clean of blood, child?

Phaedra.
My hands are clean. It is my mind that carries the pollution.

Nurse.
Not from some enemy's spell, brought in from outside?

Phaedra.
Someone I love is destroying me. Neither of us wants it.

Nurse.
Has Theseus done you some wrong?

Phaedra.
May I never be seen doing wrong to him.

Nurse.
Then what is this terrible thing that is driving you to die?

Phaedra.
Let me do my wrong. It is not you I am wronging.

Nurse.
Not willingly, no. And if I fail, the fault will lie with you.

Phaedra.
What are you doing? Forcing me, hanging on to my hand?

Nurse.
And your knees. And I will never let go.

Phaedra.
It will be bad for you, poor woman, if you find out. Bad.

Nurse.
What could be worse for me than to lose you?

Phaedra.
It will kill you. And yet the thing brings me honour.

Nurse.
And then you hide it, when what I beg for is good?

Phaedra.
Yes. Out of what is shameful I am building something noble.

Nurse.
Then if you tell it, you will look more honourable still.

Phaedra.
Go away, by the gods. Let go of my right hand.

Nurse.
No. Because you are not giving me the gift you should.

Phaedra.
I will give it. I respect the reverence owed to your hand.

Nurse.
Then I will be quiet now. From here on it is yours to speak.

Phaedra.
Poor mother, what a love you loved.

Nurse.
The love she had for the bull, child? Or what do you mean?

Phaedra.
And you, my poor sister, wife of Dionysus.

Nurse.
Child, what is wrong with you? Are you speaking ill of your own
family?

Phaedra.
And I am the third. Look how I am being destroyed.

Nurse.
You have struck me dumb. Where is this going?

Phaedra.
That is where our misfortune comes from. It is not new.

Nurse.
I know no more of what I want to hear than I did.

Phaedra.
Pheu. If only you could say for me what I have to say.

Nurse.
I am no prophet, to know for certain what is hidden.

Phaedra.
What is this thing people talk about, being in love?

Nurse.
The sweetest thing there is, child, and the most painful, both at
once.

Phaedra.
I would say I have found the second one.

Nurse.
What are you saying? You are in love, child? With whom?

Phaedra.
Whoever he is, the Amazon's...

Nurse.
Hippolytus, you mean?

Phaedra.
You heard that from yourself, not from me.

Nurse.
Oh. What are you going to say, child? You have destroyed me. Women,
this is unbearable. I will not bear it and live. Hateful day, hateful
light I look at. I will throw myself down, I will let my body go, I
will be rid of life and die. Goodbye. I no longer exist. Because
people who keep themselves in hand fall in love with evil too. Not
willingly, but they do. Cypris is not a god after all. She is
something greater than a god, if there is such a thing, and she has
destroyed this woman, and me, and the house.

Chorus.
	Did you hear? Oh, did you hear
	the queen crying out
	her wretched suffering, not to be listened to?
	Let me die, dear one, before I come
	to the state of your heart. Io moi. Pheu, pheu.
	Poor woman, for these pains.
	Oh, the troubles that feed mortals.
	You are destroyed. You have brought your evil out into the light.
	What does this day, the whole long day of it, hold for you?
	Something new will come to its end in this house.
	It is no longer unclear where the fortune
	Cypris sends is setting, poor daughter of Crete.

Phaedra.
Women of Trozen, who live here on the furthest edge of the land of
Pelops, before now, in the long hours of the night, I have thought
about other things: about how human life comes to be destroyed. And it
seems to me that it is not by the nature of their judgement that
people go wrong. Plenty of people have good sense. No, you have to
look at it this way. We know what is right, and we recognise it, and
we do not work it out in practice. Some from laziness. Some because
they put some other pleasure ahead of what is right. And life has many
pleasures: long talks, and leisure, a pleasant evil, and shame. There
are two kinds of shame. One is not bad; the other is a weight on a
house. If it were clear which was which, and when, there would not be
two things spelt with the same letters.

Since that is what I think, then, there was no drug that could have
made me change my mind and fall the other way. And I will tell you the
road my judgement took. When love wounded me, I looked for the way I
could carry it best. So I began with this: to be silent and hide my
sickness. Because the tongue cannot be trusted. It knows how to lecture
other people about their attitudes, out of doors, and it gathers the
most evil of all to itself. Second, I planned to bear the madness
well, and to beat it by keeping myself in hand. And third, when I could
not master Cypris by those means, I decided to die. The best of my
plans; nobody will say otherwise. Because I want my good acts to be
seen, and when I do wrong, not to have many witnesses. I knew the act
and the sickness were disgraceful, and I knew very well, on top of
that, that I was a woman, a thing everyone hates. May she die the worst
of deaths, whoever she was, the first woman who started shaming her bed
with men from outside it. It was in noble houses that this evil began
among women. Because when what is shameful looks right to the good, it
will certainly look right to the bad. And I hate the women too who keep
themselves in hand in words, but who have the nerve, in secret, for
things that are not right. How do they ever, Lady Cypris, queen of the
sea, look their husbands in the face? How do they not shudder at the
darkness, their accomplice, and at the timbers of the house, in case
they find a voice?

Because this is the very thing that is killing me, friends: that I
never be caught shaming my husband, or the children I bore. Let them
live as free men, flourishing in freedom of speech, in the city of
glorious Athens, with a good name because of their mother. It makes a
slave of a man, even a bold one, when he knows his mother's or his
father's evil. There is only one thing, they say, that can hold its
own against life: a just and good judgement, in whoever has it. Time
shows up the bad among mortals when it happens to, holding up a mirror
to them as if to a young girl. May I never be seen among those.

Chorus.
Pheu, pheu. How beautiful self-control is, everywhere, and what a good
name it harvests among men.

Nurse.
Mistress, your misfortune, when I heard it a moment ago, gave me a
terrible sudden fright. But now I realise I was being stupid. Among
human beings, too, second thoughts are somehow wiser. Nothing has
happened to you that is out of the ordinary or beyond explanation. A
goddess's anger has fallen on you. You are in love. What is so
astonishing about that? So are a great many people. And then you are
going to destroy your life because of love? That is a poor bargain for
everyone who loves their neighbour, and everyone who is going to, if
they have to die for it. Cypris is not to be borne when she comes in
full flood. The one who gives way she goes after gently; but whoever
she finds proud and thinking himself too big, she takes hold of him
and, how do you think, humiliates him. Cypris goes through the air,
she is in the swell of the sea, everything is born from her. She is
the one who sows love and gives it, and every one of us on the earth
is her offspring.

People who have the writings of the old poets, and who live among the
Muses themselves, know how Zeus once longed for the bed of Semele, and
know how the lovely shining Dawn once carried Cephalus off to live
among the gods, for love. And yet they live in heaven still, and do
not run away from the other gods. They put up with it, I think, beaten
by what happened to them. And you will not put up with it? Then your
father should have begotten you on special terms, or under different
gods for masters, if you will not accept these laws. How many men do
you think, with perfectly good sense, see their beds sick and pretend
not to see? How many fathers help their sons carry Cypris when they
have gone astray? Because this is part of human wisdom: to keep what is
not good out of sight. Human beings should not work too hard at getting
life exactly right. They cannot even finish the roof that covers a
house to the last detail. And you have fallen into a misfortune as deep
as this, and think you can swim out of it? No. If you have more good in
you than bad, being human, you are doing very well indeed.

Dear child, stop thinking these evil thoughts. Stop your outrage. That
is all this is, outrage: wanting to be stronger than the gods. Have the
nerve to love. A god has willed it. You are sick; find some good way to
turn the sickness. There are charms, and words that soothe. Some cure
for this sickness will be found. Men would take a long time finding it,
if we women did not find the way.

Chorus.
Phaedra, what she says is more useful for the trouble you are in now.
But it is you I praise. And this praise is harder to hear than her
words, and hurts you more.

Phaedra.
This is the thing that destroys well-run cities and human houses:
words that are too fine. You should not say what pleases the ear. You
should say what will bring a person a good name.

Nurse.
Why the solemn speech? It is not fine words you need. It is the man. We
have to see it through as fast as we can, and tell him the truth about
you, straight out. If your life were not in a crisis like this, if you
happened to be a woman who kept herself in hand, I would never be
leading you this way for the sake of your bed and your pleasure. But
now it is a great struggle to save your life, and nobody can begrudge
that.

Phaedra.
You have said a terrible thing. Will you not shut your mouth and stop
letting out these shameful words again?

Nurse.
Shameful, yes. But better for you than fine ones. The deed is better,
if it saves you, than the name you will die boasting of.

Phaedra.
By the gods, and because what you say is well said but shameful, do
not go a step further. My soul is ploughed and ready for love already,
and if you dress up what is shameful in fine words, I will be spent on
the very thing I am running from now.

Nurse.
If that is how you see it, then you ought not to have done wrong. But
since you have, listen to me. That is the next best favour. I have love
charms in the house that soothe it. It has just this moment come into
my head. They will end this sickness for you without disgrace and
without harm to your mind, if you do not lose your nerve. But we need
something from him, the one you long for, a token, a word or a scrap
of his clothes, to tie the two of you into one love.

Phaedra.
Is the drug something to rub on, or something to drink?

Nurse.
I do not know. Want to be helped, child; do not want to be told.

Phaedra.
I am afraid you will turn out too clever for me.

Nurse.
Then you would be afraid of everything, believe me. What are you
frightened of?

Phaedra.
That you might tell any of this to the son of Theseus.

Nurse.
Leave it, child. I will arrange it all well. Only you, Lady Cypris,
queen of the sea, be my partner in the work. As for the rest of what I
have in mind, it will be enough to tell it to our friends inside.

(The Nurse goes into the house.)
