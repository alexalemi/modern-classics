Electra
(Part 2 of 3)

(The Old Man, who was Agamemnon's tutor, comes slowly up the path to the
farmhouse, carrying a lamb, a wineskin and a basket.)

Old Man.
Where is she? Where is my young lady, my mistress, Agamemnon's child,
the girl I brought up once? What a steep climb this house has, for an
old man with a wrinkled foot to get up. Still, when it is for the people
you love, you drag the bent old spine along, and the knee that keeps
giving way under you. Daughter. I have only just seen you there at the
door. Here I am, and look what I have brought you from my own flock:
this newborn lamb, pulled away from its mother, and garlands, and
cheeses I took out of the presses, and this old treasure of Dionysus,
ripe with the smell of it. Not much of it, but sweet to pour a cup of
into something weaker. Someone take these into the house for the guests.
As for me, I want to wipe my eyes on this rag of a cloak. They are wet.

Electra.
Why are your eyes wet, old man? Have my troubles reminded you of
themselves, after all this time? Or are you groaning over Orestes and
his miserable exile, and over my father, whom you once held in your arms
and brought up, and all for nothing: no good to you, and none to the
people you love?

Old Man.
For nothing. Yes. But all the same there was one thing I could not bear.
I went by his grave on the way here, a little off the road, and when I
found it deserted I fell on it and cried. And I opened the wineskin I am
bringing for your guests and poured him an offering, and I laid sprigs
of myrtle round the tomb. And on the altar itself I saw a sheep with a
black fleece, slaughtered, and blood that had not been spilled long, and
curls of yellow hair, cut off. And I wondered, child, who on earth had
the nerve to go near that tomb. It was not any Argive, that much is
certain. But perhaps your brother has come, in secret, somehow, and
found his father's poor grave and paid it honour. Look at the hair. Hold
it against your own and see whether the colour of the cut lock is the
same as yours. It usually happens that people who share a father's blood
come out alike in most parts of the body.

Electra.
That is not worthy of a clever man, old man, what you are saying. Do you
think my brother would come sneaking into this country, afraid of
Aegisthus? Him? He has more courage than that. And then, how is a lock
of hair going to match: one grown in the wrestling schools, on a
well-born man, and the other soft, a woman's hair, made with combing? It
is impossible. And you could find plenty of people, old man, with curls
of hair exactly alike, who are no blood relations at all.

Old Man.
Then go and stand in the footprint. See whether the print of his boot
measures the same as your foot, child.

Electra.
How could there be any print of a foot on rocky ground? And if there is
one, a brother's foot and a sister's would not be the same size, a man's
and a woman's. The male is bigger.

Old Man.
Is there nothing, then, even if your brother has come to this country,
nothing by which you could recognise the weaving of your own shuttle,
the cloth I once stole him away in and saved his life?

Electra.
Do you not know I was still a little girl when Orestes was driven out of
the country? And even if I had woven him clothes, how could he, a child
then, still be wearing the same ones now, unless his clothes grew along
with his body? No. Either some stranger took pity on the grave and cut
his hair for it, or else someone slipped past the watchmen of this
country and —

Old Man.
And where are the guests? I want to see them and question them about
your brother.

Electra.
Here they are, coming out of the house. Quickly, too.

(Orestes and Pylades come out of the house.)

Old Man.
Well, they look well-born. But that can be counterfeit. Plenty of
well-born men are bad. Still. Greetings to the guests. I wish them well.

Orestes.
Greetings, old man. Electra, which of your friends does this ancient
relic of a man belong to?

Electra.
This is the man who brought up my father, stranger.

Orestes.
What? This is the one who smuggled your brother out?

Electra.
This is the man who saved him, if he is still alive.

Orestes.
Ah. Why is he staring at me like that, as if he were examining the
bright stamp on a silver coin? Is he comparing me to somebody?

Electra.
Perhaps he is glad to see someone the same age as Orestes.

Orestes.
Yes, a man I love. But why is he walking round and round me?

Electra.
I am looking at it too, stranger, and I am amazed.

Old Man.
My lady. Daughter. Electra. Pray to the gods.

Electra.
For what? Something I have, or something I have not?

Old Man.
To take hold of a treasure you love, which a god is showing you.

Electra.
There. I call on the gods. Or what do you mean, old man?

Old Man.
Now look at this man, child. The man you love most.

Electra.
I have been looking for a while, wondering whether you have lost your
mind.

Old Man.
Lost my mind? When I am looking at your brother?

Electra.
What did you say, old man? That is a word I never hoped to hear.

Old Man.
That I see Orestes here. Agamemnon's son.

Electra.
What mark have you seen, that I should believe you?

Old Man.
A scar by his eyebrow. He fell and cut himself, once, in his father's
house, chasing a fawn with you, and it bled.

Electra.
What are you saying? I see it. I see the mark of the fall.

Old Man.
And you are still slow to throw your arms round the one you love most?

Electra.
Not any more, old man. My heart is convinced by your proofs. You have
appeared at last. I am holding you. I never hoped —

Orestes.
And I am holding you, at last.

Electra.
I never thought it would happen.

Orestes.
I never hoped it would either.

Electra.
Are you really him?

Orestes.
The one ally you have. If I can haul in the catch I have come for. And I
am confident I can. Or else we should stop believing in gods at all, if
wrong is going to win out over justice.

Chorus.
	You have come, you have come, oh, day so long in coming,
	you have blazed up, you have shown the city
	a beacon to see by: the man who long ago
	went out of his father's house into exile,
	poor wretch, and wandered.
	A god, my dear, a god is bringing
	our victory home.
	Lift up your hands, lift up your voice, send prayers
	to the gods, that with luck, with luck,
	your brother may walk into the city.

Orestes.
Well. I have had the sweet pleasure of your welcome, and later on we
will give it back to each other. But you, old man, you have come at
exactly the right time, so tell me: what should I do to pay back my
father's murderer, and my mother, his partner in an unholy marriage? Is
there anyone in Argos who still wishes me well? Or am I bankrupt all
round, the way my fortunes are? Who do I join up with? By night, or in
daylight? Which road do I take against my enemies?

Old Man.
Child, when you are down on your luck you have no friend. It is a rare
find, believe me, someone who will share the bad with you as well as the
good. As for you, you have been torn out by the roots as far as your
friends are concerned, and you have left them no hope, so hear me and
understand: everything you have is in your own right arm and in your
luck. Your father's house and your city are yours to take with those.

Orestes.
Then what do I do, to get there?

Old Man.
Kill Thyestes's son, and your mother.

Orestes.
That is the crown I came for. But how do I get my hands on it?

Old Man.
Not by going inside the walls, even if you wanted to.

Orestes.
He is well supplied with guards, and spearmen at his side?

Old Man.
You have it. He is afraid of you, and he does not sleep well.

Orestes.
Well. It is for you to plan the next move, old man.

Old Man.
Then listen to me. Something has just come into my head.

Orestes.
I hope it is something good, and I hope I see it.

Old Man.
I saw Aegisthus as I was crawling along here.

Orestes.
I like the sound of that. Where was he?

Old Man.
Near these fields. By his horse pastures.

Orestes.
Doing what? I see a hope coming out of nowhere.

Old Man.
Getting up a feast for the Nymphs, it looked like to me.

Orestes.
For children already born and reared, or for a birth on the way?

Old Man.
I know only one thing. He was getting ready to slaughter an ox.

Orestes.
With how many men? Or alone, with his slaves?

Old Man.
There was no Argive there. Only his own household.

Orestes.
There is nobody who will know me when he sees me, old man?

Old Man.
They are slaves. Slaves who have never seen you.

Orestes.
Would they be on our side, if we won?

Old Man.
That is the way with slaves. And a good thing for you.

Orestes.
So how do I ever get near him?

Old Man.
Walk where he will see you while he is killing the ox.

Orestes.
His fields are right by the road, then?

Old Man.
Yes, and when he sees you there he will invite you to share the feast.

Orestes.
A bitter guest he will find at his table, if a god is willing.

Old Man.
From there, work it out for yourself, as it falls.

Orestes.
Well said. And the woman who bore me. Where is she?

Old Man.
In Argos. But she will join her husband for the feast.

Orestes.
Why did my mother not set out with her husband?

Old Man.
She stayed behind. She is frightened of what the people say.

Orestes.
I understand. She knows the city looks at her sideways.

Old Man.
Something like that. An unholy woman is hated.

Orestes.
So how do I kill her and him at the same time?

Electra.
I will arrange my mother's murder myself.

Orestes.
And as for the other one, luck will set it right.

Electra.
Let this man help us with both.

Old Man.
It will be done. But how do you find a way to kill your mother?

Electra.
Go to Clytemnestra, old man, and tell her this. Tell her I have had a
child. A boy.

Old Man.
Had it some time ago, or just now?

Electra.
Ten days ago. The time a new mother keeps herself pure.

Old Man.
And how does that bring your mother's death any nearer?

Electra.
She will come, when she hears I have been through childbirth.

Old Man.
Come? Why? Do you think she cares about you, child?

Electra.
Yes. And she will cry over my child's low birth.

Old Man.
Perhaps. Now bring your story round the turn again, to the finish.

Electra.
Well then, if she comes, obviously, she dies.

Old Man.
Yes. She will come right up to the doors of your house.

Electra.
And then, is it not a small step from there to Hades?

Old Man.
Let me die once I have seen that.

Electra.
First of all, then, old man, take my brother the way —

Old Man.
To where Aegisthus is sacrificing to the gods right now?

Electra.
Then go and find my mother and tell her my news.

Old Man.
So that it sounds as if the words came from your own mouth.

Electra.
Your work starts now. You drew the first turn in the killing.

Orestes.
I would go, if someone would show me the road.

Old Man.
I will take you myself. And gladly.

Orestes.
Zeus of my fathers, be the one who turns my enemies to flight —

Electra.
and pity us. We have suffered pitiful things —

Old Man.
Yes, pity them. They are your own descendants.

Electra.
And Hera, you who rule over the altars of Mycenae —

Orestes.
give us victory, if what we ask for is just.

Old Man.
Yes, give them justice for their father. Give them their revenge.

Orestes.
And you, father, living under the earth by an unholy act —

Electra.
and Earth, our queen, whom I strike with my hands —

Old Man.
defend them, defend these children. You love them best.

Orestes.
Now come, and bring every dead man with you as an ally.

Electra.
all the men who wiped out the Phrygians at Troy beside you, spear in
hand —

Old Man.
and everyone who hates unholy men with pollution on them.

Electra.
Do you hear me, you who suffered dreadful things from my mother?

Old Man.
Your father hears all of it, I know. But it is time to go.

Electra.
And I say this to you now, in front of everyone: Aegisthus is to die.
Because if you are thrown in this wrestling match and go down dead, then
I am dead too. Do not call me a living woman any more. I will drive a
two-edged sword through my liver. I am going into the house to get
things ready. If good news comes from you, the whole house will ring
with women's cries of joy. If you die, it will be the opposite. That is
what I have to say to you.

Orestes.
I know. All of it.

Electra.
Then you must be a man now. And you, women, light me the signal fire of
a shout, when this fight is decided. I will stand guard with a sword
ready in my hand. Because if I am beaten, I will never let my enemies
take their satisfaction out of me, and outrage my body. (Orestes and
Pylades go out with the Old Man. Electra goes into the house.)

Chorus.
	Under the Argive hills, once,
	a lamb, still at its soft mother's side:
	the story keeps its place in the grey old tales,
	how Pan, who breathes sweet music
	on his well-fitted reeds,
	Pan the steward of the fields,
	brought it down, a lamb with a fleece
	of gold, beautiful curls of gold.
	And a herald stood up on the stone steps
	and cried, "To the assembly, Mycenaeans,
	to the assembly, come and see
	the wonder, the terror, that has come
	to our blessed kings." And the dancers
	paid their honours to the house of Atreus.

Chorus.
	Altars of beaten gold were set out,
	and through the town the fire
	blazed on the altars of the Argives.
	The pipe, the servant of the Muses,
	rang out its loveliest note,
	and lovely songs went swelling up
	in praise of the golden lamb: Thyestes's, they sang,
	Thyestes's luck. For he had talked
	the dear wife of Atreus into a secret bed,
	and carried the wonder off to his own house,
	and went into the assembly and shouted
	that he had the horned sheep
	with the golden fleece at home. His.

Chorus.
	Then, it was then, that Zeus changed
	the shining roads of the stars,
	and the light of the sun,
	and the white face of the dawn,
	and drove the sun across the western
	back of the sky with a hot flame from heaven's fire;
	the rain-clouds went north,
	and the seat of Ammon dried up
	and withered, knowing no dew,
	robbed of the lovely rain that Zeus sends.

Chorus.
	That is what they say. I put small
	faith in it myself: that the sun
	turned his hot golden throne around
	and changed his course, for the misfortune of mortals,
	because of one man's crime and his punishment.
	But stories that frighten people
	are good for the worship of the gods.
	You never gave them a thought when you killed
	your husband, you, the sister
	of the famous brothers, Castor and Polydeuces.

Chorus.
Ah. Ah. Friends, did you hear a shout? Or is it an empty fancy that has
crept over me? Like the thunder of Zeus, under the ground. Listen. There
is a wind rising, and it is not without meaning. Mistress. Electra. Come
out of the house.

(Electra comes out.)

Electra.
Friends, what is it? How do we stand in the fight?

Chorus.
I know one thing only. I hear a cry, and it is a cry of killing.

Electra.
I heard it too. A long way off, but I heard it.

Chorus.
Yes, the sound is coming from far away. But it is clear enough.

Electra.
That groan was an Argive's. Was it one of ours?

Chorus.
I cannot tell. The whole tune of the shouting is mixed together.

Electra.
That is my death you are calling out. Why am I waiting?

Chorus.
Hold on. Wait until you know your fortune for certain.

Electra.
No. We have lost. Where are the messengers?

Chorus.
They will come. It is no small thing to kill a king.

(A Messenger runs in.)

Messenger.
Women of Mycenae, girls in glory, I bring news to all his friends:
Orestes has won, and Agamemnon's murderer, Aegisthus, is lying on the
ground. Now we must pray to the gods.

Electra.
Who are you? How do I know this news of yours is true?

Messenger.
Do you not know your brother's servant when you look at him?

Electra.
Dearest of men. I was so frightened I could not place your face. Now I
know you. What are you saying? Is he dead, my father's hateful murderer?

Messenger.
Dead. I say it twice, since it is what you want to hear.

Electra.
Gods. And Justice, who sees everything: you have come at last. How did
he do it? What kind of death did he give Thyestes's son? I want to hear
it.

Messenger.
When we left this house we came out onto the wide cart-road, the one
with the two ruts, and walked to where the famous king of Mycenae was.
He turned out to be walking in a garden full of running water, picking
sprays of tender myrtle to make a wreath for his head, and when he saw
us he called out, "Welcome, strangers. Who are you? Where have you come
from, and what country are you from?" And Orestes said, "Thessalians. We
are on our way to the Alpheus to sacrifice to Olympian Zeus." When
Aegisthus heard that, he said, "Then you must stay and share the feast
with me, as my guests. As it happens I am sacrificing an ox to the
Nymphs. Get up at dawn tomorrow and you will be no worse off. Come, let
us go inside." And even while he was saying it he had taken us by the
hand and was leading us off the road. "I will not have you refuse."

And when we were in the house he said, "Somebody fetch water for the
guests, quickly, so they can stand round the altar by the basin." But
Orestes said, "We have just purified ourselves, in clean water, in the
running stream of a river. If strangers may sacrifice alongside
citizens, Aegisthus, we are ready. We will not refuse you, my lord." So
they let that conversation drop, out of the middle of things. The slaves
who made up the master's bodyguard put down their spears, and every one
of them set his hands to the work. Some brought the bowl for the blood,
others lifted the baskets, others lit the fire and set the cauldrons
upright round the hearth, and the whole roof rang with it.

Then your mother's bedfellow took the barley grains and threw them on
the altars, and this is what he said as he did it: "Nymphs of the rocks,
grant that I and my wife at home, the daughter of Tyndareus, may
sacrifice like this many times over, doing as well as we are doing now,
and that my enemies do badly." Meaning Orestes and you. And my master
prayed for the opposite, not speaking the words out loud: to take back
his father's house. Aegisthus took a long straight knife out of the
basket, cut off some of the calf's hair and laid it on the holy fire
with his right hand, and when the slaves had lifted the calf up on their
shoulders he cut its throat. And he said this to your brother: "One of
the fine things Thessalians boast of, they say, is this: a Thessalian
knows how to cut up a bull properly, and how to break horses. Take the
knife, stranger, and show us that the talk about Thessalians is true."

He grabbed the well-hammered Dorian blade in his hands, threw the
handsome pinned cloak off his shoulders, picked Pylades to help him with
the work and pushed the slaves back. Then he took the calf by the hoof
and stripped the white flesh bare, arm outstretched, and he had the hide
off it quicker than a runner does two laps of the horse-track, and laid
the flanks open. Aegisthus took the sacred parts in his hands and
studied them. And the liver had no lobe. And the portal vein and the
gall-bladder beside it showed the man who was reading them bad things
coming his way. He scowled, and my master asked him, "Why so
downhearted?" "Stranger, I am afraid of some treachery from outside. Of
all men alive the one I hate most is Agamemnon's son, and he is my
house's enemy." And Orestes said, "You are afraid of treachery from an
exile? You, the ruler of a city? Come. So that we can feast on the
innards, will somebody bring me a Thessalian cleaver instead of this
Dorian knife, and let me break the breastbone open?" He took it and
chopped. Aegisthus picked up the innards and was studying them, sorting
them out. And as he bent his head down, your brother rose up on the tips
of his toes and struck him on the spine, and broke the joints of his
backbone apart. His whole body heaved up and down, he thrashed, he
screamed, he died hard, in his blood.

When the slaves saw it they ran straight for their spears, a crowd of
them to fight two men. But Pylades and Orestes stood their ground like
men, facing them head on, shaking their weapons. And he said, "I have
not come as an enemy to this city, or to my own servants. I have paid
back my father's murderer. I am Orestes, the wretched Orestes. Do not
kill me, old slaves of my father's." And when they heard what he said
they held their spears back, and an old man who had been in the
household a long time recognised him. And at once they were putting a
wreath on your brother's head, cheering, shouting for joy. And now he is
coming to show you a head. Not the Gorgon's. The man you hate:
Aegisthus. Blood for blood. The dead man has paid off a bitter loan
today.

Chorus.
	Set your feet dancing, my dear,
	like a fawn leaping high as heaven,
	light with joy.
	Your brother has won a crown, and it outshines
	any that was ever won beside the streams of Alpheus.
	Come, sing a song of glorious victory
	to go with my dance.

Electra.
Light. Blaze of the sun and his four horses. Earth, and the night that
was all I could see before. Now my eyes are free, and I can open them,
because Aegisthus, my father's murderer, has fallen. Come, friends.
Whatever I own, whatever the house has hidden away, to make hair
beautiful, let me bring it out, and I will crown my brother's head. He
has won.

(Electra goes into the house. Orestes and Pylades come in, and behind
them slaves carrying the body of Aegisthus.)

Chorus.
	You bring out the ornaments
	for his head. Our dance goes on,
	the dance the Muses love.
	Now the kings we loved before
	will rule this land again, and justly,
	having brought down the unjust.
	Let the shout go up, and the pipe with it, for joy.

(Electra comes out again with garlands in her hands.)

Electra.
Glorious winner. Orestes, son of a father who won the war under Troy,
take these bands for the curls of your hair. You have come home from the
race, and it was not a useless one, not a sprint down the track for a
prize: you killed our enemy, Aegisthus, who destroyed your father and
mine. And you, his shield-mate, Pylades, brought up by the most
god-fearing of men, take a garland from my hand. You carried an equal
share of this contest with him. May I always see the two of you as lucky
as you are today.

Orestes.
First, Electra, think of the gods as the leaders of this good luck of
ours, and then, after them, praise me, the servant of the gods and of
fortune. I have come back having killed Aegisthus, and not in words. In
deeds. And so that you may know it for certain, I have brought you the
dead man himself. Do what you like with him. Put him out for the wild
animals to tear, or drive a stake through him and set him up as a prize
for the birds, the children of the air. He is your slave now, the man
who used to be called your master.

Electra.
I am ashamed to. But I want to say it all the same.

Orestes.
Say what? Speak. You are out of reach of fear now.

Electra.
To outrage the dead. I am ashamed to. Someone might throw it back in my
face out of spite.

Orestes.
There is no one who would blame you.
