Iphigenia Among the Taurians
(Part 1 of 3)

(The land of the Taurians, on the shore of the Black Sea. In front of
the temple of Artemis stands an altar, stained dark, with trophies hung
on the wall above it. Iphigenia, the priestess, comes out of the
temple.)

Iphigenia.
Pelops, the son of Tantalus, came to Pisa with his fast horses and
married the daughter of Oenomaus. From her Atreus was born, and the sons
of Atreus were Menelaus and Agamemnon. Agamemnon is my father. I am
Iphigenia, his child, and my mother was the daughter of Tyndareus. And
my father killed me. Or so he believes. He cut my throat for Artemis,
for Helen's sake, in the famous bay of Aulis, beside the narrows where
the Euripus spins in its whirlpools and twists the dark blue water round
in the constant winds.

That is where lord Agamemnon gathered his fleet, a thousand Greek ships,
wanting to take the victor's crown of Troy for the Achaeans and to
punish the outrage done to Helen's marriage. He was doing Menelaus a
favour. But the winds turned terrible and the ships could not sail, and
he went to the burnt offerings to find out why, and Calchas told him
this. "Agamemnon, commander of this Greek army, you will not get your
ships off this shore until Artemis has your daughter Iphigenia, with her
throat cut. You made a vow once, to sacrifice to the goddess who carries
the torch the most beautiful thing the year brought forth. Well, that
year your wife Clytemnestra bore you a child in your house" (and there
he was handing the prize for beauty to me) "and that child you must
sacrifice." So Odysseus worked one of his tricks, and they took me away
from my mother on the pretext that I was to marry Achilles.

I came to Aulis. Poor girl. They lifted me high over the altar and were
killing me with the sword. But Artemis stole me away. She gave the
Achaeans a deer in my place, and carried me through the bright air and
set me down to live here, in the land of the Taurians, where a barbarian
rules barbarians: Thoas, whose name means Swift, and who earned it
because he runs as fast as a bird flies. She made me priestess in this
temple. And so, by the customs Artemis takes pleasure in, I preside at a
festival whose name is the only fine thing about it. The rest I keep
quiet about; I am afraid of the goddess. I sacrifice, by a law this city
had before I came, any Greek man who lands in this country. I begin the
rite, that is all. The killing itself is other people's business, inside
the goddess's temple, and it is not to be spoken of.

But the night has brought me strange visions, new ones, and I will tell
them to the open air, if telling them is any cure. I dreamed I had left
this country and was living in Argos, sleeping among the unmarried
girls, and the back of the earth shook and heaved, and I ran, and stood
outside, and watched the cornice of the house come down and the whole
roof thrown to the ground in ruins from its high pillars. One pillar was
left standing, I thought, one, out of my father's whole house, and
yellow hair came down from its capital and it took a human voice. And I,
keeping up this trade of mine, this killing of strangers, sprinkled it
with the holy water as if it were about to die, and I was crying.

This is how I read the dream. Orestes is dead, and I was the one who
began the rite over him. The pillars of a house are its sons; and
whoever my holy water touches, dies. I cannot make the dream fit anyone
else I love: Strophius had no son when I was killed. So now I want to
pour the offerings for my brother, he far away and I here, since that
much at least I can do, with the attendants the king gave me, Greek
women. But why are they not here yet? What is keeping them? I will go
inside, into the goddess's temple, where I live.

(She goes into the temple. Orestes and Pylades come in cautiously from
the direction of the shore.)

Orestes.
Look. Watch out. Make sure there is nobody on the path.

Pylades.
I am looking. I am turning my eyes every way and checking.

Orestes.
Pylades, do you think this is the goddess's house? The one we launched
the ship from Argos to find?

Pylades.
I do, Orestes. And you had better think so too.

Orestes.
And the altar, where Greek blood drips down?

Pylades.
Well, the hair hanging on it is red with blood, at any rate.

Orestes.
And do you see the spoils hanging under the cornice itself?

Pylades.
Trophies. The first pickings from the strangers who died here. But we
ought to keep our eyes moving and look carefully all round.

Orestes.
Apollo, where have you led me this time, into what net, with your
oracles? Ever since I avenged my father's blood by killing my mother, I
have been hunted by Furies, relay after relay of them, an exile driven
off my own land, and I have run many a long lap of the course. I came to
you and asked you how I could reach the end of this madness that spins
me like a wheel, and of the labours I had worked through, wandering all
over Greece. And you told me to go to the borders of the Taurian
country, where your sister Artemis has her altars, and take the statue
of the goddess, which they say here fell into this temple from the sky;
and when I had taken it, by cunning or by some stroke of luck, and come
through the danger, to give it to the land of Athens. What came after
that, you did not say. And if I did this, I would have a breathing space
from my labours.

So I have come, obedient to what you said, to a country I do not know
and that does not welcome strangers. And I am asking you, Pylades,
because you are my partner in this labour: what do we do? You see how
high the walls are all round. Do we climb the steps up to the house? And
how would we not be seen? Or do we break the bronze-forged bolts of the
doors with crowbars, when we know nothing about them? If we are caught
opening the gates and rigging a way in, we die. No. Before we die, let
us run for the ship, the one we sailed here in.

Pylades.
Running is not to be borne, and we are not in the habit of it. And the
god's oracle is not to be turned into a coward's excuse. Let us leave
the temple and hide ourselves in the caves, where the black sea washes
in with its spray, well away from the ship, so that nobody sees the hull
and tells the king, and then we are taken by force. And when the eye of
dark night comes on, then we have to have the nerve to take the polished
statue out of the temple, whatever it takes. Look there, between the
triglyphs: there is a gap where a man could let himself down. Good men
have the nerve for hard labours. Cowards are nothing, anywhere.

Orestes.
No. We did not row all this way to turn round at the finish line and
head for home. You are right; we do as you say. We must go wherever on
this land we can hide ourselves and not be seen. It will not be the
god's fault that his sacred oracle falls useless to the ground. We must
have the nerve. No labour is an excuse when you are young.

(They go out toward the shore. The Chorus, Greek women who are slaves of
the temple, come in.)

Chorus.
	Keep holy silence,
	you who live by the twin rocks
	that clash together over the Unfriendly Sea.
	Daughter of Leto,
	Artemis of the nets, goddess of the mountains,
	to your court, to the gold cornices
	of your temple with its beautiful pillars,
	I bring my virgin step, holy to the holy one,
	I, the slave of the holy keeper of your keys.
	I have left the towers and the walls
	of Greece, the land of fine horses,
	and Europe, with its forests and its pastures,
	the home of my fathers.
	I am here. What is it? What is on your mind?
	Why have you called me, called me to the temple,
	daughter of the man who went against the towers of Troy
	with his famous fleet,
	a thousand ships,
	ten thousand spears, the son of Atreus, that famous house?

Iphigenia.
	Io, women, slaves of this house,
	how I am caught in dirges that are hard to sing,
	in the cry of a song no Muse would own,
	in laments with no lyre, aiai,
	aiai, in wails for the dead.
	Ruin has come down on me.
	I am mourning my brother, mourning his life.
	Such a sight, such a sight I saw in my dreams
	in the night whose darkness has just gone out.
	I am lost. I am lost.
	My father's house is no more.
	Oh. Oh. The family is gone.
	Ah, ah, for the sorrows of Argos.
	Io, fate,
	who rob me of my only brother
	and send him to Hades. For him
	I am going to pour these offerings,
	the bowl of the dead, over the back of the earth:
	streams of milk from the cows on the mountain,
	the wine that Dionysus gives, poured out,
	and the labour of the tawny bees.
	These are the things that soothe the dead.
	Give me the golden vessel now,
	and the offering for Hades.
	Child of Agamemnon under the earth,
	I send you these, as to a dead man.
	Take them. To your grave I will never bring
	my yellow hair, or my tears.
	I live too far, far from your country
	and mine, where they believe I lie,
	poor woman, with my throat cut.

Chorus.
	I will answer you, mistress, song for song,
	a barbarian cry, the hymns of Asia,
	I will sing it out for you:
	the music that belongs to the dead
	in laments, the music Hades sings
	in his songs, with no note of triumph in it.
	Oh, for the house of the sons of Atreus.
	The light of the sceptre is gone, oh,
	the light of your father's house.
	Once there was a rule in Argos
	of kings the gods had blessed,
	and now trouble darts out of trouble:
	Pelops on his wheeling winged horses,
	and the man he threw into the sea;
	and the Sun, who moved out of his seat
	the holy, holy eye of his light.
	And pain came on pain to the house,
	for the golden lamb,
	killing on killing, grief on grief,
	and out of the Tantalids who died before
	the punishment comes down on the house,
	and fate hurries against you
	what no one would hurry to meet.

Iphigenia.
	From the beginning my fate went wrong,
	from my mother's belt undone
	and that first night; from the beginning
	the Fates who stand by at a birth
	pulled my upbringing tight and hard,
	the girl the Greeks came courting,
	the first-born child in the house,
	whom Leda's unhappy daughter
	bore and reared, because of her father's outrage,
	as a victim, a sacrifice with no joy in it,
	the answer to a vow.
	In a chariot drawn by horses
	they set me down on the sands of Aulis,
	a bride, oh, no bride at all
	for the son of Nereus's daughter, aiai.
	And now I live a stranger by the Unfriendly Sea,
	the sea that is no friend to strangers,
	in a house on barren ground,
	no husband, no child, no city, no friend,
	not singing to Hera at Argos,
	not weaving on the sweet-sounding loom
	with my shuttle the picture of Athenian Pallas
	and the Titans. No. I stain the altars
	with the blood of strangers, a ruin
	that drips blood and no lyre could play,
	and they cry out, and the cry is pitiful,
	and the tears they shed are pitiful.
	And now I forget all of that,
	and I weep for the brother who died in Argos,
	the one I left at the breast,
	still a baby, still small, still a young shoot
	in his mother's arms, at her breast,
	Orestes, who holds the sceptre in Argos.

Chorus.
Look, here comes a herdsman. He has left the shore of the sea, and he is
coming to tell you something new.

Herdsman.
Daughter of Agamemnon and of Clytemnestra, hear the news I am bringing.
It is strange.

Iphigenia.
What is there in it to startle anyone?

Herdsman.
Two young men have come to this country in a ship, escaping the dark
Clashing Rocks. An offering the goddess will love, a sacrifice for
Artemis. You cannot be too quick getting the holy water ready, and the
first cuttings for the rite.

Iphigenia.
Where are they from? What country's dress do the strangers wear?

Herdsman.
Greeks. That one thing I know, and nothing beyond it.

Iphigenia.
You did not hear a name? You cannot tell me what the strangers are
called?

Herdsman.
One of them was called Pylades by the other.

Iphigenia.
And his partner, the other stranger, what was his name?

Herdsman.
Nobody knows. We did not hear it.

Iphigenia.
How did you see them? How did you come across them and catch them?

Herdsman.
On the edge of the breakers, on the Unfriendly Sea.

Iphigenia.
And what do herdsmen have to do with the sea?

Herdsman.
We went down to wash the cattle in the salt water.

Iphigenia.
Go back to what I asked. How did you catch them, and in what way? That
is what I want to know. They have been a long time coming. Not once yet
has the goddess's altar been reddened with streams of Greek blood.

Herdsman.
We were driving our cattle, the ones that feed in the woods, into the
sea that flows in through the Clashing Rocks. There is a cleft there, a
hollow in the rocks broken open by the constant pounding of the waves,
where the men who dive for purple shellfish shelter. And there one of
our herdsmen saw two young men, and came tiptoeing back to us, ferrying
his footsteps on the very tips of his toes, and said, "Don't you see
them? Those are gods, of some kind, sitting there." And one of us, a
god-fearing man, lifted his hands when he saw them and prayed. "Son of
Leucothea the sea-goddess, guardian of ships, lord Palaemon, be kind to
us. Or is it you two sitting on our shore, the twin sons of Zeus? Or the
darlings of Nereus, who fathered the noble chorus of the fifty Nereids?"

But another man, an idiot, with the nerve that comes from having no
respect for any law, laughed at the prayers and said they were
shipwrecked sailors sitting in the cleft, afraid of our custom, because
they had heard that we sacrifice strangers here. Most of us thought he
was talking sense, and that we should hunt down the victims the goddess
is owed by the custom of the country.

And just then one of the two strangers left the rock and stood up, and
shook his head up and down, and groaned, and his hands shook to the
fingertips, wandering in a fit of madness, and he shouted like a hunter:
"Pylades, do you see her? This one? Don't you see her, the she-dragon of
Hades, how she wants to kill me, with her terrible vipers, mouths open
at me? And that one, breathing fire and slaughter out of her robes,
rowing the air with her wings, with my mother in her arms, a mass of
rock, to throw down on me. Oh, she is going to kill me. Where can I
run?" There were no such shapes there to see. He was turning the lowing
of the calves and the barking of the dogs into what he said were the
noises the Furies send out.

We huddled together, and sat there in silence, amazed. And he drew his
sword and rushed into the middle of the calves like a lion and struck at
them with the iron, into their flanks, driving it into their ribs,
thinking that this was how he was fighting off the Furies, the
goddesses, until the sea itself blossomed red with blood. Then every man
of us, seeing the herd falling and slaughtered, armed himself, and blew
the conch shells, and gathered the neighbours; because we thought
herdsmen would make a poor fight of it against strangers who were young
and well fed. It took a long time, but we filled our numbers. And the
stranger fell. The pulse of the madness let go of him, and the foam ran
down from his chin. And when we saw him fallen, so conveniently, every
man went to work, throwing, battering. The other stranger wiped the foam
away and looked after his body and covered him with the fine weave of
his cloak, watching for the blows as they came and doing everything he
could for his friend, tending him. Then the stranger came to his senses
and leapt up from where he had fallen, and saw the wave of enemies
breaking over them, and the disaster that was on the two of them right
there, and he groaned. And we did not let up. We kept throwing stones,
pressing in from every side. And then we heard that terrible cry of his:
"Pylades, we are going to die. But let us die the best way we can. Draw
your sword and follow me."

When we saw the two enemy swords swinging, we ran and filled the rocky
gullies. But if some ran, others pressed in and threw at them; and if
they drove those off, the ones who had just given way came back and
pelted them with stones again. And it was unbelievable: with all those
thousands of hands, not one of us managed to hit the goddess's victims.
In the end we got them, but hardly by courage. We circled round and with
the stones we knocked the swords out of their hands, and they sank on
their knees to the ground, worn out. And we brought them to the king of
this land. He looked at them, and as fast as he could he sent them to
you, for the holy water and the slaughter. This is what you have been
praying for, lady, strangers like these, for victims. Kill strangers
like these, and Greece will have paid for your murder. It will be
justice done for the slaughter at Aulis.

Chorus.
That is an amazing story you have told about the madman, whoever he is,
who has come from Greece to the Unfriendly Sea.

Iphigenia.
Well. You go and bring the strangers here. We will see to the holy
business at this end.

(The Herdsman goes back toward the shore to fetch the prisoners.)

Poor heart of mine. Until now you were calm with strangers, and always
full of pity, and you measured out your tears for our shared blood
whenever Greek men came into your hands. But now, because of the dreams
that have made me savage, believing Orestes no longer looks at the sun,
you will find me hard, whoever you are who have come here. And this is
true, I see it now, my friends: people who are unlucky, when they are
doing badly themselves, have no kind thoughts for people who are more
unlucky still.

But no wind from Zeus has ever come, no ship, that carried Helen here
through the Clashing Rocks, the woman who destroyed me, and Menelaus
with her, so that I could pay them back, and set an Aulis here against
the one there, where the Greeks took hold of me like a calf and cut my
throat, and the priest was my own father, the man who begot me. Oh. I
have not forgotten the evils of that day. How many times I threw my
hands at his beard, and clung to his knees, the knees of the father who
made me, and said things like this: "Father, this is a shameful wedding
you are marrying me into. While you are killing me, my mother and the
women of Argos are singing wedding hymns, and the whole house is full of
flutes, and I am being destroyed by you. Achilles was Hades after all,
then, not the son of Peleus, the husband you offered me, when you
brought me in your chariot to a bloody marriage by a trick." And I was
looking out through a fine veil, and I did not take my brother up in my
arms, and now he is dead, and I did not kiss my sister, out of modesty,
because I was going to the house of Peleus. I put off so many embraces
until later, thinking I would come back again to Argos.

Poor Orestes. If you are dead, what good things you have lost, what a
father's greatness you had coming to you.

And the goddess: I blame her cleverness. Any mortal who touches blood,
or lays a hand on a woman in childbed or on a corpse, she bars from her
altars, and calls him polluted. And she herself takes pleasure in
sacrifices of human beings. It is not possible that Leto, Zeus's bride,
gave birth to anything so stupid. For my part, I do not believe the
story of the feast Tantalus gave the gods, that they enjoyed eating his
son. And I think the people here, who are killers of men themselves, put
their own vile behaviour onto the goddess. I do not believe any god is
evil.

Chorus.
	Dark, dark, the meeting-place
	of two seas, where the gadfly
	that flew from Argos drove Io
	across the unfriendly swell,
	when she changed the land of Europe
	for the land of Asia.
	Who can they be, who left
	the reedy green waters of the Eurotas
	or the holy streams of Dirce,
	and came, came to this land that mixes with no one,
	where for the daughter of Zeus
	the blood of mortals wets
	the altars and the pillared temples?

Chorus.
	Was it to the double beat
	of pinewood oars in the surf
	that they sailed over the waves of the sea,
	a ship for a chariot, in breezes that fill the linen sails,
	racing each other for riches,
	to make their houses greater?
	Hope is a sweet thing, and it never has enough,
	and it is the ruin of human beings,
	the ones who carry off the weight of wealth,
	wandering over the swell, crossing to barbarian cities,
	all with the one idea.
	And for some the thought of wealth comes at the wrong time,
	and for others it comes in due measure.

Chorus.
	How did they pass the rocks that run together,
	how did they pass the sleepless shores
	of the sons of Phineus,
	running along the salt beach
	on the surf of Amphitrite,
	where the fifty daughters of Nereus
	dance in a ring and sing,
	with the wind filling the sails
	and the rudder creaking
	in its bed at the stern,
	on the south wind,
	or on the breath of the west wind,
	to the land of many birds,
	the white beach,
	the beautiful race-course of Achilles,
	along the Unfriendly Sea?

Chorus.
	If only, as my mistress prays,
	Helen, Leda's darling child,
	would leave the city of Troy
	and come here, and here,
	with the bloody water wound round her hair,
	die by my mistress's hand,
	her throat cut,
	and pay the penalty that matches her crime.
	But the sweetest news I could hear
	would be that someone had come
	from the land of Greece, a sailor,
	to put an end to my miserable slavery.
	Even in dreams let me be
	in my home, in my father's city,
	enjoying the pleasure of sleep,
	a happiness that belongs to everyone.

Chorus.
	But here they come, the two of them,
	their hands tied tight together, a fresh
	offering for the goddess. Be quiet, my friends.
	The first pickings of Greece
	are walking up to the temple now.
	The herdsman's report was no lie.
	Lady, if this city does these things
	in a way that pleases you, accept the sacrifice
	that the custom here holds out to you,
	the sacrifice that Greeks call unholy.

(Orestes and Pylades are brought in by the temple guards, their hands
bound. Iphigenia comes out of the temple.)

Iphigenia.
Well. First I must see that the goddess's business is done properly.
Untie the strangers' hands. They are consecrated now, and they should
not be in chains any longer. Go inside the temple and get ready what is
needed, and what the custom is, for what we have to do.

(The guards free the prisoners' hands and go into the temple.)

Ah. Who was the mother who bore you two, who was your father? And your
sister, if you happen to have one: what a pair of young men she is going
to lose, and be left without a brother. Who knows whom fortunes like
these will fall on? Everything the gods do creeps along in the dark, and
nobody knows what evil is coming; luck leads us off where we cannot see.
Where have you come from, poor strangers? You sailed a long way to reach
this country, and you will be a long way from home for a long time. For
ever. Down below.

Orestes.
Why are you crying over this, woman, whoever you are, and grieving in
advance for the evils that are coming to the two of us? I do not call it
clever, when a man is about to be killed, to try to conquer his fear of
dying by crying about it. Nor when a man with Hades this close and no
hope of safety pities himself; he makes two evils out of one, he gets
convicted of foolishness and he dies all the same. Let fate be. Do not
mourn for us. We know the sacrifices they do here. We understand them.

Iphigenia.
Which of the two of you is the one they call Pylades? That is the first
thing I want to know.

Orestes.
This man. If knowing it gives you any pleasure.

Iphigenia.
And a citizen of what Greek city, by birth?

Orestes.
What would you gain by learning that, woman?

Iphigenia.
Are you brothers, from one mother?

Orestes.
In love, yes. We are not brothers by birth, woman.

Iphigenia.
And you, what name did your father give you?

Orestes.
If I got my rights, I would be called Unlucky.

Iphigenia.
That is not what I asked. Put that down to fortune.

Orestes.
If we die without a name, nobody can laugh at us.

Iphigenia.
Why so grudging about it? Are you so proud?

Orestes.
It is my body you will be sacrificing, not my name.

Iphigenia.
Will you not even tell me what city is yours?

Orestes.
There is no profit in what you are asking. I am going to die.

Iphigenia.
And what stops you doing me this one favour?

Orestes.
Famous Argos. That is the country I claim as mine.

Iphigenia.
By the gods, stranger, is it true? You were born there?

Orestes.
From Mycenae, yes. It was a rich place once.

Iphigenia.
Did you leave your country as an exile? Or what happened to you?

Orestes.
I am in exile, in a way. Against my will, and willingly.

Iphigenia.
Then would you tell me some things I want to know?

Orestes.
It would be a small thing, beside my own bad luck.

Iphigenia.
I am so glad you have come from Argos. I have longed for it.

Orestes.
I have not. If you have, enjoy it.

Iphigenia.
Perhaps you know Troy. Everyone everywhere talks about it.

Orestes.
I wish I did not. Not even in a dream.

Iphigenia.
They say it is gone. Nothing left. Taken by the spear.

Orestes.
That is how it is. What you heard was not empty talk.

Iphigenia.
And Helen has come home again to Menelaus's house?

Orestes.
She has. And her coming was an evil to someone I love.

Iphigenia.
And where is she? She owes me an evil turn as well.

Orestes.
She lives in Sparta, with the man whose bed she shared before.

Iphigenia.
Thing that all Greece hates. Not only me.

Orestes.
I have had a taste of that woman's marriage myself.

Iphigenia.
And the Achaeans came home, as they are saying?

Orestes.
You bundle everything up and ask me all of it at once.

Iphigenia.
Before you die, I want to get some good out of you.

Orestes.
Cross-examine me, then, since you want it so much. I will tell you.

Iphigenia.
A prophet called Calchas: did he come back from Troy?

Orestes.
He is dead. That was the story in Mycenae.

Iphigenia.
Lady goddess, how good that is. And Odysseus, the son of Laertes?

Orestes.
He has not come home yet. But he is alive, they say.

Iphigenia.
Let him die. Let him never reach his country.

Orestes.
Do not curse him. Everything he has is sick.

Iphigenia.
And is the son of Thetis, the Nereid, still living?

Orestes.
He is not. The marriage he made at Aulis was for nothing.

Iphigenia.
It was a trick. The people it was done to know that.
