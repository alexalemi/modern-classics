The Suppliants
(Part 3 of 3)

Adrastus.
	Mothers, the groan
	for the dead under the earth:
	cry it out, shout it back, in answer to mine,
	when you hear me groaning.

Chorus.
	Sons. Oh, sons. A bitter word
	for the mothers who loved you to call you by.
	I am speaking to you, and you are dead.

Adrastus.
	Io. Io.

Chorus.
	And I for griefs of my own.

Adrastus.
	Aiai.

Adrastus.
	We have suffered, oh,

Chorus.
	the cruellest pains there are.

Adrastus.
	City of Argos, do you not see what has become of me?

Chorus.
	They see me too, a wretched woman
	with no children, my children gone.

Adrastus.
	Bring them near, the bodies of the unlucky men,
	dripping with blood,
	killed as they did not deserve, by men who did not deserve to kill them,
	the men on whom the whole contest was decided.

Chorus.
	Give them to me, so that I can fold
	my arms around them
	and hold my children in my embrace.

Adrastus.
	You have him. You have him.

Chorus.
	Grief enough, and the weight of it.

Adrastus.
	Aiai.

Chorus.
	And for the parents, nothing to say?

Adrastus.
	Listen to me.

Chorus.
	You groan for the pain of us both.

Adrastus.
	I wish the Theban ranks had cut me down in the dust.

Chorus.
	And I wish my body had never been yoked
	to a husband's bed.

Adrastus.
	Look at this sea of evils,
	mothers, poor mothers of these children.

Chorus.
	We have ploughed our cheeks with our nails,
	we have poured ashes over our heads.

Adrastus.
	Io. Io moi moi.
	Let the floor of the earth take me down,
	let a whirlwind tear me in two,
	let the flame of Zeus's fire fall on my head.

Chorus.
	Bitter the marriages you saw made,
	bitter the word Apollo spoke.
	The Fury of Oedipus, heavy with groans,
	left his house empty and came to you.

Theseus.
I was going to ask you something, while you were pouring out your
laments to the army, but I let it go; I left the words unsaid where
they were. Now I turn to Adrastus. Where did these men come from, who
stood out among mortals for their courage? Tell it, since you are
wiser, to the young men of this city. You know these things. I saw for
myself their deeds of daring, more than words could describe, the
deeds they hoped would take the city. But there is one thing I will
not ask you, or I would make myself a laughing-stock: which enemy each
of them stood against in the battle, or whose spear gave him his
wound. Those are empty stories, for the ones listening and for the one
telling them: a man who stood in the fight, with spears flying thick
in front of his eyes, and reported clearly who the brave one was. I
could not ask that, and I could not believe the men who have the nerve
to say it. When you stand face to face with the enemy you can barely
see even what you have to see.

Adrastus.
Listen, then. You give me a task I take on gladly: to speak the truth
about these friends, and speak it fairly, which is what I want to do.

Do you see that fine-looking man, the one the bolt flew through? That
is Capaneus. He had a great deal to live on, and he was the last man
to be proud of his wealth. He thought no more of himself than a poor
man does, and he kept clear of anyone who puffed himself up over his
dinner table and looked down on having enough. Goodness does not live
in stuffing the belly, he used to say; a moderate amount will do. He
was a true friend to his friends, whether they were there or not, and
there are not many of those. A character without a lie in it, a
courteous way of speaking, and no promise left unkept, to his own
household or to his fellow citizens.

The second I name is Eteoclus, who practised a different kind of
goodness. He was a young man short of money, and he held more offices
of honour than anyone in the land of Argos. His friends kept offering
him gold, and he would not let it into his house: he would not put his
character under the yoke of money and make a slave of it. It was the
men who did wrong that he hated, not the city, because a city is not
to blame if it gets a bad name through a bad pilot.

The third of them, Hippomedon, was like this. From boyhood he had the
nerve not to turn to the pleasures of the Muses and the soft side of
life. He lived in the fields, gave his body hard things to do, and
enjoyed it for the manliness of it: out hunting, delighting in horses,
bending a bow in his hands, because he wanted to give the city a body
it could use.

Then the son of Atalanta the huntress, the boy Parthenopaeus, the
handsomest of them all. He was an Arcadian, but he came to the streams
of Inachus and was brought up in Argos. And when he was grown, first,
as a foreigner living in another people's city should, he was no
nuisance to the city and gave nobody cause to resent him, and he was
no wrangler in arguments, which is what makes a man hardest to bear,
citizen or foreigner. He stood in the ranks like a born Argive and
defended the country. When the city did well he was glad, and when
anything went badly he took it hard. He had many lovers, men and as
many women, and he took care to do nothing wrong.

Tydeus I will praise greatly, and briefly. He was no shining speaker,
but behind a shield he was a formidable expert, clever at finding many
clever things. In judgement he fell short of his brother Meleager, but
he made his name the equal of his brother's through his skill with
the spear, because he had worked out an exact science of fighting. An
ambitious nature, and rich in ambition; a spirit that showed itself in
deeds, not in words.

So from what I have told you, Theseus, do not wonder that these men
had the nerve to die in front of the towers. To be brought up well
brings a sense of shame with it, and every man who has practised what
is good is ashamed to turn coward. Courage can be taught, if a baby
can be taught to speak, and to hear things it does not yet understand.
And what a person learns, he tends to keep into old age. So bring up
your children well.

Chorus.
	Io, my child. It was for misery
	I raised you, carried you under my heart,
	bore the pains of labour. And now
	Hades has all my work,
	all my wretched labour, and I have no one
	to look after my old age,
	I who bore a son. Poor woman.

Theseus.
And then there is the noble son of Oecles, Amphiaraus: the gods
snatched him alive into the depths of the earth, chariot and four
horses and all, and that is their praise of him, in plain sight. And
the son of Oedipus, Polyneices I mean: we would not be lying if we
praised him. He was my guest-friend before he left the city of Cadmus
and crossed over to Argos, an exile by his own choice. But do you know
what I want to do about these men?

Adrastus.
I know only one thing: to do what you tell me.

Theseus.
Capaneus, the one Zeus's fire struck down,

Adrastus.
You want to bury him apart, as a holy corpse?

Theseus.
Yes. All the others on one pyre.

Adrastus.
Then where will you set his tomb, apart from them?

Theseus.
Right here, beside this temple. I have built the grave.

Adrastus.
That is work for the slaves, and they should be at it now.

Theseus.
And these men are our work. Let the dead be carried forward.

Adrastus.
Go to your children, poor mothers. Go close to them.

Theseus.
Adrastus, that is the last thing you should be saying.

Adrastus.
Why? Must the mothers not touch their children?

Theseus.
It would kill them to see them changed like this.

Adrastus.
Yes. The blood and the wounds of the dead are a bitter sight.

Theseus.
Then why do you want to add to their grief?

Adrastus.
You win. You must wait, and bear it. Theseus is right. When we have
put them on the fire, you will gather up the bones. Wretched human
beings. Why do you get yourselves spears and deal out death to one
another? Stop it. Give up this labour, and keep your cities in peace,
quiet people among quiet people. Life is a small thing. We should get
through it as easily as we can, and not with pain.

Chorus.
	No longer blessed in children, no longer
	blessed with sons; no share for me now
	in the good fortune of the Argive women who have borne boys.
	Artemis who watches over childbirth
	would not speak a word to women with no children.
	Life is an ill wind,
	and like some wandering cloud
	I am driven along by winter storms.

Chorus.
	Seven mothers, and seven sons
	we bore, we wretched women,
	the most famous men in Argos.
	And now, no son, no child,
	I grow old in utter misery,
	counted neither among the dead
	nor among the living,
	with a fate of my own, apart from both.

Chorus.
	What is left to me is tears.
	In my house the sad memorials of my son
	lie stored: hair cut in mourning,
	garlands for his head,
	drink-offerings for the dead and gone,
	and songs, songs that golden-haired
	Apollo will not accept.
	I will wake at dawn to wail,
	and the fold of my dress at my breast
	will be wet with tears for ever.

Chorus.
	And look: I see the burial chamber now,
	the holy tomb of Capaneus,
	and outside the temple
	the offerings Theseus has set for the dead,
	and near this man the lightning killed
	his famous wife, Evadne,
	the daughter King Iphis fathered.
	Why is she standing high on the rock
	that towers over this temple,
	walking out along that path?

(Evadne appears on the rock above the temple, over the pyre of
Capaneus. She is dressed as she was for her wedding.)

Evadne.
	What light, what brightness
	did the sun drive across the sky that day,
	and the moon through the upper air,
	where the swift-running nymphs
	ride through the dark with their torches,
	the day the city of Argos
	built the wedding songs high,
	the songs of my happiness, for my marriage
	to my husband in his bronze armour, aiai, Capaneus.
	To you I have come, running from my house,
	driven wild, out of my mind,
	to step into the light of your pyre
	and into the same grave,
	to end my toiling life in Hades
	and the labours of living.
	The sweetest death there is
	is to die with the ones you love as they die,
	if a god decrees it.

Chorus.
There. You see the pyre, the one you are standing over, the
treasure-house of Zeus: your husband is inside it, struck down by the
torches of the lightning.

Evadne.
	I see the end,
	from where I stand. May luck
	go with my feet. For honour's sake
	I will launch myself from here,
	leap from this rock
	into the fire,
	and mix my body with my husband's
	in the blazing flame,
	my dear skin laid against his skin,
	and come to Persephone's bridal chambers,
	never betraying you, dead
	under the earth, to save my own life.
	Let the light go, and marriage with it.
	And in Argos, let honest weddings
	and honest marriages appear
	for my children to see. And let the husband
	who shares the bed be true, true,
	melted into one with the guileless spirit
	of a noble wife.

Chorus.
And look, here is your own father coming this way, old Iphis, to hear
news he has not heard before. It will hurt him to hear it.

Iphis.
Poor women. And I am a poor old man too. I have come with a double
grief in my own blood. One is my son Eteoclus, killed by the Theban
spear: I have come to ship his body home to his own country. And I am
looking for my daughter, the wife of Capaneus, who has rushed out of
the house and gone, because she is in love with dying with her
husband. Up to now she was watched, in the house. But when I let the
guard drop, because of the troubles on us, she went. We think she is
most likely to be here. Tell me if you have seen her.

Evadne.
Why ask them? Here I am, on the rock, father, above the pyre of
Capaneus, hovering like a bird, a wretched thing hanging in the air.

Iphis.
Child, what wind blew you here? What journey is this? Why did you slip
out of the house and come to this country?

Evadne.
You would be angry if you heard my plans. I do not want you to hear
them, father.

Iphis.
What? Is it not right for your own father to know?

Evadne.
You would not be a wise judge of my decision.

Iphis.
And why have you dressed yourself up like this?

Evadne.
This dress means something glorious, father.

Iphis.
You do not look like a woman in mourning for her husband.

Evadne.
No. I am dressed for something new.

Iphis.
And you show yourself like that, beside a tomb and a pyre?

Evadne.
Yes. This is where I come as the winner, wearing the crown.

Iphis.
Winning what? What victory? I want to learn that from you.

Evadne.
Over every woman the sun looks down on.

Iphis.
In Athena's handiwork, or in good sense?

Evadne.
In courage. I am going to lie down dead beside my husband.

Iphis.
What are you saying? What is this rotten riddle you are hinting at?

Evadne.
I am going to jump into this pyre, where Capaneus lies dead.

Iphis.
Daughter, do not say that in front of all these people.

Evadne.
That is exactly what I want. For every Argive to know it.

Iphis.
But I will not let you do it. I will not stand for it.

Evadne.
It makes no difference. You will never catch me, never get your hand
on me. There. My body is let go. Not dear to you, but dear to me, and
to the husband who burns with me.

(She leaps from the rock into the pyre.)

Chorus.
Io. Woman, what a terrible thing you have done.

Iphis.
I am finished. Daughters of Argos, I am destroyed.

Chorus.
	E, e. You have suffered a cruel thing,
	and now you will look at what she dared, poor man, the whole of it.

Iphis.
	You could not find another man more wretched.

Chorus.
	Io, poor man.
	You have taken your share of the fortune of Oedipus, old man,
	you and my poor city both.

Iphis.
Oh. Why is this one thing not given to mortals: to be young twice, and
old again a second time? Inside a house, if something is not right, we
straighten it out with second thoughts; but a life we cannot. If we
were young twice, and old twice, then whenever someone went wrong he
would have a second life to put it right in. Look at me. I saw other
men having children and I fell in love with children; I was dying of
the longing for them. If I had come to this first, had been through it
and learned what it is for a father to be robbed of his children, I
would never have come to the evil I am in now: I fathered a son, and
raised him to be the best young man there was, and now I am robbed of
him.

Well. What should a wretched man like me do now? Go home? And look at
the emptiness of all those rooms, and no way forward for my life? Or
go to the house of Capaneus here? I loved to go there before, when my
daughter was alive. But she is gone now. She used to draw my beard to
her lips, and take this head of mine between her hands. There is
nothing sweeter to an old father than a daughter. Sons have bigger
spirits, but they are less sweet when it comes to fondness.

Take me home, quickly. Put me in the dark. There I will starve this
old body of mine, waste it away and destroy it. What good will it do
me to touch my child's bones? Old age, you are a hard wrestler. How I
hate having you. And I hate the men who want to stretch out their
lives, who use food and drink and magic to turn the stream aside so
that they will not die. When they are no use to the earth any more
they should die and be gone, and get out of the young people's way.

Chorus.
	Io. Io.
	Here they come, the bones
	of my dead children, carried in. Take hold of me, servants,
	a feeble old woman. There is no strength
	left in her, for grief over her sons.
	She has lived out the measure of a long time
	and wasted away with many sorrows.
	What greater suffering could you find
	for mortals than this:
	to see your children dead?

(The sons of the Seven come in, each carrying an urn of his father's
ashes.)

Children.
	I carry them, I carry them,
	poor mother, my father's limbs out of the fire,
	a weight that is not light, for the pain of it,
	though all I have is packed into this little space.

Chorus.
	Io. Io.
	Where are you carrying tears
	to the loving mother of the dead?
	A little heap of ash, in place of the bodies
	of men once so well thought of in Mycenae?

Children.
	No child. No child.
	And I, robbed of my poor father, wretched,
	will take an empty house and be an orphan in it,
	not in the arms of the father who made me.

Chorus.
	Io. Io.
	Where is the labour I spent on my children,
	where the thanks for bearing them,
	a mother's nursing, the nights my eyes did not sleep,
	the loving kisses on their faces?

Children.
	They are gone. They are no more. Oh, father.
	They are gone.

Chorus.
	The air holds them now,
	melted to ash in the fire.
	On wings they have finished the journey to Hades.

Children.
	Father, do you hear your children crying?
	Will I ever stand in the line with a shield
	and avenge your killing? If only it could be.

Children.
	One day, if the god is willing, justice will come
	for my father.

Chorus.
	This evil is not asleep yet.
	Aiai, the wailing. I have had enough of fortune,
	enough of pain already.

Children.
	One day the bright water of the Asopus will receive me
	in bronze armour, leading the army of the Argives,
	the avenger of my dead father.

Children.
	I think I still see you, father, before my eyes,

Chorus.
	laying your loving kiss against your son's cheek.

Children.
	But the encouragement of your words
	is carried off on the wind and gone.

Chorus.
	He left his grief to two: to his mother,
	and the pain for your father will never leave you.

Children.
	I carry a weight as heavy as what destroyed me.

Chorus.
	Come. Let me lay the dear ash against my breast.

Children.
	I wept to hear that word,
	the most hateful of all. It touched my heart.

Chorus.
	Child, you are gone. Never again will I see you,
	your loving mother's darling.

Theseus.
Adrastus, and you women of the Argive race: you see these boys holding
in their hands the bodies of their fathers, the best of men, whom I
recovered. I and the city give them to you as a gift. You must
remember this and keep your gratitude for it safe, looking at what you
have had from me, and say the same words to these boys: to honour this
city, and to hand on from children to children for ever the memory of
what you received. Let Zeus be the witness, and the gods in heaven, of
the treatment you had from us as you leave.

Adrastus.
Theseus, we know everything you have done, all the good to the land of
Argos when it needed help, and our gratitude will never grow old. We
have been treated nobly, and we owe you the same in return.

Theseus.
Then what else is there that I must do for you?

Adrastus.
Goodbye. You deserve it, and so does your city.

Theseus.
So be it. And may the same come to you.

(Athena appears above the temple.)

Athena.
Listen, Theseus, to these words from Athena: what you must do, and by
doing it help this city. Do not hand these bones over to the boys to
carry to the land of Argos, letting them go as lightly as that. In
return for your own labours and the city's, first take an oath.
Adrastus must be the one to swear it: he has the authority, being
king, to swear for the whole land of the Danaans. And this is what the
oath will be: that the Argives will never bring an army in full armour
against this land, and that if others come, they will put their spears
in the way. And if they break the oath and come, pray that the land of
Argos may be destroyed again, and miserably.

Now hear me on where you must cut the throats of the victims. You have
a bronze-footed tripod in your house, which Heracles once, when he had
torn up the foundations of Troy and was setting off on another labour,
charged you to set up at the Pythian hearth, at Delphi. Cut the
throats of three sheep over it, and engrave the oath inside the hollow
bowl of the tripod, and then give it to the god who cares for Delphi
to keep, a memorial of the oath and a witness for all Greece. And the
sharp-edged knife you open the victims with, and make the wound that
kills them: hide it in the depths of the earth, right beside the seven
pyres of the dead. If ever they come against the city, it will be
shown to them, and it will put fear in them, and send them home the
worse for it. When you have done all this, send the dead out of the
land. And the sacred ground where their bodies were purified by fire,
give up to the god, at the place where the three roads meet on the way
to the Isthmus.

That is what I have to say to you. To the sons of the Argives I say
this. When you are grown you will sack the city on the Ismenus,
avenging the killing of your dead fathers: you, Aegialeus, standing in
your father's place as a young general, and the son of Tydeus, coming
from Aetolia, the boy his father named Diomedes. As soon as the beard
shadows your cheeks you must launch the army of the Danaans, heavy
with bronze, against the seven-gated fortress of the Cadmeans. You
will come to them bitter, lion cubs grown up, and sack their city. It
cannot be otherwise. Through all Greece you will be called the
After-born, the Epigoni, and you will give the men who come after you
something to sing. That is the kind of army you will lead, with the
god's help.

Theseus.
Lady Athena, I will do as you say. You set me straight, so that I do
not go wrong. I will bind this man with oaths. Only keep me on the
straight road. As long as you are kind to the city, we will live in
safety from now on.

Chorus.
	Let us go, Adrastus, and give our oaths
	to this man and his city. What they have laboured for,
	on our behalf, deserves our reverence.
