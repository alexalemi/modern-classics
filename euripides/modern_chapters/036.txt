Helen
(Part 2 of 4)

Old Woman.
Get away from here. That is my one job, stranger: no Greek comes near
this house.

Menelaus.
Ah. Keep your hands off me. Do not shove.

Old Woman.
You will not listen to a word I say. That is your own fault.

Menelaus.
Go inside and tell your masters that I am here.

Old Woman.
And I would be the one to pay for it, I think, if I carried your
message in.

Menelaus.
I am a shipwrecked man, a stranger and a guest. People like me are
not to be touched.

Old Woman.
Then go and find some other house instead of this one.

Menelaus.
No. I am going in. Do as I tell you.

Old Woman.
You are being a nuisance, I warn you. And you will be thrown out any
minute now, by force.

Menelaus.
Aiai. Where are my famous armies now?

Old Woman.
You were somebody over there, I suppose. You are nobody here.

Menelaus.
Oh, my fortune. To be treated with this contempt, and deserve none of
it.

Old Woman.
Why are you wetting your eyes with tears? Who are you crying to?

Menelaus.
To the way things used to be. To my old happiness.

Old Woman.
Then go away and give your tears to your friends.

Menelaus.
What country is this? Whose palace is this?

Old Woman.
Proteus lives in this house. The country is Egypt.

Menelaus.
Egypt? Oh, misery. Look where I have sailed to.

Old Woman.
And what is wrong with the shining Nile, that you complain of it?

Menelaus.
I was not complaining about the Nile. I am groaning at my own luck.

Old Woman.
Plenty of people are doing badly. You are not the only one.

Menelaus.
So is he in the house, this king you named?

Old Woman.
This is his tomb. His son rules the country.

Menelaus.
And where would the son be? Out, or in the house?

Old Woman.
Not inside. And he is the worst enemy the Greeks have.

Menelaus.
What has he got against them? I am the one who has paid for it.

Old Woman.
Helen is in this house. The daughter of Zeus.

Menelaus.
What did you say? What are you telling me? Say it again.

Old Woman.
Tyndareus's daughter, the one who used to live in Sparta.

Menelaus.
Where did she come from? What is the story?

Old Woman.
She came here from the land of Lacedaemon.

Menelaus.
When? I have not had my wife stolen out of the cave, have I?

Old Woman.
Before the Achaeans went to Troy, stranger. Now get away from the
house. Something is going on inside, a stroke of luck that has the
whole house in an uproar. You have come at the worst possible moment.
If my master catches you, death is the gift you will get as his
guest. I am on the Greeks' side myself, whatever bitter things I said
to you. That was fear of my master talking.

(The Old Woman goes back into the palace.)

Menelaus.
What can I say? What can I make of this? After everything that went
before, what I am hearing now is one more misery to add to the pile.
I come here bringing my wife, the wife I took back from Troy, and she
is safe in the cave, and some other woman with the same name as my
wife is living in this house. The old woman said she was born a
daughter of Zeus. Is there some man by the banks of the Nile with the
name Zeus? There is only one in the sky. And where on earth is there
a Sparta, except the one place, by the streams of the Eurotas with
its lovely reeds? Tyndareus is a name one man has, and no other. What
country shares a name with Lacedaemon, or with Troy? I do not know
what to say.

But then, there are probably many people in the wide world who have
the same names, one city with another and one woman with another.
There is nothing to be amazed at. And I am not going to run away from
a servant's frightening talk, either. No man alive is so barbarian in
his heart that he would hear my name and refuse me a meal. The fire of
Troy is famous, and so am I, the man who lit it: Menelaus, and not
unknown in any country on earth. I will wait for the master of the
house. He gives me two things to watch for. If he is a savage sort of
man, I will keep myself out of sight and go back to the wreck. If he
shows some softness, I will ask him for what my present trouble calls
for.

This is the last and worst of the evils that have come on me in my
misery, to go begging my living from other kings when I am a king
myself. But it has to be. The saying is not mine, but it is a wise one:
nothing is stronger than dreadful Necessity.

Chorus.
	I heard the girl who sings the god's answers,
	what she made plain to the royal house:
	that Menelaus is not yet gone
	down into the dark where light goes black,
	hidden in the earth.
	No. He is still out on the salt swell,
	worn to nothing, and has not yet touched
	the harbours of his own country.
	Wandering for his living,
	wretched, with no friend left of all his friends,
	setting his foot on every kind of shore,
	his oar in the sea,
	out of the land of Troy.

(Helen comes back from the palace towards the tomb.)

Helen.
Here I am, back again to my seat at this tomb, and I have heard the
words I wanted from Theonoe, who knows everything, and knows it truly.
She says my husband is alive and in the light, and looking at the sun.
She says he has sailed the straits of the sea ten thousand times over,
driven this way and that, and that he is no beginner at wandering by
now, and that he will come here, when his troubles find their end. One
thing she did not say: whether, once he has come, he will get away
alive. And I held back from asking her that outright. I was so glad
when she told me he was safe. She said he was somewhere near this
country, thrown up on the shore, shipwrecked, with a few friends. Oh,
when will you come? How I have longed for you to come.

Ah. Who is this? I am not being ambushed, am I, by some scheme of
Proteus's godless son? Shall I not run for the tomb like a racing
filly, or a Bacchant of the god, and throw my legs at it? There is
something wild about the look of this man who is hunting me down.

Menelaus.
You there. The woman straining so desperately to reach the base of the
tomb and the pillars where the offerings are burned. Stop. Why are you
running? You have shown me your body, and the sight has knocked me
senseless. I cannot speak.

Helen.
Women, I am being wronged. This man is keeping me from the tomb. He
wants to catch me and hand me over to the king, whose marriage I have
been running from.

Menelaus.
I am no thief, and I do not do bad men's dirty work.

Helen.
And yet the clothes on your body are ugly enough.

Menelaus.
Stop running. Let go of your fear and stand still.

Helen.
I am standing still, now that I have my hand on this place.

Menelaus.
Who are you? Whose face is this I am looking at, woman?

Helen.
And who are you? The same question fits us both.

Menelaus.
I have never seen a body so like hers.

Helen.
Oh, gods. Recognising the people you love is a god too.

Menelaus.
Are you a Greek woman, or from this country?

Helen.
Greek. But I want to hear about you too.

Menelaus.
You look more like Helen than anyone I have ever seen, woman.

Helen.
And you like Menelaus. I do not know what to say.

Menelaus.
You have recognised him right. The unluckiest man alive.

Helen.
Oh, at last. At last you have come to your wife's arms.

Menelaus.
Wife? What wife? Do not touch my clothes.

Helen.
The wife Tyndareus, my father, gave you.

Menelaus.
Hecate, torch-bearer, send me visions that mean me no harm.

Helen.
You are not looking at a ghost of the night, one of the servants of
the goddess of the crossroads.

Menelaus.
One man cannot be the husband of two women. I am not.

Helen.
And what other bed are you master of?

Menelaus.
The woman the cave is hiding. The one I am bringing home from
the Phrygians.

Helen.
There is no other wife of yours. There is only me.

Menelaus.
Am I in my right mind, and it is my eyes that are sick?

Helen.
When you look at me, do you not think you are looking at your wife?

Menelaus.
The body is the same. What I know for certain takes it away again.

Helen.
Look. What more do you need? Who knows better than you do?

Menelaus.
You are like her. That much I will not deny.

Helen.
Then who is going to teach you, if not your own eyes?

Menelaus.
That is where I am sick. I have another wife.

Helen.
I never went to Troy. That was a phantom.

Menelaus.
And who manufactures bodies that can see?

Helen.
The air. That is where your wife came from. The gods laboured over her.

Menelaus.
Which god shaped her? You are telling me things nobody could hope for.

Helen.
Hera. A substitute, so that Paris would not get me.

Menelaus.
Then how could you be here and in Troy at the same time?

Helen.
A name can be in many places at once. A body cannot.

Menelaus.
Let go of me. I came here with grief enough already.

Helen.
You are going to leave me? And take away that empty bed?

Menelaus.
Yes. And good luck to you, for looking so like Helen.

Helen.
I am finished. I found my husband, and I will not have him.

Menelaus.
The size of what I went through over there convinces me. You do not.

Helen.
Oh, no. Was any woman ever more wretched than I am? The people I love
most are leaving me, and I will never reach the Greeks, or my own
country. Never.

(Menelaus's old sailor comes in, hurrying.)

Messenger.
Menelaus. I have found you at last. I have been searching for you all
over this barbarian country. The comrades you left behind sent me.

Menelaus.
What is it? You are not being robbed by the barbarians?

Messenger.
It is a marvel. And the word is smaller than the thing.

Menelaus.
Tell me. Hurrying like that, you are bringing something new.

Messenger.
I am telling you that you went through ten thousand labours for
nothing.

Menelaus.
You are mourning old troubles. What is your news?

Messenger.
Your wife is gone. She was lifted up into the folds of the air and
vanished. The sky is hiding her. She left the holy cave where we were
guarding her, and this is all she said. "Poor Phrygians, and all you
Achaeans. It was for me that you kept dying on the banks of the
Scamander, by Hera's contriving, thinking Paris had Helen when he did
not. And I, now that I have stayed as long as I was meant to stay and
kept what fate required, am going away to the sky, to my father. And
the poor daughter of Tyndareus has heard evil said of her name for
nothing. She was not to blame at all."

(He turns and sees Helen standing there.)

Well. Greetings, daughter of Leda. You were here all the time, were
you? I was just announcing that you had gone off to the hidden places
of the stars, and I had no idea you were carrying a body with wings on
it. I am not going to let you make fools of us like this a second
time. You gave your husband and his allies trouble enough at Troy.

Menelaus.
That is it. That is the answer. Her words fit together, and they are
true. Oh, the day I longed for, that has given you to my arms to hold.

Helen.
Menelaus, dearest of men, the time was long, but the joy is here now,
at last. I have my husband back, friends, and I am glad, and I have
wrapped my loving arms around him, after the long fire of so many
days' light.

Menelaus.
And I have you. And there is so much to say between us that I do not
know where to begin.

Helen.
	I am so happy the hair stands up on my head
	and my tears run down.
	I have thrown my arms round your body,
	husband, to take my joy.

Menelaus.
	Dearest sight. I have no complaint.
	I have the daughter of Zeus and Leda,
	the girl her brothers on their white horses
	blessed, and blessed, under the torches,
	long ago. The god who took you out of my house
	is driving us on now
	to another fortune, better than this one.
	An evil that turned good has brought you together with me, your husband,
	after so long. Still, let me get some good of my luck.

Chorus.
Good luck to you. I pray the same thing. When there are two of you,
the one cannot suffer and the other not.

Helen.
	Friends, my friends, the old things:
	I do not groan for them any more. It does not hurt.
	I have my husband, I have him, the man I waited for,
	waited for, all those years, to come from Troy.

Menelaus.
	You have me, and I have you. Ten thousand suns
	I barely lived through, and now I understand what the goddess did.
	My tears are tears of joy. There is more
	gladness in them than grief.

Helen.
	What can I say? What mortal ever hoped for this?
	I hold you against my breast, and never thought I would.

Menelaus.
	And I hold you, the woman I thought had gone
	to the city under Ida and the wretched towers of Troy.
	By the gods, how were you taken out of my house?

Helen.
	E, e. That is a bitter beginning you are starting on.
	E, e. That is a bitter story you are asking for.

Menelaus.
Tell it. Everything the gods give has to be heard.

Helen.
I spit on the story I am about to tell. The very story.

Menelaus.
Tell it anyway. It is sweet, hearing about hard times.

Helen.
	Not to the bed of a young barbarian,
	on flying oars,
	on the flying wings of a lust for a lawless marriage.

Menelaus.
	Then what god, or what fate, robbed you from your country?

Helen.
	The son of Zeus, of Zeus, my husband: Hermes.
	He brought me to the Nile.

Menelaus.
Astonishing. And who sent him? What a dreadful story.

Helen.
I have wept and wept, and my eyes are wet with tears now. The wife
of Zeus destroyed me.

Menelaus.
Hera? What did she want with heaping evil on the two of us?

Helen.
	Oh, my dreadful story. The baths and the springs
	where the goddesses made their beauty shine,
	and from there the judging came.

Menelaus.
	And the judging. Hera made it the cause of all this for you?

Helen.
	To take me away from Paris.

Menelaus.
	How? Tell me.

Helen.
	The man Aphrodite had promised me to.

Menelaus.
	Oh, poor woman.

Helen.
	Poor, poor. And so she brought me to Egypt.

Menelaus.
	Then she gave him a phantom in your place, from what I hear from you.

Helen.
	And the sufferings in your house, the sufferings,
	mother. Oh, no.

Menelaus.
What do you mean?

Helen.
My mother is dead. She tied a strangling noose round her neck, for
shame of my disgraceful marriage.

Menelaus.
Oh. And our daughter Hermione. Is she alive?

Helen.
	Unmarried, childless, husband, she mourns
	my marriage that was no marriage.

Menelaus.
	Paris, who sacked my whole house from top to bottom,
	this destroyed you too, and tens of thousands
	of Greeks in their bronze armour.

Helen.
	And me the god threw out of my country,
	cursed, doomed, out of my city and away from you,
	when I left my house and my bed, and did not leave them,
	for a shameful marriage.

Chorus.
If the rest of your luck turns out happy, it might make up for what
went before.

Messenger.
Menelaus, give me a share of this happiness too. I can see it for
myself, but I do not understand it clearly.

Menelaus.
Come, old man. You join the conversation too.

Messenger.
This woman here is not the one all that trouble at Troy was fought
over?

Menelaus.
No, she is not. We were tricked by the gods. What we had in our arms
was the grim image of a cloud.

Messenger.
What are you saying? We went through all that for a cloud? For
nothing?

Menelaus.
Hera's work. And the quarrel of the three goddesses.

Messenger.
And this woman here really is your wife?

Menelaus.
She is. Take my word for it.

Messenger.
Daughter, what a complicated thing the god is, how hard to make out.
Somehow, and for the good, he turns everything over, carrying it this
way and that. One man labours. Another never laboured at all, and
comes to a bad end just the same, with nothing to hold on to in a
fortune that never stops changing. You and your husband have had your
share of trouble, you in what people said of you, he in the heat of
his fighting. When he pushed, and pushed hard, he got nothing. Now he
has the best good fortune there is, and it came to him of its own
accord.

So you never disgraced your old father after all, or the twin sons of
Zeus, and you never did the things they say you did. I am seeing your
wedding all over again now. I remember the torches I carried, running
beside the four-horse team, and you in the chariot, a bride, leaving
your happy home with this man here. A servant who does not care about
his master's affairs, who does not rejoice with him and ache with him
when things go wrong, is worthless. I was born a slave, but let me be
counted among the noble slaves all the same. I do not have the name of
a free man. I have the mind of one. That is better than the double
evil of one man carrying a rotten heart and, slave that he is, taking
orders from everyone around him too.

Menelaus.
Come, old man. You have carried out plenty of hard labour beside my
shield, sweating it out for me. Now share my good fortune too. Go and
tell the friends I left behind how you found things here, and where
our luck stands. Tell them to wait on the shore and keep watch for the
struggles that I expect are waiting for me; and if we can find some
way to steal this woman out of the country, tell them to stand guard,
so that we can throw our luck in together and get away from the
barbarians, if we can.

Messenger.
It will be done, my lord. But I tell you, I have seen now how
worthless the prophets' business is, and how full of lies. There was
nothing sound, after all, in the flame of the burnt offering, or in
the cries of birds. To think that birds can help human beings is
simple-minded. Calchas never said a word, never gave the army a sign,
when he saw his friends dying for a cloud. Nor did Helenus. The city
was torn down for nothing. You might say: because the god did not
want them to. Then why do we consult prophets at all? We should
sacrifice to the gods and ask them for good things, and leave
prophecy alone. It was invented as a bait, a way of making a living
and nothing more, and no one ever got rich from burnt offerings by
being lazy. Good judgement and good sense are the best prophets there
are.

(The Messenger goes off towards the shore.)

Chorus.
My opinion of prophets comes out exactly where this old man's does.
Whoever has the gods for friends has the best prophecy there is, at
home.

Helen.
Well. So far, everything is going well. But how you came out of Troy
alive, poor man: there is nothing to gain from knowing it, but people
who love you long to hear what the people they love have suffered.

Menelaus.
That is a great deal to ask in one question and one breath. Why should
I tell you about the wrecks in the Aegean, and Nauplius's false
beacons on Euboea, and Crete, and the cities of Libya I put in at, and
the lookout of Perseus? I could never fill you up with stories, and
telling you the evils I would suffer them all over again, as I did
when I was living through them. I would grieve twice over.

Helen.
That is a better answer than my question. Leave out all the rest and
tell me one thing: how long were you worn down on the back of the
sea, wandering the salt water?

Menelaus.
On top of the ten years at Troy, I went through seven circling years
on board ship.

Helen.
Pheu, pheu. That is a long time you name, poor man. And after coming
out of that alive, you have come here to be slaughtered.

Menelaus.
What do you mean? What are you going to say? You have finished me,
woman.

Helen.
Run. Get out of this country as fast as you can. The man whose house
this is will kill you.

Menelaus.
What have I done to deserve that?

Helen.
You have turned up, out of nowhere, in the way of my marriage.

Menelaus.
What? Someone wants to marry my wife?

Helen.
And to do me the outrage I have been putting up with.

Menelaus.
Some private strongman, or the man who rules the country?

Helen.
The man who is king of this land. The son of Proteus.

Menelaus.
That is the riddle I heard from the servant.

Helen.
Which of the barbarian's gates were you standing at?

Menelaus.
This one. I was being driven off it like a beggar.

Helen.
You were not begging for food? Oh, misery.

Menelaus.
That is what it was. It just did not have that name.

Helen.
Then you know everything about my marriage, it seems.

Menelaus.
I know. What I do not know is whether you escaped his bed.

Helen.
You can be sure your bed has been kept for you. Untouched.

Menelaus.
What proves it? If you are telling the truth, it is sweet to hear.

Helen.
Do you see my miserable seat here at this tomb?

Menelaus.
I see a wretched bed of straw. What has it got to do with you?

Helen.
This is where I have been begging, as a suppliant, to escape his bed.

Menelaus.
For lack of an altar? Or is that the barbarians' custom?

Helen.
This has protected me as well as the temples of the gods.

Menelaus.
So I cannot even sail you home?

Helen.
A sword is waiting for you, sooner than my bed.

Menelaus.
Then I would be the most wretched of mortals.

Helen.
Do not be ashamed now. Run. Get out of this country.

Menelaus.
And leave you? I sacked Troy for your sake.

Helen.
Yes. Better that than let my marriage kill you.

Menelaus.
That is a coward's talk, and unworthy of Troy.

Helen.
You could not kill the king, which is what you have in mind, I
suppose.

Menelaus.
What, has he got a body that iron cannot wound?

Helen.
You will find out. But daring the impossible is not what a clever man
does.

Menelaus.
So I am to hold out my hands and let them be tied, in silence?

Helen.
You are at a dead end. We need some contrivance.

Menelaus.
Yes. Better to die doing something than doing nothing.

Helen.
There is one hope. Only one way we could be saved.

Menelaus.
Do we buy it, or dare it, or talk our way to it?

Helen.
If the king never found out that you had come.

Menelaus.
And who is going to tell him? He will not know who I am.

Helen.
He has an ally inside the house, as good as a god.

Menelaus.
Some voice, settled in the inner rooms?

Helen.
No. His sister. Theonoe, they call her.

Menelaus.
The name says she is an oracle. Tell me what she does.

Helen.
She knows everything. And she will tell her brother you are here.

Menelaus.
Then we die. There is no way I can hide from her.

Helen.
Perhaps we could persuade her, if we begged her.

Menelaus.
To do what? What hope are you leading me towards?

Helen.
Not to tell her brother that you are in the country.

Menelaus.
And if we persuade her, could we get our feet out of this land?

Helen.
Easily, with her on our side. Behind her back, no.

Menelaus.
That is your job. Woman to woman is the fitting way.

Helen.
My hands will be on her knees. Count on it.

Menelaus.
Right. And if she refuses to hear us?

Helen.
You die. And I am married by force. Poor me.

Menelaus.
Then you would be a traitor. The force is just your excuse.

Helen.
But I have sworn a holy oath, on your head.

Menelaus.
What? To die? And never to trade my bed for his?

Helen.
By the same sword, yes. And I will lie beside you.

Menelaus.
Then on those terms, touch my right hand.

Helen.
I touch it. If you die, I leave the light.
