The Children of Heracles
(Part 1 of 2)

(Marathon, in the country of Athens, in front of the temple of Zeus.
Iolaus, an old man, sits at the altar with the young sons of Heracles
gathered around him.)

Iolaus.
I made up my mind about this a long time ago. One kind of man is born
fair to the people around him. Another kind has let his heart run loose
after profit, and he is no use to his city and hard to deal with, and
the only person he is any good to is himself. I know this. I did not
learn it from talk. Out of respect, and because I honour the ties of
blood, I gave up living quietly in Argos, which I could have done, and
shared Heracles' labours instead, more of them than any other single
man, while he was still among us. And now that he lives in the sky, I
have his children here under my wings, and I keep them safe, when I need
saving myself.

When their father left the earth, the first thing Eurystheus wanted was
to kill us. But we got away. Our city is gone, but our lives were saved.
And since then we have been on the run, wandering, driven over the
border of one city after another. Because on top of everything else
Eurystheus has done to us, he thought fit to add this outrage: wherever
he heard we had settled, he sent heralds there, demanding us back and
having us shut out of the country, holding out Argos to them, no small
city to have as a friend or as an enemy, and his own good fortune along
with it. And they look at me and see how little strength I have, and
they see how small these children are, with no father, and they bow to
the stronger side and shut us out of their land. And I share the
children's exile with them, I share their bad luck with them, because I
cannot bring myself to abandon them. I do not want anyone to be able to
say, "Look. Now that the boys have no father, Iolaus, their own kinsman,
did not stand up for them."

So, with no place left to us anywhere in Greece, we have come to
Marathon and the country that goes with it, and we sit at the altars of
the gods as suppliants, and ask them to help. Because they say two sons
of Theseus live on the plains of this country, land that fell to them by
lot out of the family of Pandion, and they are close kin to these
children. That is why we have come this far, to the border of famous
Athens. Two old people are the generals of this retreat. I fret over
these boys here, and Alcmene, inside the temple, holds her son's
daughters in her arms and keeps them safe, because we are ashamed to
bring young unmarried girls near a crowd and stand them at an altar. And
Hyllus and his brothers who are old enough are out looking for some
walled place where we could make a home, if we are pushed out of this
country by force.

Children, children, here, take hold of my robe. I can see a herald of
Eurystheus coming towards us. He is the man who hunts us, the reason we
are wanderers, shut out of every country on earth. You hateful thing. I
hope you die, you and the man who sent you. Out of that same mouth you
brought plenty of bad news to these children's noble father as well.

(The Herald comes in.)

Herald.
I suppose you think you have found yourself a fine seat there, and
arrived at a city that will fight on your side. You are a fool. There is
not a man alive who will pick your useless strength over Eurystheus.
Move. What is the point of all this effort? You have to get up and go to
Argos, where the sentence waiting for you is death by stoning.

Iolaus.
No, I will not. The god's altar will protect me, and so will the free
ground we are standing on.

Herald.
Do you want to give this hand of mine some work to do?

Iolaus.
You will not take me by force. And you will not lay hands on these
children and take them either.

Herald.
You will find out. You were not much of a prophet about that, it seems.

(The Herald takes hold of the children and throws the old man to the
ground.)

Iolaus.
That will never happen while I am alive.

Herald.
Out of the way. Whether you like it or not, I am taking these boys,
because I count them as the property of Eurystheus, which is what they
are.

Iolaus.
You men who have lived in Athens so long, help us! We are suppliants of
Zeus of the marketplace, and we are being dragged away by force, our
wreaths defiled, a disgrace to your city and an insult to the gods.

(The Chorus, old men of Marathon, come hurrying in.)

Chorus.
Ah. Ah. What is this shouting that has gone up beside the altar? What
kind of trouble is it going to show us?

Chorus.
	Look at the old man, feeble, sprawled
	on the ground. Poor man.
	Who did this? Who threw you down so pitifully in the dirt?

Iolaus.
This man, strangers. He shows your gods no respect, and he is dragging
me by force from the steps of Zeus's altar.

Chorus.
	And where have you come from, old man, to the four towns
	that live here as one people? Have you put in
	from across the water with the oar that beats the sea,
	leaving the shore of Euboea behind?

Iolaus.
I do not lead an islander's life, strangers. We have come to your
country from Mycenae.

Chorus.
	And what name, old man,
	did the people of Mycenae call you by?

Iolaus.
You know, I think, of the man who stood beside Heracles in battle,
Iolaus. This body of mine is not unheard of.

Chorus.
	I know. I heard of you before now. But whose are these
	little boys you are carrying, holding them by the hand,
	still so young? Tell me.

Iolaus.
These are the sons of Heracles, strangers. They have come as suppliants
to you and to your city.

Chorus.
	What is it they want? Tell me. Is it a hearing
	with the city they are anxious to get?

Iolaus.
Not to be handed over. Not to be torn from your gods by force and taken
to Argos.

Herald.
That will not satisfy your masters, though. They have rights over you,
and they have found you here.

Chorus.
	It is right to respect the gods' suppliants, stranger,
	and not to make them leave the seats of the gods
	by force of your hand.
	Lady Justice will not put up with that.

Herald.
Then send these people of Eurystheus's out of your country, and I will
not use this hand on them at all.

Chorus.
	It is a godless thing for a city to let go
	of a suppliant's appeal, the plea of strangers.

Herald.
It is a good thing, too, to keep your foot out of trouble, by taking the
better piece of advice.

Chorus.
Then you should have told the king of this land what you meant to do
before you had the nerve to do it, instead of dragging strangers away
from the gods by force, in a free country you ought to respect.

Herald.
And who is the ruler of this land and this city?

Chorus.
Demophon, son of Theseus, a good man's son.

Herald.
Then it is with him that this argument really has to be fought out.
Everything else has been said for nothing.

Chorus.
And here he is himself, coming in a hurry, and Acamas his brother with
him, to hear what is being said.

(Demophon and Acamas come in with attendants.)

Demophon.
Since you got here before us, old as you are, running to help at this
altar of Zeus ahead of younger men, tell me: what has happened to bring
this crowd together?

Chorus.
The sons of Heracles are sitting here as suppliants, my lord. They have
put wreaths on the altar, as you can see. And with them is Iolaus, the
man who stood faithfully at their father's side in battle.

Demophon.
And why did that call for screaming?

Chorus.
This man here. He was trying to drag them away from the altar by force,
and he raised the shouting, and he knocked the old man's legs out from
under him, so that I wept for pity.

Demophon.
Well, he wears Greek clothes, and the cut of his robe is Greek, but this
is the work of a barbarian's hand. Now it is your turn to speak, and no
delay about it. What country's borders did you leave to get here?

Herald.
I am an Argive. That is what you want to know. And I am willing to tell
you what I have come for, and who sent me. Eurystheus, king of Mycenae,
sends me here to bring these children back. And I have come, stranger,
with plenty of justice on my side, for what I do and for what I say. I
am an Argive myself, and it is Argives I am taking away, runaways from
my own city, condemned to death by the laws there. We live in our own
city and we have the right to pass sentences on our own people that hold
good. They have come to the hearths of many other cities, and there we
took our stand on these same arguments, and nobody had the nerve to
bring trouble of his own making down on his own head.

So either they have spotted some foolishness in you and come here for
it, or, out of sheer desperation, they are throwing the dice, whether it
comes off or not. Because they surely do not expect that you, if you are
in your right mind, alone in all the breadth of Greece they have
travelled through, are going to pity their stupid troubles. Come on,
then, weigh one thing against the other. Let them into your country, or
let us take them out of it: what do you gain? Here is what you get from
our side: the whole great hand of Argos, and the entire strength of
Eurystheus, added to this city. But if you look at their speeches and
their weeping and go soft, then this business is settled by a wrestling
match of spears. Do not imagine we are going to give up this contest
without cold steel.

So what will you say? What land have you been robbed of, what plunder
taken off you, that you should go to war with the Argives? What allies
are you defending? On whose behalf will you be burying your dead? You
will get a bad name from your own citizens, if for the sake of an old
man, a tomb, a nothing, to put it plainly, and these children, you wade
into the bilge of a sinking ship. The best you can say is that you will
find hope in them, only hope. And even that falls a long way short of
what is needed now. These boys, once they are grown up, would make a
poor showing in a fight against Argives under arms, if that is what
raises your spirits. And there is a long stretch of time in between, in
which you could be wiped out. No. Listen to me. Give me nothing. Just
let me take what is mine, and you have Mycenae for a friend. And do not
do the thing you Athenians love to do, taking the weaker side when you
had the chance to choose the stronger as your friends.

Chorus.
Who could judge a case, or understand an argument, before he has heard
clearly what both sides have to say?

Iolaus.
My lord, in your country I have this to begin with: I am allowed to
speak and to be heard in turn, and no one will push me away before I
have spoken, the way they did everywhere else. Between us and this man
there is nothing in common. Since we have no share in Argos any more,
since that was decided by vote and we are exiles from our own country,
how can he justly take us away as Mycenaeans, when we are what we are,
people they drove out of the land? We are foreigners. Or is your
position that whoever is exiled from Argos is exiled from the whole of
Greece? Not from Athens, at any rate. The Athenians will not drive the
children of Heracles out of their country for fear of Argos. This is not
Trachis, and it is not that town in Achaea, the places you drove these
children from while they sat as suppliants at the altar. Not by any
justice, but by puffing up Argos, exactly the way you are doing now. If
that happens here, if they judge your arguments the winners, then I no
longer know this Athens as a free city. But I know the spirit of these
people, and their nature. They will be willing to die. Among decent men,
shame is reckoned worse than death.

That is enough about the city. Too much praise is a thing people resent,
and I know it myself, I have often felt the weight of being praised too
much. But I want to explain to you why you are bound to save these
children, since you are the one who stands at the head of this land.
Pittheus was the son of Pelops. From Pittheus came Aethra, and from her
your father Theseus was born. Now let me go back over these children's
family for you. Heracles was the son of Zeus and Alcmene, and Alcmene
was the daughter of Pelops' daughter. So your father and their father
were the sons of first cousins. That is how you stand to them by blood,
Demophon. But now I will tell you what you owe these children beyond
what kinship demands. I say that I once sailed with their father,
carrying his shield, when he went with Theseus after the Amazon queen's
belt, the belt that cost so many lives. And he brought your father up
out of the deep fortress of Hades. All Greece is witness to that.

In return for all this, they ask you for one favour: not to be handed
over, not to be torn away from your gods by force and thrown out of the
country. It would be shameful for you personally, and bad for the city,
for suppliants, wanderers, your own kin, oh, look at them, look at them,
to be dragged off by force. I beg you, I lay my suppliant wreath on you
with my hands, I touch your beard: do not despise the children of
Heracles now that you have them in your hands. Be a kinsman to them. Be
their friend, their father, their brother, their master. Any one of
those is better than falling into the hands of the Argives.

Chorus.
Hearing what has happened to them, my lord, I pity them. Never have I
seen noble birth beaten by fortune as plainly as now. These are the
children of a great father, and they are suffering what they do not
deserve.

Demophon.
Three roads of reasoning force me, Iolaus, not to turn away these guests
of yours. The greatest is Zeus. You are sitting at his altar with this
flock of nestlings around you. Then there is the blood tie, and the old
debt: these children have a claim on us, for their father's sake, to be
treated well. And then there is the shame of it, and that is what I have
to weigh most of all. If I let this altar be plundered by force, by a
foreigner, then people will say the country I live in is not free, and
that I handed suppliants over out of fear of Argos. That is near enough
to hanging myself. I wish you had come here under a luckier star, but as
it is, do not be afraid that anyone is going to tear you and these
children away from this altar by force.

You. Go back to Argos and tell Eurystheus this, and tell him that if he
has any charge against these strangers he will get justice. But you will
never take them.

Herald.
Not even if my case is just, and I win the argument?

Demophon.
And how can it be just to take a suppliant by force?

Herald.
Then the disgrace is mine, and no harm to you.

Demophon.
Harm to me, if I let you drag them away.

Herald.
Then you banish them, and we will take them from over the border.

Demophon.
You are a fool by nature, thinking you know better than the god.

Herald.
So it seems this is the place for criminals to run to.

Demophon.
The seat of the gods is a shelter open to everyone.

Herald.
The Mycenaeans may not see it that way.

Demophon.
Am I not the master of what happens here?

Herald.
As long as you do them no harm. If you have the sense to keep yourself
in hand, you will not.

Demophon.
Be harmed, then. I am not going to pollute the gods.

Herald.
I do not want you at war with Argos.

Demophon.
Neither do I. But I will not let go of these children.

Herald.
All the same, I will take what is mine and go.

Demophon.
Then you will not find it easy to get back to Argos.

Herald.
I will try it and find out, right now.

Demophon.
Touch them and you will be sorry, and not in a while, either. Now.

Chorus.
By the gods, do not dare to strike a herald.

Demophon.
I will, unless the herald learns to keep himself in hand.

Chorus.
Go. And you, my lord, do not touch him.

Herald.
I am going. One arm is a weak thing to fight with. But I will come back,
and I will bring the whole bronze spearhead of Argive war with me. Ten
thousand men with shields are waiting for me, and King Eurystheus
himself in command. He is waiting on the borders of Megara, Alcathous's
country, watching to see how things turn out here. When he hears of your
outrage he will come down like a flash of light on you and on your
citizens, on this land and on everything that grows in it. It would be a
waste of all the young men we have in Argos if we did not punish you.

(The Herald turns and goes off the way he came.)

Demophon.
Go to hell. I am not afraid of your Argos. You were never going to shame
me and take these children out of here by force. This is not a city I
hold as a subject of Argos. I hold it free.

Chorus.
	Now is the time to think ahead, before the Argive army
	comes near our borders.
	The war god of Mycenae is very sharp,
	and after this, sharper still than before.
	This is the way of every herald:
	to build what happened up to twice its size.
	What do you think he will tell his king,
	how terribly he suffered, how close he came
	to having the life scraped out of him?

Iolaus.
There is no finer prize for children than this: to be born of a good and
noble father, and to marry into good families. The man who gives in to
desire and mixes his blood with the low-born, I have no praise for him.
He leaves his children a disgrace for the sake of his own pleasure. Good
birth stands up to misfortune better than bad birth does. Look at us. We
fell to the very bottom of trouble, and we found friends and kinsmen
here, the only people in the whole inhabited Greek world who stood up
for these children.

Give them your right hands, children. Give them. And you, give yours to
the boys, and come close to them. Children, we have put our friends to
the test. If ever the day comes when you go home to your own country and
live in your father's house and hold his honours, remember: these men
are your saviours and your friends, always, and never lift a hostile
spear against this land. Remember that for me. Count this city the
dearest of all. They deserve your reverence. They took on themselves the
enmity of a country as great as Argos and the whole Pelasgian people,
for our sake, when they could see we were beggars and wanderers. And
still they did not hand us over, and they did not drive us out of the
land.

And I, alive and dead, when I die, will praise you loud and high, my
friend, when I stand beside Theseus, and I will make him glad by telling
him how well you took in the children of Heracles and protected them,
how you are a nobleman all through Greece and keep your father's good
name, and how, born of a great man, you have turned out no worse than
your father. Very few can say that. Out of many men you might find one,
perhaps, who is not worse than his father.

Chorus.
This land has always wanted to help the helpless, where justice is on
their side. That is why it has borne countless hardships for its
friends, and now I see another struggle of that kind close at hand.

Demophon.
You have spoken well, old man, and I promise you these children will
turn out as you say. The favour will be remembered. I am going to call
an assembly of the citizens and set them in order, so that I meet the
Mycenaean army with a strong force. First I will send scouts out to meet
it, so it does not fall on me unawares; every man in Argos is quick to
answer a call to arms. And I will gather the prophets and make
sacrifice. You go into the house with the children, and leave the altar
of Zeus. There are people who will look after you, even if I am away. Go
into the house, old man.

Iolaus.
I will not leave the altar. Let us sit here as suppliants and wait, and
pray the city does well. When you have come through this struggle with
honour, we will go to the house. The gods on our side are no weaker than
the Argives', my lord. Hera, the wife of Zeus, stands at the head of
theirs. Athena stands at the head of ours. And I say that this too
counts towards success, to have the better gods. Pallas Athena will not
stand being beaten.

(Demophon and Acamas go off towards the city.)

Chorus.
	Boast as loudly as you like, there are others
	who care no more for you for it,
	stranger come from Argos.
	Your big talk will not
	frighten my heart.
	May it never come to that for great Athens,
	the city of beautiful dances.
	You are a fool, and so is the son of Sthenelus,
	the king in Argos,

Chorus.
	who comes into another city,
	one no smaller than Argos,
	and drags off suppliants of the gods, wanderers
	who are begging my land for help,
	a stranger using force,
	not giving way to our kings,
	not making one just argument.
	How could that look right
	to anyone with sense?

Chorus.
	Peace is what I like myself.
	But I tell you, wrong-headed king,
	if you come to my city,
	you will not get what you expect
	as easily as that. You are not the only one
	with a spear and a bronze-plated shield.
	No. You who love war,
	do not stir up with your spear
	this city that the Graces have blessed.
	Hold yourself back.

(Demophon comes back alone, with a troubled face.)

Iolaus.
My boy, why have you come back with that worried look in your eyes? Is
there news of the enemy? Are they on their way? Are they here? What have
you heard? You are not going to tell me the herald lied. Their general
has had good luck from the gods so far, and he will come against Athens,
I know it, and with no small opinion of himself, either. But Zeus
punishes the thoughts of people who think too much of themselves.

Demophon.
The Argive army is here, and King Eurystheus with it. I saw him with my
own eyes. A man who claims to know how to be a general should not look
at the enemy through messengers. He has not sent his army down onto the
plains of this country yet. He is sitting on a rocky ridge, looking
things over, and, I am only telling you what I think, deciding which way
to bring his army in without a fight, and where in this country he can
pitch camp safely. As for my side, everything is already well in hand.
The city is under arms, and the victims stand ready for the gods who are
owed them, to turn the enemy back and save the city, and the prophets
are filling the town with sacrifice.

I gathered every singer of oracles into one place and questioned them,
and I went through the old prophecies, the public ones and the secret
ones, the ones that keep this land safe. And the oracles differ from
each other on many points, but on one thing they all say the same, and
it stands out: they tell me to sacrifice a virgin to Persephone,
Demeter's daughter, a girl whose father is of noble blood. Now, you can
see how much goodwill I have towards you. But I will not kill my own
child, and I will not force any citizen of mine to do it against his
will. And who would be willing? Who is so far gone in his wits that he
would hand over the children he loves most out of his own hands? Already
you could see bitter meetings in the streets, some saying it was right
to help strangers who are suppliants, others accusing me of folly. And
if I actually do this thing, it is civil war, there and then.

So look at this yourself, and help me find a way for you to be saved,
and this country too, without my being slandered before my citizens. I
do not hold a tyrant's power, the way barbarians do. If I do what is
just, I will be treated justly.

Chorus.
Can it be that the god will not let this city help strangers, when it is
eager to and wants to?

Iolaus.
Children, we are like sailors who escaped the wild rage of a storm and
came close enough to touch the land with their hands, and then were
driven off the shore by the winds and back out to sea. That is how we
are being pushed away from this country, when we were already at the
shore and thought we were saved. Oh. Why did you make me so glad, then,
hope, you wretched thing, when you never meant to finish the favour you
started? I can forgive this man. He does not want to kill his own
citizens' children. And I can find good things to say about how we were
treated here. If this is what the gods want to happen to me, the thanks
I owe you is not wiped out.

Children, I do not know what to do with you. Where can we turn? What
god's altar have we not wreathed? What walled town have we not come to?
We are finished, children. We are going to be handed over. For myself, I
do not care whether I have to die, except that in dying I might please
my enemies. But I weep for you, children, and I pity you, and your
father's old mother Alcmene. Poor woman, for that long life of yours.
And poor me, all the labour I have done for nothing. So it was our fate,
after all, to fall into an enemy's hands and lose our lives in shame and
misery.

But wait. Do you know how you can help me? All hope of saving these
children has not left me yet. Hand me over to the Argives instead of
them, my lord. Run no risk yourself, and let my children be saved. I
have no business loving my own life. Let it go. Eurystheus would like
nothing better than to get his hands on me, Heracles' comrade in arms,
and outrage me. The man is a brute. A wise man should pray to have a
wise man for his enemy, not a proud fool. From a wise enemy you would
get plenty of respect, and justice.

Chorus.
Old man, do not blame this city. It might be a lie, but it would still
do us harm, the reproach that we betrayed strangers.

Demophon.
That was nobly said, but it cannot be done. It is not for you that the
king is marching his army here. What good is a dead old man to
Eurystheus? It is these children he wants to kill. To an enemy, the
young of a noble house are a danger as they grow up: young men, and
remembering the outrages done to their father. He has to look ahead to
all of that. But if you know of any other plan that fits the moment
better, have it ready, because I am at a loss since I heard those
oracles, and full of fear.

(Macaria, the eldest daughter of Heracles, comes out of the temple.)

Macaria.
Strangers, do not put my coming out here down to boldness. That is the
first thing I will ask of you. For a woman, silence and keeping herself
in hand are the finest things, and staying quietly inside the house. But
I heard your groaning, Iolaus, and I came out. Not because I have been
appointed to take the lead in the family. But I am fit for it in a way,
and my brothers matter to me more than anything, and about them and
about myself I want to know: has some new pain been added to the old
troubles, and is it eating at your heart?

Iolaus.
My girl, it is nothing new for me to praise you, and justly, above all
the children of Heracles. Our course seemed to be running well, and now
it has swung back again to where there is no way out. This man says the
singers of oracles are telling him to slaughter, not a bull or a calf,
but a virgin of noble blood, to Demeter's daughter, if we are to live,
and this city is to stand. That is what we have no answer to. He says he
will not slaughter his own children, or anyone else's. And to me he
says, not in so many words, but he says it all the same, that unless we
find some way out of this we must go and find ourselves another country,
because he wants to save this one.

Macaria.
Is that the condition our safety hangs on? That one thing?

Iolaus.
That is it. In everything else we have done well.

Macaria.
Then stop being afraid of the enemy's Argive spear. I myself, before
anyone orders me, old man, am ready to die and to stand at the altar for
the knife. What are we going to say, if this city thinks it right to run
a great risk for our sake, and we, who could save them, put our troubles
onto other people's shoulders and run away from dying? No. It would be a
joke, wouldn't it, to sit as suppliants of the gods and groan, and then,
born of the father we were born of, to be seen to be cowards. Where
among decent people does that look right? Better, I think, if this city
were taken, and may that never happen, and I fell into the enemy's
hands, and then, my noble father's daughter, suffered what I would
suffer and looked on Hades all the same. Or shall I be thrown out of
this country and wander? And will I not be ashamed when somebody says,
"Why have you come here with your suppliant branches, when you love your
own lives so much? Get out of the country. We do not help cowards."

And even if these boys died and I alone were saved, I have no hope of
doing well. Many people before now have betrayed their friends in that
hope. Who would want to take a girl with no family as his wife, or to
have children by me? Is it not better to die than to meet with that,
which I do not deserve? That might suit some other girl better, one who
is not as marked out as I am.

Lead me to where this body has to die. Put the wreaths on me and begin
the rite, if that is what you decide. And beat your enemies. This life
is here for you, freely given and not under compulsion. I declare it: I
will die for these brothers of mine and for myself. Because by not
loving my life too much I have found the finest thing there is to find,
a way to leave it with honour.

Chorus.
Ah, ah. What am I to say, hearing this great speech from a girl who is
willing to die before her brothers do? Who could speak more nobly than
this? What human being could do more?

Iolaus.
My child, your head comes from nowhere else. You are the seed of that
godlike spirit. You are Heracles' own. I am not ashamed of your words.
It is your fate that hurts me. But let me say how it could be done more
fairly. All this girl's sisters should be called out here, and then the
one who draws the lot should die for the family. It is not just for you
to die without a lot being drawn.

Macaria.
I will not die by the luck of a lot. There is no grace in that. Do not
say it, old man. If you will accept me, and if you want to make use of
my willingness, then I give my life for these children freely. Not
because I was forced.

Iolaus.
Ah. This speech of yours is nobler than the last, and the last was the
best I ever heard. You outdo daring with daring, and one good speech
with another. I am not ordering you to die, child, and I am not
forbidding it. But your brothers are helped by your dying.

Macaria.
That is a clever way to order it. Do not be afraid that you will share
in the pollution of my death. Let me die free. Come with me, old man. I
want to die in your arms. Be there, and cover my body with your robe.
Because I am going to the horror of the knife, if I really am the
daughter of the father I claim.

Iolaus.
I could not stand by and watch you die.

Macaria.
Then at least ask this of the king for me: that I breathe out my life in
women's hands, not men's.

Demophon.
It will be so, poor girl. It would be a disgrace to me too if you were
not honoured properly, for many reasons: for your courage, and because
it is just. You are the bravest woman my eyes have ever seen. Now, if
you have anything to say, go and speak to these children and to the old
man. Your last words.

Macaria.
Goodbye, old man, goodbye. And teach these boys for me to be like you,
clever in everything. No more than that. That will be enough for them.
And try to keep them from dying, as eagerly as you are doing now. We are
your children. Your hands brought us up. You can see how I am giving up
my own wedding day and dying in their place. And you, my brothers, all
of you here with me now, be happy. And may everything come to you that
my heart is going to be cut open for. Honour this old man, and the old
woman inside the house, Alcmene, my father's mother, and these
strangers. And if the gods ever find you a release from your troubles
and a way home, remember to give the woman who saved you the burial she
is owed. The finest you can give would be only fair. I did not fail you.
I stood by you, and I died for the family. That is my treasure to keep,
in place of children, in place of my girlhood, if there is anything at
all under the earth. I hope there is nothing. Because if we who are
about to die are going to have cares there too, I do not know where a
person could turn. Death is supposed to be the greatest cure there is
for evil.

Iolaus.
You stand out above all women for courage. Know this: living, you will
be the most honoured of all to us, and dead, far more. Goodbye. I shrink
from saying an ill word against the goddess your body has been
consecrated to, Demeter's daughter. Children, we are finished. My limbs
are coming loose with grief. Take hold of me and set me down on the
seat, right here, and cover me with these robes, children. I take no
pleasure in what has been done, and if the oracle is not carried out,
there is no living either. The ruin would be greater. But this too is a
disaster.
