Hecuba
(Part 2 of 3)

Chorus.
	Breeze, breeze off the sea,
	you who carry the quick ships
	over the swell of the water,
	where will you take me, in my misery?
	Whose house will I come to, bought,
	to be a slave in it? To a harbour
	in the Dorian land?
	Or to Phthia, where they say
	the Apidanus, father of the loveliest waters,
	makes the plains fat and rich?

Chorus.
	Or to the islands, sent on my miserable way
	by an oar that sweeps the salt,
	to a pitiful life in someone's house,
	where the first palm tree ever born, and the laurel,
	lifted their holy branches
	for Leto, whom they loved,
	the glory of her labour, when she bore the children of Zeus?
	And there, with the girls of Delos,
	will I sing of the golden headband
	and the bow of Artemis, their goddess?

Chorus.
	Or in the city of Pallas,
	on the saffron robe of Athena
	of the lovely chariot,
	will I yoke her horses, working them
	into the web in threads of many colours,
	bright as flowers? Or the race of the Titans,
	that Zeus, the son of Cronos,
	puts to sleep with his fire
	that burns from both ends?

Chorus.
	Oh, my children,
	oh, my fathers, and my country,
	crumbling in smoke,
	smouldering, taken by the spear
	of the Argives. And I, on foreign
	soil, am called a slave now,
	leaving Asia behind
	for a house in Europe,
	which is a bedroom in Hades.

(Talthybius, the herald of the Greek army, comes in. He is an old man.)

Talthybius.
Where can I find Hecuba, who was queen of Troy once, girls of Troy?

Chorus.
Here, Talthybius, close by you. She is lying on her back on the ground,
wrapped up in her clothes.

Talthybius.
Zeus, what am I to say? That you watch over human beings? Or that we
hold this belief for nothing, and it is false, and there is no race of
gods at all, and it is chance that oversees everything that happens to
mortals? Was this not the queen of the Phrygians, with all their gold?
Was this not the wife of Priam, the greatly blessed? And now the whole
city has been torn up by the spear, and she lies on the ground, a slave,
an old woman with no children, fouling her poor head in the dust. Pheu,
pheu. I am an old man, but let me die before I fall into some shameful
piece of luck like this. Get up, poor woman. Lift your side off the
ground, and that head, all white.

Hecuba.
Ah. Who is this, who will not let my body lie? Whoever you are, why are
you moving me, when I am grieving?

Talthybius.
I am Talthybius, the servant of the Greeks. Agamemnon sent me to fetch
you, lady.

Hecuba.
Dearest man. Have the Achaeans decided to kill me too, at the grave? Is
that what you have come for? What a welcome thing to say. Let us hurry.
Let us be quick. Lead the way, old man.

Talthybius.
I have come to fetch you so that you can bury your daughter, lady. She
is dead. The two sons of Atreus sent me, and the Achaean people.

Hecuba.
Oh. What are you going to say? So you did not come to fetch us to die,
but to bring bad news? You are gone, child, snatched away from your
mother. And I have no children now, as far as you are concerned. Oh,
wretched woman that I am. How did you finish her? Did you show her any
respect? Or did you come at the terrible thing as if she were an enemy,
old man, and kill her that way? Tell me, even though what you say will
not be kind.

Talthybius.
Lady, you want me to earn a double set of tears, out of pity for your
daughter. I will wet these eyes now, telling the bad news, as I did at
the tomb when she was dying.

The whole crowd of the Achaean army was there, at full strength, in
front of the tomb, for your daughter's slaughter. The son of Achilles
took Polyxena by the hand and stood her on the top of the mound, and I
was close by. Picked young men, the pick of the Achaeans, came behind,
to hold your young heifer down with their hands if she kicked. Then
Achilles' son took a full cup, all gold, in his hands and lifted it, a
drink offering to his dead father, and he signalled to me to proclaim
silence to the whole Achaean army. I took my place in the middle of them
and said this: "Silence, Achaeans. Let the whole people be silent.
Silence. Quiet." And I settled the crowd until there was not a breath of
wind in it. Then he spoke. "Son of Peleus, my father, accept from me
these offerings that soothe the dead and draw them up. Come, and drink
the black, unmixed blood of a girl, which the army and I are giving you.
Be kind to us. Let us loose the sterns and the cables of our ships, and
grant us all a kind homecoming from Troy, and let us all come home to
our own country." That is what he said, and the whole army prayed with
him.

Then he took his sword, with the gold on the hilt, by the handle, and
drew it from the sheath, and nodded to the picked young men of the
Argive army to take hold of the girl. And she, when she realised, spoke
these words: "Argives, who sacked my city, I die of my own free will.
Let no one touch my body. I will offer my neck with a good heart. By the
gods, let me go free when you kill me, so that I die free. I am a king's
daughter, and I am ashamed to be called a slave among the dead." The
people roared their approval, and King Agamemnon told the young men to
let the girl go. And as soon as they heard that last word from the man
whose power was greatest, they let go. And when she heard what her
masters had said, she took her dress at the top of the shoulder and tore
it down to the middle of her side, beside the navel, and showed her
breasts and her chest, lovely as a statue's, and she sank on one knee to
the ground and spoke the bravest words of all: "Look. Here. If it is my
chest you want to strike, young man, strike. If you want it under the
neck, here is my throat, ready." And he, not wanting to and wanting to,
out of pity for the girl, cut through the channels of her breath with
the iron, and the springs ran. And she, even as she died, took great
care to fall decently, hiding what should be hidden from the eyes of
men.

When she had let go her breath in the deadly slaughter, no two Argives
had the same task. Some of them threw leaves on the dead girl from their
hands. Others were heaping up a pyre, carrying logs of pine, and the man
who carried nothing heard things like this from the man who did: "Are
you standing there, you worthless man, with no dress and no ornament in
your hands for the girl? Are you not going to give something to the girl
with the great heart beyond all others, the best in spirit?" That is
what I have to say about your daughter's death, and I see you as the
most blessed of all women in your children, and the most unlucky.

Chorus.
Some terrible trouble has boiled over onto the sons of Priam and onto my
city. The gods forced it on us.

Hecuba.
Daughter, I do not know which of my evils to look at. There are so many
here. If I take hold of one, this one will not let me, and then another
grief calls me over from there, evil taking over from evil. And now I
cannot wipe what happened to you out of my mind so as not to groan for
it. But you have taken the edge off it, reported to me as noble. Is it
not strange? Bad soil, if it gets its season from the gods, bears a good
crop, and good soil, if it misses what it ought to get, gives bad fruit.
But among human beings the worthless man is never anything but bad, and
the good man is good, and no disaster ruins his nature: he is decent
always. Is it the parents that make the difference, or the upbringing?
Being brought up well does teach a man goodness, though; and if someone
learns that lesson well, he knows what is shameful, because he has
learned it by the measuring rod of the good. My mind has been shooting
those arrows into the air for nothing.

You, go and tell the Argives this: no one is to touch my daughter. Keep
the crowd away. In an army of tens of thousands the mob has no
discipline, and sailors' lawlessness is stronger than fire, and the man
who does no harm is the bad one.

(Talthybius goes.)

And you, old servant, take a jar, dip it in the salt sea and bring it
here, so that I can wash my child in her last bath, a bride who is no
bride, a virgin who is no virgin, and lay her out. As she deserves?
Where would I get the means? I could not. But as I can. What else is
there for me? And I will gather ornaments from the captive women who
live beside me inside these tents, if any of them has stolen something
out of her own house without her new masters knowing.

(The old Maid goes off towards the shore with the jar.)

Shape of a house. House that was happy once. Priam, who had the most of
everything and the finest of it, the most blessed of fathers, and I,
this old woman, the mother of your children: how we have come to
nothing, stripped of the pride we had. And then we puff ourselves up,
one of us in a rich house, another with a name for honour among the
citizens. Those things are nothing. Plans in the mind, and boasting with
the tongue. The happiest man is the one who meets nothing bad, day by
day.

(Hecuba goes into the tent.)

Chorus.
	It had to come to me, disaster,
	it had to come to me, grief,
	the day Paris first cut
	the pine on Mount Ida
	to sail over the swell of the sea
	to Helen's bed, the loveliest woman
	the Sun with his golden light
	shines on.

Chorus.
	Because troubles, and harder than troubles,
	necessities, are wheeling round,
	and out of one man's folly came an evil
	shared by all, and deadly, to the land of Simois,
	and disaster from other men's hands.
	The quarrel was judged, the one that Paris,
	a cowherd then, judged on Ida
	between three daughters of the blessed gods,

Chorus.
	and it ended in the spear, and killing, and the wrecking of my house.
	And a Spartan girl is groaning too,
	weeping her eyes out, at home
	beside the lovely stream of the Eurotas,
	and a mother whose sons are dead
	lays her hand on her grey head
	and tears at her cheeks,
	and her nails are bloody with the tearing.

(The Maid comes back from the shore. Women come with her, carrying a
body under a cloth.)

Maid.
Women, where is Hecuba? Where is the most miserable woman alive, who
beats every man and every woman ever born at suffering? Nobody is going
to take that crown from her.

Chorus.
What now, you poor woman, with your ugly shouting? Your painful
announcements never sleep.

Maid.
I am bringing Hecuba this pain. In evil times it is not easy for a
mortal mouth to speak good words.

Chorus.
Look, she is just coming out of the tent. She is appearing at the right
moment for what you have to say.

(Hecuba comes out of the tent.)

Maid.
Mistress. Wretched beyond everything, and more than I can say. You are
destroyed, you no longer exist, though you are looking at the light. No
children, no husband, no city. Wiped out.

Hecuba.
That is nothing new. You are throwing it at people who know. But why
have you come, bringing me this corpse, Polyxena's, when I was told the
whole Achaean army was busy with her burial, hand to hand?

Maid.
She knows nothing. She is mourning Polyxena to me, and she has not
touched the new trouble.

Hecuba.
Oh, wretched woman. You are not bringing me the frenzied head of
Cassandra, the god-singer, here?

Maid.
The one you cry out for is alive. It is the dead one here you are not
groaning for. Look at the body, uncovered, and see if it strikes you as
a wonder, and past all your hopes.

(She draws back the cloth.)

Hecuba.
Oh. I see my son dead. Polydorus, whom the Thracian was keeping safe for
me in his house. I am destroyed, wretched woman, I no longer exist.
Child, child, aiai, I begin the dirge, a frenzied song I learned just
now from an avenging spirit.

Maid.
So you knew your son's ruin, poor woman?

Hecuba.
Unbelievable, unbelievable, new, new, what I see. Evils are coming one
out of another, evils out of evils. Never a day without a groan, without
a tear, will hold me again.

Chorus.
Terrible, poor woman. Terrible, what we are suffering.

Hecuba.
	Child, child of a miserable mother,
	how did you die? What death
	lays you here?
	At whose hands, of all men?

Maid.
	I do not know. I found him on the shore of the sea.

Hecuba.
	Thrown up by the waves, or fallen there,
	killed by a bloody spear,
	on the smooth sand?

Maid.
	The open swell of the sea carried him out onto the beach.

Hecuba.
	Oh, aiai. I understand the dream my eyes saw
	in my sleep. It did not pass me by,
	the black-winged ghost, the one I saw around you,
	child, no longer alive in the light of Zeus.

Chorus.
Then who killed him? Can you read your dream and say?

Hecuba.
My guest. Mine. The Thracian horseman, in whose house his old father put
him away and hid him.

Chorus.
Oh. What are you going to say? He killed him to keep the gold?

Hecuba.
	Unspeakable. Unnameable. Past all wondering at.
	Not holy, not to be borne. Where is the justice between guest and host?
	Cursed man, worst of men, how you carved up
	his flesh, cutting with an iron sword
	the limbs of this child, and felt no pity.

Chorus.
Poor woman. Whatever god is heavy on you has made you the most suffering
of mortals. But I see the shape of our master, Agamemnon, coming. From
here on, friends, let us be quiet.

(Agamemnon comes in.)

Agamemnon.
Hecuba, why are you delaying? Why have you not come to hide your
daughter in her grave, on the terms Talthybius brought me from you, that
none of the Argives should touch your girl? We let her be, then, and we
did not touch her. But you are taking your time, and I am amazed at it.
I have come to send you on your way. Everything over there has been well
done, if any of this is well. Ah. What man is this I see by the tents, a
dead Trojan? He is no Argive. The clothes wrapped round his body tell me
that.

Hecuba.
Wretched woman. I mean myself, Hecuba, when I say you. What am I to do?
Fall at Agamemnon's knees here, or carry my troubles in silence?

Agamemnon.
Why have you turned your back to my face? Why are you crying, and not
saying what has happened? Who is this?

Hecuba.
But if he thinks of me as a slave and an enemy, and pushes me away from
his knees, I would be adding pain to pain.

Agamemnon.
I was not born a prophet. If I am not told, I cannot trace the road your
thoughts are taking.

Hecuba.
Am I reckoning this man's mind as more hostile than it is, when he is
not hostile at all?

Agamemnon.
If you want me to know nothing about this, we have arrived at the same
place. I have no wish to hear it either.

Hecuba.
Without this man I could not avenge my children. Why am I turning it
over and over? I have to have the nerve, whether I win or not.
Agamemnon, I beg you, by these knees, and by your beard, and by your
fortunate right hand.

Agamemnon.
Looking for what? Not to make your life free? That is easy enough for
you.

Hecuba.
No, not that. Let me punish the wicked, and I am willing to be a slave
for the whole of my life.

Agamemnon.
Well then, what are you calling on us to help with?

Hecuba.
None of the things you are thinking of, my lord. Do you see this corpse,
that I am dripping tears on?

Agamemnon.
I see it. But what comes next I cannot make out.

Hecuba.
I gave birth to him once. I carried him under my belt.

Agamemnon.
Which of your children is this, poor woman?

Hecuba.
Not one of Priam's sons who died under the walls of Troy.

Agamemnon.
You had another son, then, besides them, lady?

Hecuba.
For no good, as it turns out. This one, that you are looking at.

Agamemnon.
Where did he happen to be when the city was being destroyed?

Hecuba.
His father sent him away, for fear he would die.

Agamemnon.
Where did he send him, alone, apart from the children he had then?

Hecuba.
To this country, where he was found dead.

Agamemnon.
To the man who rules this land? Polymestor?

Hecuba.
He was sent here to keep gold. The bitterest gold there ever was.

Agamemnon.
And who killed him? What death did he meet?

Hecuba.
Who else? His Thracian host destroyed him.

Agamemnon.
Poor woman. Was he so greedy to get the gold?

Hecuba.
Exactly that, once he learned of the Phrygians' disaster.

Agamemnon.
Where did you find him? Or did someone bring the body?

Hecuba.
This woman. She came upon him on the seashore.

Agamemnon.
Looking for him, or busy with some other work?

Hecuba.
She had gone to fetch water from the sea, to wash Polyxena.

Agamemnon.
So his host killed him, it seems, and threw him out.

Hecuba.
To drift in the sea. After cutting up his body like this.

Agamemnon.
Cruel, what you have suffered. Troubles without measure.

Hecuba.
I am destroyed, Agamemnon. There is no evil left.

Agamemnon.
Pheu, pheu. What woman was ever born so unlucky?

Hecuba.
There is none, unless you mean Luck herself. But hear why I am falling
at your knees. If you think what I have suffered is what the gods allow,
I will put up with it. But if you think the opposite, then be my avenger
on this man, the most godless of hosts, who feared neither the gods
below the earth nor the gods above, and did the most godless thing. He
sat at my table many times. He was counted first among my friends in the
tally of guest-friendship. He had everything he should have had, and
then he took his precautions and killed my son. And having chosen to
kill him, he did not think him worth a grave, but threw him into the
sea.

We are slaves, then, and weak, perhaps. But the gods are strong, and so
is what rules over them: Law. It is by law that we believe in the gods
at all, and live our lives with right and wrong marked out. If that law
comes up to you for judgement and is destroyed, if the men who kill
their guests, or have the nerve to carry off the holy things of the
gods, do not pay for it, then there is nothing fair left anywhere among
human beings. Count this as a disgrace, then, and have some respect for
me. Pity us. Stand back from me like a painter from his picture, and
look at me, and look closely at the evils I have. I was a queen once,
and now I am your slave. I had fine children once, and now I am old, and
childless at the same time, with no city, alone, the most wretched of
mortals.

(Agamemnon turns away.)

Oh, wretched woman. Where are you drawing your foot away from me? It
seems I will get nothing. Oh, poor woman that I am. Why is it that we
mortals labour at all the other kinds of knowledge, as we should, and
search them out, but Persuasion, the one thing that rules human beings
absolutely, we take no more trouble to learn to the end, and pay fees
for it, so that a man could persuade people of whatever he wanted and
get it at the same time? How can anyone hope to do well after this? The
sons I had are no longer mine, and she, my daughter, is a captive, in
shame. I am gone. I see the smoke of my city leaping up over there.

And then, perhaps this is an empty part of the argument, to put
Aphrodite forward, but all the same it is going to be said. At your side
sleeps my daughter, the god-possessed girl, the one the Phrygians call
Cassandra. Where will you show that those nights were dear to you, my
lord? What thanks will my daughter have for the loving embraces in your
bed, and I for hers? It is out of the dark, and the love-charms of the
night, that mortals get their greatest gratitude. Listen, then. Do you
see this dead man? If you do right by him, you do right by a kinsman of
your own bed. My speech is short of one thing still. If only I had a
voice in my arms, and in my hands, and in my hair, and in the tread of
my feet, by the arts of Daedalus or some god, so that all of them
together could take hold of your knees, weeping, and press every kind of
argument on you. Master, greatest light of the Greeks, be persuaded.
Lend the old woman your hand for her revenge, even if she is nothing. Do
it anyway. It is a good man's part to serve justice, and to do evil to
the evil, always, everywhere.

Chorus.
It is strange how everything falls together for mortals, and how the
laws mark out what has to be, making friends of the bitterest enemies,
and enemies of the people who used to be kind.

Agamemnon.
Hecuba, I pity you, and your son, and your fortunes, and the hand you
hold out to me. And for the gods' sake, and for justice, I want this
godless host to pay you this penalty, if only some way could be found
for it to go well for you, and for me not to look, to the army, as if I
had planned this killing of the king of Thrace for Cassandra's sake.
There is a point where confusion has come over me. The army counts this
man a friend, and the dead man an enemy. If he is dear to you, that is a
separate matter, and the army has no share in it. Think about that. You
have me willing to share your work and quick to help, but slow, if I am
going to be slandered to the Achaeans.

Hecuba.
Pheu. There is no mortal who is free. He is a slave to money, or to
luck, or the mass of the city or the written laws keep him from acting
the way his own judgement tells him. Since you are afraid, and give the
mob too much weight, I will set you free of that fear. Know about it, if
I plan some evil against the man who killed this boy, but do not do it
with me. And if there is an uproar among the Achaeans, or an attempt to
rescue the Thracian while he is suffering what he is going to suffer,
hold them back, without seeming to do it for my sake. The rest, take
heart, I will arrange it all well.

Agamemnon.
How? What will you do? Take a sword in your old hand and kill the
barbarian? Or with poisons, or with somebody's help? What hand will be
with you? Where will you get friends?

Hecuba.
These tents hide a crowd of Trojan women.

Agamemnon.
You mean the captives? The Greeks' catch?

Hecuba.
With them I will punish the man who murdered my son.

Agamemnon.
And how are women going to get the better of men?

Hecuba.
Numbers are a terrible thing, and with cunning added, hard to fight.

Agamemnon.
Terrible. Still, I have a low opinion of the female race.

Hecuba.
Why? Did women not kill the sons of Aegyptus, and empty Lemnos of men,
every one of them? But let it be so. Drop this argument, and send this
woman safely through the army for me.

(She turns to the Maid.)

And you, go up to my Thracian guest-friend and say this: "Hecuba, who
was queen of Troy once, calls for you, on your own business no less than
hers, and for your children too. Your children must hear what she has to
say as well."

(The Maid goes off through the camp to find Polymestor.)

And hold back the burial of Polyxena, Agamemnon, slaughtered so lately,
so that these two, brother and sister, can be hidden in the earth side
by side, in one flame, a double grief for their mother.

Agamemnon.
It shall be so. If the army could sail, I could not have given you this
favour. But as it is, since the god is sending no favouring wind, we
have to wait, and watch for a calm crossing. May it turn out well,
somehow. This much is common to everyone, to each man privately and to
the city: that the bad man suffers something bad, and the good man
prospers.

(Agamemnon goes. Hecuba waits beside the body.)
