Andromache
(Part 1 of 3)

(Phthia, in Thessaly, before the house of Neoptolemus. Beside the house
stands the shrine of Thetis, with the statue of the goddess and an
altar. Andromache is sitting at the altar.)

Andromache.
City of Thebe, the pride of Asia. That is where I came from, once, to
Priam's royal hearth, with a dowry heavy with gold, given to Hector as
his wife, to bear his children. Andromache. In those days it was a name
other women envied. Now, if any woman ever was, I am the unluckiest
woman alive. There is nobody born, and nobody who ever will be, who has
been through what I have. I watched my husband Hector killed by
Achilles. I watched the son I bore him, Astyanax, thrown from the high
towers, on the day the Greeks took the plain of Troy. And I myself, who
was counted a daughter of the freest house there was, came to Greece a
slave, handed over to the islander Neoptolemus as his prize of war,
picked out of the Trojan plunder specially for him. So here I live, on
the flat land where Phthia's ground runs into the ground of the city of
Pharsalus, the place where Thetis the sea-goddess lived with Peleus,
away from other people, keeping clear of the crowd. The Thessalians call
it Thetideion, the goddess's ground, in memory of her wedding. This is
where the son of Achilles made his home. He lets Peleus go on ruling
Pharsalus: he does not want to take the sceptre from the old man while
he is alive. And in this house I have given birth to a boy, by the son
of Achilles. My master.

Before, bad as things were, I always had one hope to lead me on: if my
child lived, I might find some defence in him, some help against my
troubles. But since my master married the Spartan woman, Hermione, and
pushed my slave's bed to one side, she has hounded me with cruelties.
She says I am making her childless with secret drugs, and making her
husband hate her, and that I want to live in this house in her place
and throw her out of her own marriage bed by force. I never wanted that
bed in the first place, and now I have left it altogether. Great Zeus
be my witness: I did not share that bed by choice. But I cannot make her
believe it. She wants to kill me, and her father Menelaus is helping his
daughter do it. He is in the house right now. He came from Sparta for
exactly this. I was terrified, so I have come to the shrine of Thetis,
next door to the house, and here I sit, hoping it will stop them killing
me. Peleus and the family of Peleus hold it sacred: it is the monument
of his marriage to the Nereid. My only son I have smuggled away in
secret to another house, because I am afraid they will kill him. His
father is not here to help me, and he is no use to the child at all. He
is away in the country of Delphi, paying Apollo for his madness: the day
he went to Pytho and demanded that Apollo pay for killing his father.
Now he is asking pardon for those old offences, hoping to make the god
kind to him from here on.

(The Maid, one of the Trojan slaves of the house, comes out.)

Maid.
Mistress. I am not afraid to call you by that name, because I thought
it was your due in your own house too, when we lived in the land of
Troy. I was loyal to you, and to your husband while he lived, and now I
have come to you with fresh news. I am frightened, in case one of the
masters notices, but I pity you. Menelaus and his daughter are planning
terrible things against you, and you must be on your guard.

Andromache.
Dearest fellow slave. Because that is what you are now, a fellow slave
to the woman who used to be your queen, and is now in misery. What are
they doing? What plots are they weaving this time, wanting to kill me,
wretched as I already am?

Maid.
Your son, poor woman. They are going to kill your son, the one you
smuggled out of the house.

Andromache.
Oh. She has found out that I sent my child away? How? From where? Oh,
this is misery. I am finished.

Maid.
I do not know how. But I heard it from them myself. And Menelaus has
left the house to go and fetch him.

Andromache.
Then I am finished. My child, the two vultures will take you and kill
you, and the man they call your father is still in Delphi.

Maid.
Yes. I do not think you would be treated like this if he were here. As
it is, you have no friends left.

Andromache.
Has there been no word about Peleus, that he is coming?

Maid.
He is old. Too old to help you, even if he were here.

Andromache.
And yet I have sent for him, and not just once.

Maid.
You do not really think any of your messengers cares about you?

Andromache.
Why would they? Well then. Will you go, as my messenger?

Maid.
And what do I say, when I have been so long away from the house?

Andromache.
You will find plenty of excuses. You are a woman.

Maid.
It is dangerous. Hermione does not keep a careless watch.

Andromache.
You see? You back out on your friends when they are in trouble.

Maid.
No, I do not. Do not throw that at me. I will go. A slave woman's life
is not much looked at anyway, whatever happens to me.

(The Maid goes off to find Peleus.)

Andromache.
Go, then. And I will do what I always do: stretch out to the sky the
laments and groans and tears I live in. It is born in women, to take a
kind of pleasure in the troubles they have, keeping them always in the
mouth and on the tongue. And I have not one thing to groan over but
many: my father's city, Hector dead, the hard fate I was yoked to when I
fell into the day of slavery, and did not deserve it. Never call any
mortal happy until he is dead, and you have seen how he got through his
last day and went down below.

Andromache.
	It was no bride Paris brought home to high Ilium. It was ruin
	he brought to bed in his room, when he brought Helen.
	For her sake, Troy, the swift war-god of Greece came with a thousand ships
	and took you, a prey to the spear and the fire,
	and took my husband, Hector, mine, unhappy woman, whom the son of Thetis of the sea
	dragged behind his chariot round the walls.
	And I was led out of my room to the edge of the sea,
	slavery, the hateful thing, thrown over my head.
	Many tears ran down my face, when I left
	the city and my room and my husband in the dust.
	Oh, unhappy me. Why did I have to go on seeing the light,
	as Hermione's slave? Worn down by her,
	I have come to this statue of the goddess and thrown my arms round it, begging,
	and I melt like a spring of water trickling out of the rock.

(The Chorus, women of Phthia, come in.)

Chorus.
	Lady, you have sat so long on the floor of Thetis's shrine
	and you will not leave it.
	I am a woman of Phthia, but I have come to you, a daughter of Asia,
	to see if there is anything I can do,
	any cure I can cut for these troubles no one can untie,
	the troubles that have locked you and Hermione in a hateful quarrel,
	poor woman, over a bed
	shared by two,
	over the son of Achilles.

Chorus.
	Know your luck. Reckon up the evil you have come to, where you stand.
	Are you a match for your masters,
	a girl from Ilium against a daughter born in Sparta?
	Leave the house where the sheep are offered,
	the house of the goddess of the sea. What good does it do you,
	terrified, to waste your body with weeping,
	when your masters hold the power?
	Power will come down on you. Why labour,
	when you are nothing, at a labour that comes to nothing?

Chorus.
	Come. Leave the shining seat of the goddess, the Nereid.
	Know what you are: a slave on foreign ground,
	in another people's city,
	where you see not one friend
	of your own, unluckiest of women,
	a bride of every sorrow.

Chorus.
	You were the most pitiful thing I ever saw, Trojan woman, when you came
	to my masters' house. But out of fear
	we keep quiet.
	I do pity what has happened to you,
	but I am afraid the child of Zeus's daughter
	might find out that I wish you well.

(Hermione comes out of the house, richly dressed.)

Hermione.
This gold ornament on my head, and this embroidered dress on my body: I
did not come out here wearing them as the first-fruits of the house of
Achilles or of Peleus. They came from Sparta, in Laconia. My father
Menelaus gave me them, with a great dowry besides, so that I can say
what I like. That is my answer to you women. As for you: you are a
slave, a woman won at spear-point, and you want to throw us out and
take possession of this house. My husband hates me because of your
drugs. My womb is dying, barren, because of you. The women of Asia have
a terrible gift for that kind of thing. But I will put a stop to it, and
this house of the Nereid will do you no good, not the altar, not the
temple. You are going to die. And if some man or god does want to save
you, then instead of the proud thoughts of your rich old days you will
have to cower, and go down on your knees at my feet, and sweep my
house, and sprinkle water from the river Achelous out of gold jugs with
your own hand, and learn where on earth you are. There is no Hector
here, no Priam, no gold. This is a Greek city. And you, you wretched
creature, have come to such a pitch of ignorance that you have the
nerve to sleep with the son of the father who killed your husband, and
bear children to the murderer's blood. That is the whole barbarian race.
Father sleeps with daughter, son with mother, sister with brother; the
nearest and dearest kill each other, and no law stops any of it. Do not
bring that in among us. Here it is not thought decent for one man to
hold the reins of two women. Men here look to one wife's bed and are
content with it, any man who does not want a house that is a disgrace.

Chorus.
A woman's mind is a jealous thing, and always hardest on the women who
share her marriage.

Andromache.
Pheu, pheu. Youth is a bad thing in a human being, when youth comes
with injustice in it. What I am afraid of is that my being your slave
will push my arguments aside, however many good ones I have; and if I
win, I am afraid I will pay for winning. People who breathe big do not
take it kindly when the better argument comes from someone beneath
them. Still, I will not be caught betraying my own case.

Tell me, young woman: what solid ground could have persuaded me to push
you out of your lawful marriage? That Sparta is a smaller city than
Phrygia? That my luck outruns yours? That you see a free woman when you
look at me? Am I so puffed up with a young, firm body, a great city, and
friends, that I want your house instead of you? Why, so that I can bear
children in your place, slaves, a wretched string of them dragging
behind me? Or will anyone put up with my sons as kings of Phthia if you
have none? The Greeks love me so much, for Hector's sake? And I was a
nobody, was I, no queen in Phrygia? It is not my drugs that make your
husband hate you. It is that you are not fit to live with. That is a
love-charm too: it is not beauty, lady, it is goodness that pleases the
man in your bed. But if anything scratches you, then Sparta is a great
city and Scyros is nowhere; you are rich among people who are not;
Menelaus is a bigger man than Achilles. That is what your husband hates
in you. A woman, even if she is given to a bad husband, has to be
content, and not set herself up against him. If you had a king for a
husband in Thrace, the country the melting snow pours through, where
one man shares his bed with many wives in turn, would you have killed
them all? You would have been caught fastening a charge of insatiable
lust on every woman alive. A disgrace. And yet it is true we suffer this
sickness worse than men do. But we put a good face on it. Dearest
Hector, for your sake I even loved alongside you, whenever Aphrodite
tripped you up, and many a time I put my breast to your bastards, so
that I would never give you cause for bitterness. By doing that I bound
my husband to me, by being good. But you will not let a drop of the
sky's dew settle on your husband, you are so afraid. Do not try to outdo
your mother, lady, in a weakness for men. Children with any sense should
run from the ways of a bad mother.

Chorus.
Mistress, as far as it comes easily to you, let yourself be persuaded,
and come to terms with her.

Hermione.
Why the high-minded speeches? Why turn this into a debate, as if you
were the one who kept herself in hand, and I were the one who did not?

Andromache.
You certainly do not, on the arguments you have just been standing on.

Hermione.
May your kind of mind never live in my house, woman.

Andromache.
You are young, and you are talking about shameful things.

Hermione.
You do not talk about them. You do them to me, as far as you can.

Andromache.
Can you not suffer your love-troubles in silence?

Hermione.
What? Is that not the first thing for women, everywhere?

Andromache.
Yes, for women who handle it well. Otherwise it is not decent.

Hermione.
We do not run our city by barbarian laws.

Andromache.
What is shameful there is shameful here too.

Hermione.
Clever. Very clever. And you are still going to die.

Andromache.
Do you see the statue of Thetis, looking straight at you?

Hermione.
Yes. Hating your country, for the killing of Achilles.

Andromache.
Helen destroyed him, not I. Your mother.

Hermione.
Are you going to keep on poking at my wounds?

Andromache.
There. I am silent. I am holding my mouth shut.

Hermione.
Tell me the one thing I came out here for.

Andromache.
I tell you that you have less sense than you need.

Hermione.
Will you leave this holy ground of the goddess of the sea?

Andromache.
If I am not going to die. Otherwise, never.

Hermione.
Then that is settled. I will not wait for my husband to come.

Andromache.
And I will not give myself up to you before he does.

Hermione.
I will bring fire against you, and I will not stop for anything of
yours.

Andromache.
Burn away, then. The gods will know it.

Hermione.
And the pain of terrible wounds on your skin.

Andromache.
Cut my throat. Bloody the goddess's altar. She will come after you.

Hermione.
You barbarian thing, hard and bold. So you will stand up to death, will
you? Well, I will get you up off that seat, and soon, and you will get
up willingly. I have the right bait for you. But I will keep the words
to myself; the thing itself will show you soon enough. Sit tight. Even
if molten lead were holding you there, I will have you up before the
son of Achilles comes, the man you trust so much.

(Hermione goes back into the house.)

Andromache.
I do trust him. It is a strange thing: some god found cures for men
against savage snakes, but the thing that is worse than a viper, worse
than fire, a bad woman, nobody has yet found a drug for that. That is
how great an evil we are to human beings.

Chorus.
	Great griefs began, then,
	on the day the son of Maia and Zeus
	came to the glen on Ida
	driving the lovely three-horse team of goddesses,
	harnessed for a hateful quarrel over beauty,
	to the herdsman's steading,
	to the young man alone with his cattle,
	and the lonely
	hearth of his hut.

Chorus.
	And when they came to the wooded glen,
	they washed their bodies till they shone
	in the streams of the mountain springs,
	and went to the son of Priam,
	outbidding each other with spiteful words,
	and Aphrodite won with treacherous words,
	sweet to hear,
	and bitter: the wreck of a life
	for the luckless city of the Phrygians
	and the towers of Troy.

Chorus.
	If only the mother who bore him
	had struck Paris a deathblow over the head
	before he settled
	on the rock of Ida,
	when Cassandra at the holy laurel
	screamed out to kill him,
	the great ruin of Priam's city.
	Whom did she not go to? Which of the city's elders
	did she not beg
	to put the baby to death?

Chorus.
	Then the yoke of slavery would never have come
	on the women of Ilium, and you, lady,
	would have kept
	your seat in a royal house;
	and Greece would have been spared the painful
	labours of the ten years her young men
	wandered round Troy with their spears.
	Beds would never have been left empty,
	and old men
	robbed of their children.

(Menelaus comes in with attendants, leading Andromache's small son.)

Menelaus.
I have come, and I have your son, the one you smuggled out to another
house behind my daughter's back. You were so sure this image of the
goddess would save you, and the people who hid him would save him. But
you have been found out, woman, less clever than Menelaus here. And if
you do not get up and clear out of this ground, his throat will be cut
instead of yours. So reckon it up: do you want to die, or do you want
him to die for the wrong you are doing to me and my daughter?

Andromache.
Reputation. Reputation. You have puffed up ten thousand nobodies into
great lives. The people whose good name rests on the truth, I call them
happy; the ones who have it by lies, I will not allow that they have
anything but the luck of seeming clever. So it was you, was it, who led
the picked men of Greece and took Troy from Priam? A man as feeble as
this? A man who has worked himself into such a rage over the words of
a daughter who is barely more than a child, and gone into the ring
against an unlucky slave woman? I do not think you were worthy of Troy
any more, or Troy of you. The people who seem clever are bright on the
outside; inside they are the same as everybody else, except perhaps in
money, and money is a great strength.

Menelaus, come on, let us take this argument through to the end.
Suppose I am dead at your daughter's hands, and she has destroyed me.
She will never escape the stain of murder; and in most people's eyes
you will stand trial for this blood too. You helped her, and the debt of
it will drag you in. And suppose I slip out of dying myself: will you
kill my son? Then how will his father take his child's death lightly?
Troy does not call him that much of a coward. No, he will go where he
must, and he will show himself worthy of Peleus and of his father
Achilles in what he does, and he will throw your daughter out of his
house. And you, when you are offering her to another man, what will you
say? That her decency ran away from a bad husband? That is a lie, and
he will find it out. Who will marry her? Or will you keep her in your
house without a man, a grey-haired widow? Poor man, do you not see the
flood of evils pouring in on you? How many wrongs against her marriage
bed would you rather your daughter had found, than suffer what I am
telling you? You should not build great evils on small grounds; and if
we women are a deadly evil, men should not make their nature like ours.
Because if I am drugging your daughter and aborting her womb, as she
says, then willingly, not against my will, and without crouching at any
altar, I will submit to justice myself at the hands of your son-in-law,
to whom I owe no smaller damages for making him childless. That is what
I am. But as for your mind, there is one thing I am afraid of in you: it
was a quarrel over a woman that made you destroy the poor city of
the Phrygians too.

Chorus.
You have said too much, for a woman speaking to men. The sense that
should keep you in hand has shot its last arrow.

Menelaus.
Woman, these are small matters, not worth my throne, as you say, and
not worth Greece. But understand this: whatever a man happens to need,
that is a bigger thing to him than taking Troy. And I stand by my
daughter as her ally, because I count it a great thing, being robbed of
her marriage. Anything else a woman suffers comes second. When she loses
her husband she loses her life. Neoptolemus should have command of my
slaves, and my people and I of his, because friends, if they are really
friends, have nothing of their own; everything is held in common. And if
I wait for the absent instead of arranging my affairs as well as I can,
I am a fool, not a wise man. So get up out of the goddess's shrine. If
you die, this boy escapes his death; if you refuse to die, I kill him.
One of the two of you has to leave life.

Andromache.
Oh. That is a bitter lottery you are setting me, a bitter choice of
life. If I draw the lot I am wretched; if I do not draw it I am
destroyed. You, who are doing something enormous for a small cause:
listen to me. Why are you killing me? For what? What city have I
betrayed? Which of your children have I killed? What house have I
burned? I was forced into bed with my masters. And then it is me you
will kill, not him, the man who is to blame? You let the beginning go
and rush for the end that came after it? Oh, these evils. My poor
country. What terrible things I suffer. Why did I have to give birth as
well, and add a second weight on top of this one? But why do I weep over
that, and not wring out the evils at my feet and reckon them up? I saw
Hector butchered and dragged behind the chariot wheels. I saw Ilium
burning, a pitiful sight. And I went aboard the Argive ships myself, a
slave, dragged by the hair, and when I got to Phthia I was married off
to Hector's killers. What is sweet in life to me? What am I supposed to
look at? My present luck or my past? I had one child left, this one,
the eye of my life, and the people who have decided this are going to
kill him. No. Not to save my miserable life. In him there is hope, if he
lives; and for me it is a disgrace not to die for my son. Look. I leave
the altar. I am in your hands, to cut my throat, to murder, to bind, to
hang by the neck. Child, the mother who bore you is going to Hades so
that you will not die. And if you slip out of your death, remember your
mother, what I endured, how I died, and go to your father with kisses,
pouring tears, with your arms round his neck, and tell him what
happened to me. So it is true: for everyone alive, children are their
life. Whoever has none and sneers at that feels less pain, but his
happiness is a poor thing.
