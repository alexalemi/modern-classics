The Bacchae
(Part 2 of 3)

Pentheus.
Because they have far less sense than Greeks.

Dionysus.
In this they have more. Their customs are different, that is all.

Pentheus.
These rites of yours. Do you hold them at night, or by day?

Dionysus.
At night, mostly. Darkness has a solemnity to it.

Pentheus.
Where women are concerned, that is a trick, and a rotten one.

Dionysus.
Anyone who wants something shameful can find it in daylight too.

Pentheus.
You are going to pay for these dirty clever tricks of yours.

Dionysus.
And you for your ignorance, and for insulting the god.

Pentheus.
How bold the Bacchant is. And not out of practice at talking, either.

Dionysus.
Tell me what I have to suffer. What is this terrible thing you are
going to do to me?

Pentheus.
First, I will cut off that soft curl of yours.

Dionysus.
The hair is holy. I grow it for the god.

Pentheus.
Next, hand over that thyrsus.

Dionysus.
Take it from me yourself. It is Dionysus's wand, and I carry it.

Pentheus.
And we will keep your body inside, in a cell.

Dionysus.
The god himself will set me free, whenever I want.

Pentheus.
Yes. When you stand among your Bacchants and call him.

Dionysus.
He is close by right now, and he sees what is being done to me.

Pentheus.
And where is he? He is not visible to my eyes.

Dionysus.
Here, with me. You are godless yourself, so you cannot see him.

Pentheus.
Seize him. This man is sneering at me, and at Thebes.

Dionysus.
I tell you not to bind me. I have myself in hand, and you do not.

Pentheus.
And I say bind him. I have more authority here than you.

Dionysus.
You do not know what your life is, or what you are doing, or who you
are.

Pentheus.
Pentheus, the son of Agave, and my father is Echion.

Dionysus.
Pentheus. Grief. You have a name that fits a man about to come to
grief.

Pentheus.
Go. Lock him up near the horses' stalls, where he can look at nothing
but darkness. Dance in there. As for these women you have brought with
you, your partners in crime, either we will sell them off, or I will
stop their hands from this thumping and this beating on drumskins and
keep them as slaves at my looms.

Dionysus.
I will go. What is not going to happen, I do not have to suffer. But
Dionysus, the god you say does not exist, will come after you to make
you pay for these outrages. When you wrong me, it is him you are
putting in chains.

(The guards take Dionysus into the house. Pentheus follows.)

Chorus.
	Daughter of Achelous,
	lady Dirce, lovely girl of a river,
	it was in your springs, once,
	that you took the baby of Zeus,
	when Zeus his father snatched him
	out of the deathless fire and hid him in his thigh,
	and shouted this:
	Come, Dithyrambus, child of the double door,
	come into this male womb of mine.
	I show you to Thebes, Bacchic one,
	so that they will call you by this name.
	And you, blessed Dirce,
	you push me away,
	when I come to your banks with my worshippers in garlands.
	Why do you refuse me? Why do you run from me?
	A day is coming, I swear it by the clustered grapes,
	the grace of Dionysus's vine,
	a day is coming when you will care about Dionysus.

Chorus.
	What rage, what rage he shows,
	the breed that came out of the earth,
	Pentheus, sprung from the snake
	that Echion planted, Echion the earth-born,
	a wild-faced monster, not a mortal man,
	a killer, like one of the giants
	who fought the gods.
	He will have me in ropes soon,
	me, who belong to Dionysus,
	and he already has my fellow-worshipper
	inside his house,
	hidden in a dark cell.
	Do you see this, Dionysus, son of Zeus,
	your prophets wrestling with force?
	Come, lord, down from Olympus,
	shaking your gold-faced thyrsus,
	and put a stop to the outrage of a murderous man.

Chorus.
	Where are you, Dionysus? On Nysa,
	that nurses the wild beasts,
	leading your worshippers with the thyrsus,
	or on the Corycian peaks?
	Or perhaps in the deep-wooded
	hollows of Olympus, where once
	Orpheus played his lyre
	and drew the trees together with his music,
	drew the wild beasts of the fields to him.
	Blessed Pieria,
	the god of the cry honours you: he will come
	to dance his revels there,
	and cross the fast-running Axius
	and bring his whirling Maenads,
	and cross the Lydias too,
	the father who gives mortals their wealth,
	the river I have heard
	makes a country of fine horses rich
	with the loveliest of waters.

(The voice of Dionysus is heard from inside the house.)

Dionysus.
	Io.
	Hear my voice, hear it,
	io, Bacchants, io, Bacchants.

Chorus.
	Who is that? Who? Where did it come from,
	the shout that called me up, the shout of the god of the cry?

Dionysus.
	Io, io, I call again,
	the son of Semele, the son of Zeus.

Chorus.
	Io, io, master, master,
	come to us now, to your worshippers,
	Dionysus, Dionysus.

Dionysus.
	Shake the floor of the earth, Lady Earthquake.

Chorus.
	Ah. Ah.
	Soon the house of Pentheus
	will be shaken down in ruins.
	Dionysus is in the house.
	Worship him. We worship him, oh.
	Did you see the stone lintels
	on the pillars there, splitting apart?
	Dionysus is shouting his victory inside the roof.

Dionysus.
Light the blazing torch of the lightning. Burn, burn down the house of
Pentheus.

Chorus.
	Ah. Ah.
	Do you not see the fire? Do you not see it,
	round the holy tomb of Semele,
	the flame the thunderbolt of Zeus
	left there when it struck her?
	Throw your trembling bodies on the ground,
	throw them down, Maenads. The lord
	is coming against this house
	and turning it upside down,
	the son of Zeus.

(The women fall to the ground. Dionysus comes out of the ruined house,
unbound.)

Dionysus.
Barbarian women, are you so struck with fear that you have fallen to
the ground? You felt Dionysus shake the house of Pentheus, it seems.
Get up. Lift yourselves and take heart, and let the trembling go out
of your flesh.

Chorus.
Greatest light of our joyful worship, how glad I am to see you. I was
alone, and I had nobody.

Dionysus.
Did you lose heart when I was taken inside, on my way to fall into
Pentheus's dark cells?

Chorus.
How could I not? Who was there to protect me if something happened to
you? But how did you get free, after falling into that godless man's
hands?

Dionysus.
I saved myself. Easily. No trouble at all.

Chorus.
Did he not tie your hands together in knotted ropes?

Dionysus.
That was exactly where I made a fool of him. He thought he was tying
me up, and he never touched me, never laid a hand on me. He was
feeding on hope. He found a bull by the stalls where he had taken me
to lock me up, and it was the bull he threw his ropes round, knees
and hooves, panting out his rage, sweat dripping off his body, biting
his lips with his teeth. And I sat quietly close by and watched.

In the middle of this Dionysus came and shook the house and lit a fire
on his mother's tomb. When Pentheus saw it, he thought the house was
burning, and he ran this way and that, shouting to the slaves to bring
water, and every slave in the place was hard at work, and all for
nothing. Then he dropped that job, since I had escaped, snatched up a
dark sword and rushed into the house. And then Dionysus, this is how
it looked to me, I am only giving you my opinion, made a phantom of
me in the courtyard. Pentheus went for it. He charged and stabbed at
the bright air, as if he were cutting my throat. And on top of this,
Dionysus did him other damage. He brought the house down to the
ground. It lies in ruins, all of it. Pentheus has seen what my chains
cost: they turned out very bitter for him. Worn out, he has dropped
his sword and gone limp. He is a man, and he had the nerve to go into
battle against a god.

I walked quietly out of the house and here I am with you, and
Pentheus does not worry me at all. I think, I can hear a boot inside
the house, anyway, that he will be out at the front any moment. What
on earth is he going to say after all this? I will take him easily,
even if he comes out breathing hard. A clever man keeps his temper in
hand.

(Pentheus comes out of the house.)

Pentheus.
Something terrible has happened to me. The stranger has got away, the
one I had in chains a minute ago. Ah. Ah. There he is. What is this?
How are you out here, standing in front of my house? How did you get
outside?

Dionysus.
Stand still. Set your anger down and walk quietly.

Pentheus.
How did you slip your chains and get out here?

Dionysus.
Did I not say, or were you not listening, that someone would set me
free?

Pentheus.
Who? You keep bringing in new stories.

Dionysus.
The one who grows the clustering vine for mortals.

Dionysus.
You meant that as an insult. It is Dionysus's glory.

Pentheus.
I order every tower in the wall shut fast, all the way round.

Dionysus.
What for? Do gods not climb over walls too?

Pentheus.
Clever. You are very clever, except in the one thing you ought to be
clever in.

Dionysus.
In the thing that matters most, I was born clever. But first listen to
this man and hear what he has to say. He has come from the mountain
with news for you. I will wait for you. I am not going to run.

(A Messenger comes in, a herdsman down from the mountain.)

Messenger.
Pentheus, ruler of this land of Thebes, I have come from Cithaeron,
where the bright falls of white snow never let go.

Pentheus.
And what is this urgent news you have come to bring?

Messenger.
I have seen the holy Bacchants, the women who shot out of this country
like arrows, white legs flying, stung mad, and I have come to tell you
and the city, my lord, that they are doing terrible things, things
beyond wonder. But I want to hear first whether I should tell you
freely what is happening up there, or trim my story. I am afraid of
the quickness of your mind, my lord, and your sharp temper, and too
much of the king in you.

Pentheus.
Speak. Whatever you say, you will go unpunished by me. It is wrong to
be angry with people who do right. And the more terrible the things
you tell me about the Bacchants, the harder I will make this man pay,
the one who taught the women their tricks.

Messenger.
The herds of grazing cattle were just climbing up towards the high
ground, the calves with them, at the hour when the sun sends out its
rays and warms the earth. And I see three companies of women dancers.
Autonoe led one, your mother Agave the second, and Ino the third. They
were all asleep, their bodies gone slack, some leaning their backs
against the foliage of a pine, others with their heads dropped anyhow
on the oak leaves on the ground. Decently. Not as you say: drunk on
the wine-bowl and the noise of the pipe, hunting Aphrodite alone
through the woods.

Your mother heard the lowing of the horned cattle, and she stood up in
the middle of the Bacchants and gave the cry, to get their bodies
moving out of sleep. And they threw the fresh sleep off their eyes and
sprang upright, a marvel of good order to look at: young women, old
women, and girls still unyoked, unmarried. First they let their hair
down over their shoulders, and those whose fawnskins had come loose at
the knots fastened them up again, and belted the dappled hides with
snakes that licked their cheeks. Some of them held a fawn, or the wild
cubs of wolves, in their arms and gave them white milk: the ones who
had left new babies at home and whose breasts were still swollen. They
put on garlands of ivy and oak and flowering bryony. One took a
thyrsus and struck it against a rock, and a dewy stream of water leapt
out of it. Another set her wand down against the ground, and there the
god sent up a spring of wine for her. Whoever wanted the white drink
scraped the earth with the tips of her fingers and had milk in
streams; and from the ivy on the thyrsus-wands sweet rivers of honey
dripped. If you had been there and seen this, the god you are abusing
now, you would have gone to him with prayers.

We herdsmen and shepherds came together to argue about it, trading
talk about the terrible, wonderful things they were doing. And one
man, a wanderer about the town with a practised tongue, said to all of
us: "You who live on the holy uplands of the mountains, what do you
say? Shall we hunt Agave, Pentheus's mother, out of her revels, and do
the king a favour?" We thought he talked sense, and we lay in ambush,
hiding ourselves in the leaves of the bushes. At the appointed hour
they began to shake the thyrsus for the revels, calling with one voice
on the son of Zeus, calling him Iacchus, calling him Dionysus, and the
whole mountain went mad along with them, and the wild animals with it,
and nothing stood still: everything was running. Agave happened to
come leaping past close to me, and I jumped out, wanting to grab her,
and left the thicket where I had hidden myself empty. And she
screamed: "My running hounds! We are being hunted, by these men.
Follow me, follow me, with your thyrsus-wands for weapons in your
hands."

We ran, and we got away from being torn to pieces by the Bacchants.
But they went for the heifers that were grazing the grass, with no
iron in their hands. You could have seen one woman holding a young
cow, fat with milk, bellowing, and tearing it in two with her bare
hands. Others were ripping full-grown cows apart, piece from piece.
You would have seen ribs, or a cloven hoof, flung up and down; they
hung in the pine branches, dripping, smeared with blood. Bulls, the
arrogant ones who had done all their fighting with their horns before,
stumbled forward onto the ground, dragged down by ten thousand girls'
hands. The coat of flesh was pulled off them faster than you could
close your royal eyes.

Then they were off, like birds rising into flight, over the low plains
that grow the fine Theban corn along the streams of the Asopus; and
they fell on Hysiae and Erythrae, the towns that lie down below the
crag of Cithaeron, like an enemy army, and turned everything upside
down. They snatched children out of the houses. Whatever they put on
their shoulders stayed there without being tied and did not fall to
the black ground, not bronze, not iron. They carried fire in their
hair and it did not burn them. The townspeople, in a rage at being
plundered by Bacchants, ran for their weapons, and that, my lord, was
the terrible thing to see. The men's pointed spears drew no blood, but
the women threw their thyrsus-wands from their hands and wounded them,
and turned them, and put them to flight. Women did that to men. Not
without some god's help. Then they went back to where they had
started from, to the very springs the god had sent up for them, and
washed off the blood, and snakes licked the drops off their cheeks
with their tongues and made their skin clean.

So whoever this god is, master, receive him into this city. He is
great in other ways, and they say this too of him, as I hear it: that
he gave mortals the vine that puts an end to grief. And where there is
no wine there is no Aphrodite either, and nothing else that gives
human beings any pleasure.

Chorus.
I am afraid to speak freely in front of the king, but it will be said
all the same: Dionysus is second to none of the gods.

Pentheus.
It is close now, this outrage of the Bacchants, catching like fire, a
great disgrace to us in the eyes of the Greeks. No time for hesitating.
Go to the Electran gates. Order out every man who carries a shield,
and the riders of the fast horses, and everyone who swings a light
shield and plucks a bowstring with his hand. We are marching against
the Bacchants. It is past all bearing, if we are to suffer what we are
suffering at the hands of women.

Dionysus.
You hear my words, Pentheus, and you take in none of them. You have
treated me badly, and still I say it: you should not take up arms
against a god. Keep still. Dionysus will not stand for you driving his
Bacchants off the mountains where they cry his name.

Pentheus.
Do not lecture me. You escaped your chains; be content with that. Or
shall I turn the punishment back on you?

Dionysus.
I would sacrifice to him, rather than kick against the goad in a
temper, a mortal against a god.

Pentheus.
Sacrifice? I will. A slaughter of women, which is what they deserve,
and plenty of it, in the folds of Cithaeron.

Dionysus.
You will all run. And it will be a disgrace, bronze shields turning
tail from the thyrsus-wands of Bacchants.

Pentheus.
There is no getting anywhere with this stranger I am tangled up with.
Whatever is done to him, whatever he does, he will not be quiet.

Dionysus.
My friend, there is still a way to set all this right.

Pentheus.
Doing what? Being a slave to my own slaves?

Dionysus.
I will bring the women here, without any weapons.

Pentheus.
Oh. Now you are working some trick against me.

Dionysus.
What kind of trick, if I want to save you with my skills?

Pentheus.
You made this agreement together, so you can go on with your revels
forever.

Dionysus.
I did make an agreement, that is true. With the god.

Pentheus.
Bring my armour out here. And you, stop talking.

Dionysus.
Ah. Would you like to see them, sitting together up in the mountains?

Pentheus.
Very much. I would give a great weight of gold for it.

Dionysus.
Why have you fallen into such a passion for that?

Pentheus.
It would pain me to see them drunk.

Dionysus.
And yet you would gladly see what pains you?

Pentheus.
Yes. Be sure of it. Sitting quietly under the pines.

Dionysus.
But they will track you down, even if you come secretly.

Pentheus.
Then openly. You are right about that.

Dionysus.
Shall I lead you, then? Will you take the road?

Pentheus.
Lead me. As fast as you can. I resent every minute you waste.

Dionysus.
Then put on a dress of fine linen.

Pentheus.
What? Am I to stop being a man and be counted among the women?

Dionysus.
So they do not kill you, if you are seen there as a man.

Pentheus.
Right again. You have been clever all along.

Dionysus.
Dionysus taught me all of it.

Pentheus.
So how is this good advice of yours to be carried out?

Dionysus.
I will go inside the house and dress you.

Pentheus.
Dress me in what? A woman's dress? No. Shame stops me.

Dionysus.
Then you are not so eager to watch the Maenads any more.

Pentheus.
What dress is it you say you would put on my body?

Dionysus.
I will spread long hair over your head.

Pentheus.
And the second piece of my outfit?

Dionysus.
A robe down to your feet, and a headband round your hair.

Pentheus.
And will you add anything else to these?

Dionysus.
A thyrsus for your hand, and a spotted fawnskin.

Pentheus.
I could not put on a woman's dress.

Dionysus.
Then you will shed blood, if you fight the Bacchants.

Pentheus.
True. First I must go and spy.

Dionysus.
Cleverer, anyway, than hunting evil with evil.

Pentheus.
And how will I get through the city without the Thebans seeing me?

Dionysus.
We will go by empty roads. I will lead you.

Pentheus.
Anything is better than having the Bacchants laugh at me. We will go
into the house, and I will decide what seems best.

Dionysus.
You may. Whatever you decide, I am ready for it.

Pentheus.
I will go in. Either I march with my armour on, or I follow your
advice.

(Pentheus goes into the house.)

Dionysus.
Women, the man is walking into the net. He will go to the Bacchants,
and there he will pay the penalty with his death. Dionysus, now it is
your work. You are not far off. Let us punish him. First, drive him
out of his mind: put a light madness into him. In his right mind he
will never agree to put on a woman's dress; driven out of his senses,
he will put it on. And I want him laughed at by the Thebans, led
through the city in a woman's shape, after those threats of his that
made him so terrible before. Now I am going in to dress Pentheus in
the costume he will wear down to Hades, slaughtered by his mother's
hands. He will come to know the son of Zeus, Dionysus, who is a god in
the fullest sense: most terrible, and to human beings most gentle.

(Dionysus goes into the house after him.)

Chorus.
	Will I ever, in the all-night dances,
	set my white foot
	to the beat of the god, and throw back
	my throat to the dewy air,
	like a fawn playing
	in the green joys of a meadow
	when it has escaped the terror of the hunt,
	clear of the watchers,
	over the well-woven nets,
	and the huntsman shouting
	drives his hounds to run harder,
	and she, straining, fast as a gale,
	leaps along the plain
	beside the river, glad
	of the places empty of men
	and the young shoots of the shadowy wood?
	What is cleverness? What finer gift
	do the gods give to mortals
	than to hold a hand that is stronger
	over the heads of your enemies?
	What is fine is loved forever.

Chorus.
	The strength of the gods moves slowly,
	but it is sure, all the same.
	It straightens out those mortals
	who honour their own stupidity
	and do not, in their mad conceit,
	make the things of the gods greater.
	The gods have many ways of hiding
	the long foot of time,
	and they hunt the godless man down.
	Never know, never practise
	anything stronger than the laws.
	It costs little to believe
	that whatever the divine is, it has power,
	and that what has been law through long time
	is law forever, and rooted in nature.
	What is cleverness? What finer gift
	do the gods give to mortals
	than to hold a hand that is stronger
	over the heads of your enemies?
	What is fine is loved forever.

Chorus.
	Happy the man who has run from a storm at sea
	and reached the harbour.
	Happy the man who has got above his troubles.
	In one way or another each man
	passes another in wealth or power.
	And there are hopes, ten thousand of them,
	for ten thousand men: some of them
	end in wealth for mortals,
	and some go nowhere.
	But the man whose life is happy
	day by day: that man I call blessed.

(Dionysus comes out of the house.)

Dionysus.
You there, the man so eager to see what he should not see, so hot for
what nobody should chase, I mean you, Pentheus: come out in front of
the house. Let me look at you, in the costume of a woman, a Maenad, a
Bacchant, a spy on your mother and her company.

(Pentheus comes out of the house, dressed as a woman.)

You look exactly like one of Cadmus's daughters.

Pentheus.
Look. I think I see two suns, and two Thebes, two cities with seven
gates. And you seem to be a bull walking ahead of me, and horns seem
to have grown on your head. Were you an animal before? You certainly
are a bull now.

Dionysus.
The god is walking with us. He was not kind before; now he is on our
side. Now you see what you should see.

Pentheus.
So how do I look? Do I not stand just the way Ino stands, or Agave, my
own mother?

Dionysus.
Looking at you, I think I am seeing them. But this curl of yours has
slipped out of place, not the way I set it under your headband.

Pentheus.
Inside, I was tossing it forward and back, shaking my head and playing
the Bacchant, and I shook it out of its place.
