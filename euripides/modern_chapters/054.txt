Cyclops
(Part 2 of 2)

Chorus.
	Open up that wide throat of yours, Cyclops,
	stretch your lips. It is all ready for you,
	boiled and roasted and hot off the coals,
	to gnaw, to crunch,
	to carve the limbs of strangers,
	lying back on your shaggy goatskin.

Chorus.
	No. None for me, thank you.
	Fill the hold of that boat of yours alone, by yourself.
	Goodbye to this cave.
	Goodbye to the sacrifice
	with no altar, the slaughter
	the Cyclops of Etna keeps,
	gloating over a dinner of strangers' meat.

Chorus.
	Pitiless. Wretched man, to kill the strangers
	who came begging at his hearth, and butcher them in his own house,
	and feast on them boiled, and with his filthy teeth
	chop and crunch
	men's flesh, hot from the coals.

Chorus.
	No. None for me, thank you.
	Fill the hold of that boat of yours alone, by yourself.
	Goodbye to this cave.
	Goodbye to the sacrifice
	with no altar, the slaughter
	the Cyclops of Etna keeps,
	gloating over a dinner of strangers' meat.

(Odysseus comes out of the cave, white-faced.)

Odysseus.
Zeus, what am I going to say? I have seen things inside that cave that
are terrible, and past believing, things out of the stories people
tell, not out of anything human beings do.

Chorus.
What is it, Odysseus? Has that godless Cyclops been feasting on your
dear companions?

Odysseus.
Two of them. He looked them over and weighed them in his hands, and
picked the two with the fattest, best-fed flesh on them.

Chorus.
Poor man. How did you stand it, while it was happening?

Odysseus.
When we had gone into this rocky place, first he lit a fire. He threw
the trunks of a tall oak onto his broad hearth, a load heavy enough for
three wagons. Then he spread a bed of fir branches on the ground, close
to the flame. He milked his heifers and filled a mixing bowl with white
milk, a bowl that would hold ten jars. He set a cup of ivy-wood beside
it, three cubits across and, as far as I could see, four deep. He set a
bronze cauldron boiling on the fire, and laid out spits, buckthorn
branches with their points hardened in the fire and the rest trimmed
smooth with a sickle, and the Etna butchers' bowls that catch the blood
under the axe.

And when everything was ready for that god-hated cook from hell, he
snatched up two of my companions and killed them, with one steady
movement each. One he cut over the beaten-bronze belly of the cauldron.
The other he took by the tendon at the heel and swung against the sharp
edge of a rock, and spattered his brains out. Then he hacked the flesh
off with a greedy knife and roasted it over the fire, and the limbs he
threw into the cauldron to boil. And I, God help me, with the tears
running out of my eyes, stayed close to the Cyclops and waited on him.
The others were crouched in the crannies of the rock like birds, and
there was no blood left in their skin.

When he had eaten his fill of my companions and fell back, letting out
a heavy breath from his throat, something came to me from a god. I
filled a cup with this wine of Maron's and held it out to him and said,
"Cyclops, son of the sea god, look at this. This is the divine drink
that Greece makes from the vine, the bright joy of Dionysus." And he,
stuffed with his shameless dinner, took it and pulled the whole cup
down in one draught, and raised his hand and thanked me. "Dearest of
all my guests," he said, "a fine drink you have given me, on top of a
fine dinner." When I saw how much he liked it, I gave him another cup,
knowing the wine would wound him and he would soon be paying for what
he did. And sure enough he slid into singing. I kept pouring, one cup
after another, and warming his insides with the drink. So there he is
now, singing tunelessly beside my weeping shipmates, and the cave rings
with it. And I have crept out without a sound.

I want to save you, and myself, if you are willing. So tell me: do you
want to get away from this man nobody can live with, or not, and go and
live in the house of Dionysus with the water-nymphs? Your father inside
has already said yes. But he is weak, and he is making the most of the
drink. He is stuck to that cup like a bird on a limed twig, flapping
his wings. But you are young. Save yourself, with me, and get back your
old friend Dionysus. He is nothing like the Cyclops.

Chorus.
Dearest of friends, if only we could see that day, and be out from
under the godless head of the Cyclops. It has been a long, long time
since we had our dear old spout to suckle at. And there is nowhere we
can run to get away from him.

Odysseus.
Then listen to the revenge I have in mind for this beast of a villain,
and the way out of your slavery.

Chorus.
Tell us. We would not rather hear the sweetest lyre in Asia than hear
that the Cyclops is dead.

Odysseus.
He is so pleased with this drink of Dionysus that he wants to go off
partying with his brother Cyclopes.

Chorus.
I understand. You are going to catch him alone in the woods and cut his
throat, or push him off a cliff.

Odysseus.
Nothing of the kind. What I have in mind is a trick.

Chorus.
What, then? We have been hearing for years how clever you are.

Odysseus.
First, to keep him away from this party. I will tell him he should not
give this drink to the other Cyclopes, but keep it for himself and
enjoy life. Then, when he falls asleep, beaten by Dionysus, there is an
olive branch inside the cave. I will sharpen the end of it with this
sword and put it in the fire. And then, when I see it burning, I will
lift it, red-hot, and drive it into the middle of the Cyclops' face,
and melt his eye out with fire. And the way a shipwright fitting
timbers works his drill back and forth with a strap wound twice round
it, that is how I will spin the brand in the eye that gives the
Cyclops his light, and shrivel up the pupil.

Chorus.
Iou, iou. I am delighted. We are mad with joy at what you have thought
of.

Odysseus.
And then I will put you and your friends and the old man into the
hollow hull of my black ship, and with two banks of oars I will carry
you away out of this land.

Chorus.
Is there any way I could take hold of the brand that blinds him too, as
if I were pouring a libation to a god? I want a share in this killing.

Odysseus.
You will have to. The brand is big, and it will take more than one pair
of hands.

Chorus.
I would lift the weight of a hundred wagons, if it means we smoke out
the eye of that Cyclops, damn him, like a wasps' nest.

Odysseus.
Quiet now. You know the whole trick. When I give the order, do what the
architects tell you. I am not going to abandon the friends I have
inside and save my own skin. And yet I could run. I am out of the
depths of the cave already. But it is not right to leave the friends I
came here with and be saved alone.

(He goes back into the cave.)

Chorus.
	Come on, who is first, and who is second
	behind him, to grip the handle of the brand,
	drive it in under the Cyclops' eyelids
	and grind out that bright eye?
	Quiet. Quiet. Here he comes,
	drunk, making a noise he thinks is music,
	a clumsy off-key singer who will be crying soon,
	out of his house of stone.
	Come on, let us teach this lout
	how a party is done.
	He is going to be blind either way.

Half-Chorus.
	Happy the man who shouts for the god
	with the sweet streams of the grape,
	stretched out for a party,
	his arm round a friend he loves,
	and on the couch, the yellow hair
	of some soft, expensive girl,
	his own glossy curls shining with perfume,
	and he calls out, "Who is going to open the door for me?"

(The Cyclops comes out of the cave, garlanded and reeling, with
Silenus behind him.)

Cyclops.
	Papapa. I am full of wine,
	and I am loving the freshness of that feast,
	loaded like a cargo ship
	right up to the top deck of my belly.
	This nice soft grass is tempting me out
	to a spring party
	with my brother Cyclopes.
	Come on, stranger, come on, hand me the wineskin.

Chorus.
	Here comes the handsome one, out of his house,
	casting his handsome eye around.
	Papapa. Somebody loves us.
	There is a torch waiting for you, to light your skin,
	like a torch for a tender bride
	deep in a dewy cave.
	And a garland of more than one colour
	will soon be wound close round your head.

Odysseus.
Cyclops, listen. I know this Dionysus I gave you to drink very well.

Cyclops.
And this Dionysus, what sort of god is he supposed to be?

Odysseus.
The greatest there is, for the pleasure human beings take in life.

Cyclops.
Well, he tastes good coming back up, I will say that.

Odysseus.
That is the kind of god he is. He never hurts anybody.

Cyclops.
But how does a god enjoy living in a wineskin?

Odysseus.
Wherever you put him, he is comfortable.

Cyclops.
Gods should not keep their bodies in leather.

Odysseus.
What does it matter, if he gives you pleasure? Or does the leather
taste bitter?

Cyclops.
I hate the skin. The drink, I love.

Odysseus.
Then stay right here and drink, Cyclops, and enjoy yourself.

Cyclops.
Should I not give my brothers a share of this drink?

Odysseus.
Keep it yourself and you will look more important.

Cyclops.
Give it to my friends and I will look more useful.

Odysseus.
Parties love fists, and insults, and fights.

Cyclops.
I may be drunk, but nobody is going to lay a hand on me.

Odysseus.
My good man, a man who has been drinking should stay at home.

Cyclops.
Anyone who drinks and does not go partying is an idiot.

Odysseus.
And anyone who gets drunk and stays at home is wise.

Cyclops.
What do we do, Silenus? Do you think we should stay?

Silenus.
I do. Who needs other people at the drinking, Cyclops?

Cyclops.
And the ground here is soft, come to think of it, all thick with
flowering grass...

Silenus.
And it is lovely to drink in the warm sun. Lie down for me, now. Put
your side on the ground.

(The Cyclops lies down on the grass. Silenus sets the mixing bowl
behind his back.)

Cyclops.
There. Now why are you putting the mixing bowl behind me?

Silenus.
So nobody passing by knocks it over.

Cyclops.
No. You want to steal drinks out of it. Put it down in the middle. And
you, stranger, tell me your name. What am I to call you?

Odysseus.
Nobody. And what favour am I going to get from you, to thank you for?

Cyclops.
I will eat you last, after all your companions.

Silenus.
That is a fine prize you are giving the stranger, Cyclops.

Cyclops.
You. What are you doing? Drinking the wine behind my back?

Silenus.
No. It kissed me. Because of my good looks.

Cyclops.
You will be sorry, kissing the wine when the wine does not kiss you.

Silenus.
Yes it does, by Zeus. It says it is in love with me. I am so handsome.

Cyclops.
Pour, and fill the cup. Just give it to me.

Silenus.
How is it mixed, though? Come on, let us have a look.

(He drinks.)

Cyclops.
You are killing me. Give it here as it is.

Silenus.
No, by Zeus. Not until I have seen you put a garland on, and had one
more little taste...

Cyclops.
This wine-waiter is a cheat.

Silenus.
No, by Zeus. This wine is sweet. And you need to wipe your nose before
you get a drink.

Cyclops.
There. My lip is clean, and so is my beard.

Silenus.
Now set your elbow nicely, like this, and drink it down, the way you
see me drinking. And the way you do not see me.

(He buries his face in the cup and drains it.)

Cyclops.
Ah. Ah. What are you doing?

Silenus.
Down in one. That was delicious.

Cyclops.
Stranger, take it. You be my wine-waiter yourself.

Odysseus.
Well, the vine and my hand are old acquaintances.

Cyclops.
Come on, pour.

Odysseus.
I am pouring. Just be quiet.

Cyclops.
That is a hard thing to ask of a man who drinks a lot.

Odysseus.
There. Take it and drink it off, and do not leave a drop. A man
drinking should die at the same moment as the drink.

Cyclops.
Papai. What a clever piece of wood the vine is.

Odysseus.
And if you drink a lot on top of a big dinner, and soak your belly
until it has no thirst left, it will throw you into sleep. But leave
any behind, and Dionysus will dry you out.

Cyclops.
Iou, iou. I only just swam out of that one. Pure pleasure, and neat.
The sky looks to me as if it is all mixed up with the earth and
carried round with it. I can see the throne of Zeus, and the whole
holy majesty of the gods. No, I will not kiss you. The Graces are
trying to tempt me. Enough. I have my Ganymede here, and I will go and
lie down with him, and that is the finest thing of all, by the Graces.
Somehow I have always liked boys better than women.

Silenus.
What? Am I Zeus's Ganymede, Cyclops?

Cyclops.
Yes, by Zeus. And I am carrying you off from the house of Dardanus.

Silenus.
I am done for, boys. Something dreadful is going to happen to me.

Cyclops.
Finding fault with your lover? Playing hard to get, because he has been
drinking?

Silenus.
Oh. This is a very bitter wine I am going to be tasting in a minute.

(The Cyclops hauls Silenus into the cave.)

Odysseus.
Now then, children of Dionysus, sons of a noble father. The man is
inside. Any moment now, gone slack with sleep, he will be pushing meat
back out of that shameless throat. The brand inside the cave is
sending up smoke. It is ready. There is nothing left to do but burn
out the Cyclops' eye. So see that you act like a man.

Chorus.
We will have hearts of rock and iron. Get inside, before something
unspeakable happens to my father. Out here, everything is ready for
you.

Odysseus.
Hephaestus, lord of Etna, burn the bright eye out of your evil
neighbour and be rid of him once and for all. And you, Sleep, child of
black Night, come down at full strength on this beast the gods hate.
After the glorious work at Troy, do not let Odysseus and his sailors be
destroyed by a man who cares nothing for gods or men. If you do, then
we will have to count Chance as a god, and the gods as weaker than
Chance.

(He goes into the cave.)

Chorus.
	The pincers are going to grip the neck
	of the man who eats his guests,
	and grip it hard. Soon, by fire,
	he loses the eyes that give him light.
	Already
	the brand is charred through
	and hidden in the ash, a huge
	limb of oak. Let Maron's wine come on.
	Let it do its work.
	Let it take out the eye of the raving Cyclops,
	so that his drinking ends badly.
	And I
	want to see Dionysus, the roaring god
	who loves his ivy, the god I long for,
	and leave
	the Cyclops' lonely country behind.
	Will I ever get that far?

(Odysseus comes back out of the cave.)

Odysseus.
Quiet, by the gods, you animals. Be still. Shut the hinges of your
mouths. I will not have anyone so much as breathe, or blink, or clear
his throat, in case the thing wakes up, until the sight in the
Cyclops' eye has had its fight with the fire.

Chorus.
We are quiet. We have swallowed the air with our jaws.

Odysseus.
Come on, then. Get your hands on the brand and come inside. It is
beautifully red-hot.

Chorus.
Should you not tell us which of us has to go first, and take the
burning bar and burn out the Cyclops' light, so we all share the luck?

First Satyr.
We are standing too far away from the doors to push the fire into his
eye.

Second Satyr.
And we have just gone lame.

Third Satyr.
Then the same thing has happened to you as to me. We were standing
here and we sprained our feet, I do not know how.

Odysseus.
You sprained your feet standing still?

Fourth Satyr.
	And our eyes are full
	of dust, or ash, from somewhere.

Odysseus.
Cowards. These men are no allies at all.

Chorus.
Because I feel sorry for my back and my spine, and would rather not
have my teeth knocked out with a beating, that makes me a coward? But I
know a spell of Orpheus, a very good one, that will make the brand
walk into his skull all by itself and set fire to the one-eyed son of
Earth.

Odysseus.
I knew long ago what you were made of. Now I know it better. I will
have to use my own friends. But if there is no strength in your hands,
at least cheer us on, so that my friends can get some courage from your
shouting.

Chorus.
I will do that. Let the hired man run the risks. As far as cheering
goes, let the Cyclops go up in smoke.

(He goes in. The satyrs shout toward the cave.)

Chorus.
	Io, io. Push, like heroes,
	hurry, burn out the brow
	of the beast who eats his guests.
	Let him smoke, let him burn,
	the shepherd of Etna.
	Twist it, pull it, and mind he does not
	do something wild to you in his pain.

Cyclops.
Oh. The light of my eye is burnt to charcoal.

Chorus.
A lovely victory song. Sing it again for me, Cyclops.

Cyclops.
Oh, and oh again. What an outrage. I am finished. But you will not get
out of this rock and laugh about it, you nothings. I am going to stand
in the mouth of this cave and fit my hands across it.

(The Cyclops gropes his way out of the cave, blind, his face
blackened.)

Chorus.
What are you shouting about, Cyclops?

Cyclops.
I am destroyed.

Chorus.
You do look a sight.

Cyclops.
And a miserable one, on top of it.

Chorus.
Did you fall into the middle of the coals, drunk?

Cyclops.
Nobody has destroyed me.

Chorus.
Then no one has done you any harm.

Cyclops.
Nobody is blinding my eye.

Chorus.
Then you are not blind.

Cyclops.
I wish you were as blind as—

Chorus.
But how could nobody make you blind?

Cyclops.
You are making fun of me. Where is this Nobody?

Chorus.
Nowhere, Cyclops.

Cyclops.
It was the stranger, if you want to get it right. The stranger
destroyed me, the filthy thing. He gave me the drink and drowned me in
it.

Chorus.
Wine is a terrible thing. A heavy wrestler.

Cyclops.
By the gods, have they run, or are they still inside the cave?

Chorus.
	They are standing right here, in silence,
	with the overhang of the rock for cover.

Cyclops.
	Which hand?

Chorus.
	On your right.

Cyclops.
	Where?

Chorus.
	Right up against the rock.
	Have you got them?

Cyclops.
	Trouble on top of trouble. I have hit my head
	on the rock and cracked it.

Chorus.
	And they are getting away from you.

Cyclops.
	Not this way? You said this way.

Chorus.
	No. This way, I am telling you.

Cyclops.
	Which way, then?

Chorus.
	Turn round. Over there. To your left.

Cyclops.
	Oh, they are laughing at me. You are mocking me in my misery.

Chorus.
	Not any more. He is right in front of you.

Cyclops.
	You total villain, where are you?

Odysseus.
	A long way from you,
	standing guard over this body, which belongs to Odysseus.

Cyclops.
What did you say? You have changed your name. That is a new one.

Odysseus.
It is the one my father gave me. Odysseus. And you were always going
to pay for that unholy dinner. I would have burned Troy for nothing if
I had not made you pay for murdering my companions.

Cyclops.
Aiai. An old prophecy is coming true. It said I would lose my sight at
your hands, on your way home from Troy. But it also said that you
would pay for it, that you would be tossed about on the sea for a long,
long time.

Odysseus.
Go and cry about it. I have done exactly what you say. And now I am
going down to the shore, and I will launch my ship on the Sicilian sea
and sail her home.

Cyclops.
Oh no you will not. I am going to break a piece off this rock and
throw it, and crush you, and your shipmates with you. Blind or not, I
am going up onto the hill. I know the way through this tunnel.

(He feels his way back into the cave. Odysseus goes down toward the
shore.)

Chorus.
And we are shipmates of this Odysseus now. From here on, we are the
slaves of Dionysus.
