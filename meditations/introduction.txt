Marcus Aurelius ruled the Roman empire from 161 until his death in 180, and wrote this book in Greek, for himself, while he was doing it. Two of the twelve books carry a note of where they were written: one among the Quadi on the river Granua, the other at Carnuntum. Both are army camps on the Danube frontier, where he spent much of the last decade of his life fighting on the northern border while a plague worked through the empire behind him. The book has no title from his hand, no dedication and no reader.

It was never meant to be published, and that explains most of what is odd about it. There is no argument and no order. Entries run from a page to a single line. He repeats himself constantly, working over the same handful of reminders again and again, because he needed them again and again and a private notebook has no reason to avoid saying a thing twice. How the notebook survived him is not known. It was not printed until the sixteenth century.

Book One is unlike everything after it and is worth not skipping. It is a list of debts: what he learned from his grandfather, his mother, each of his tutors, and the emperor who adopted him, item by item, down to the man who taught him not to take sides between the racing factions. The rest of the book is what he did with it.

He is a Stoic and assumes the doctrine rather than explaining it. The short form is that your own judgments are the only thing fully in your power, and that everything else — your body, your reputation, your office, the people you love, your life — is held on loan and will be called in. Marcus is not cheerful about this. Death is on nearly every page: catalogues of emperors who are now dust, the olive falling ripe, the river carrying everything off. He is also often irritated, with himself for not wanting to get out of bed and with other people for being difficult, and then irritated at being irritated. The second person throughout is himself.

This edition is all twelve books, retold from George Long's 1862 translation, whose thou-and-thee English gives the notes a formality the Greek does not have. Marcus thanks one of his teachers, Rusticus, for lending him the lectures of Epictetus, who had been born a slave. Those lectures are in this collection as the Discourses, and the handbook Arrian cut out of them as the Enchiridion.
