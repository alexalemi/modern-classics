The Art of War is thirteen short chapters, about six thousand Chinese characters in the original. Tradition gives it to Sun Tzu, a general in the service of the king of Wu in the fifth century BC. Scholars have argued for a long time about whether he existed and how much of the text is his. What is not in question is that Chinese soldiers were reading it by the third century AD and have gone on reading it ever since.

It is not a manual of drill or of battlefield tactics. Its argument is that war ruins the state that wages it, that no country has ever yet profited from a long one, and that the whole business therefore turns on knowing more than the other side and moving before he is ready. The most quoted line in it says that to win a hundred battles out of a hundred is not the height of skill; the height of skill is to break the enemy's resistance without fighting.

Two things about this edition need saying first. It is a modernization of an English translation and not a translation from the Chinese, because nobody working on this collection reads classical Chinese. What has been made readable here is the version Lionel Giles published in 1910. Giles was a sinologist at the British Museum and the son of the Giles of the Wade-Giles spelling system, which is why the names here are Ts'ao Kung and Tu Mu rather than Cao Cao and Du Mu.

The second thing is that more than half of this book was not written by Sun Tzu. Giles printed the classical commentary alongside the text, and that is the reason to read his edition rather than one of the many that give the bare verses. Eleven commentators appear. The earliest is Ts'ao Kung, who is Cao Cao, the warlord of the Three Kingdoms; he annotated the book around AD 200, and once sentenced himself to death under his own order against damaging standing crops. Tu Mu was a poet of the Tang. The others are officials and soldiers of later dynasties. They gloss Sun Tzu, they illustrate him out of the histories, and they contradict him and each other, sometimes rudely.

Three voices share the page, and the page labels them. A numbered verse is Sun Tzu. A paragraph beginning "Commentary" is somebody else, usually a commentator named in its first words, sometimes Giles explaining what the commentators are fighting about. A paragraph beginning "Footnote" is always Giles, who is an Edwardian scholar with opinions, a weakness for comparisons to Napoleon and Cromwell, and several of the best remarks in the book.
