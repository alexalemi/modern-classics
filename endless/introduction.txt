Vannevar Bush was an electrical engineer, a former dean of engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and, from 1939, president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington. In the Second World War he ran American civilian science: first the National Defense Research Committee and then, from 1941, the Office of Scientific Research and Development, which placed contracts with universities and companies for radar, the proximity fuze, the mass production of penicillin and much else, and which started the atomic bomb project before the Army took it over.

On 17 November 1944 President Roosevelt wrote to Bush asking four questions about the peace: how to release what the war had taught, how to carry on the fight against disease, how the government should aid research, and how to find and train scientific talent. Bush and his associates had a hand in drafting that letter. Roosevelt died in April 1945, and Bush sent this report to President Truman on 5 July 1945, with the reports of the four advisory committees he had appointed bound in after it.

Its argument is that new knowledge comes from basic research, done without thought of practical ends, mostly in universities; that industry will not pay for enough of it and the war has used up the stock; and that the government should therefore fund it steadily, through grants and contracts, without directing it. To do so Bush proposed one new agency, a National Research Foundation run by citizens chosen for their interest in research, which would also support military research, medical research and scholarships.

The report fixed the phrase "basic research" in American policy and is still cited whenever that funding is debated. Its plan took five years to become law. Congress argued over who should control the agency, Truman vetoed a bill in 1947 because its director would not have answered to the President, and when the National Science Foundation was created in 1950 it was smaller than Bush had asked for. By then military and medical research had gone to other agencies.

This is a restored edition of the report as printed by the Government Printing Office in July 1945. It includes Bush's letter of transmittal, Roosevelt's letter, the summary, the six chapters, the organization chart and the budget table, and Appendix 1, which lists the committees consulted. The committee reports, Appendices 2 to 5, some 150 further pages, are not included. No clean transcription existed. The text is voted from three scans, two of the 1945 printing and one of the National Science Foundation's 1960 reprint, with doubtful readings settled on the 1945 page images. The bold type marks the findings Bush set in bold. As a work of the United States government, the report is in the public domain.
