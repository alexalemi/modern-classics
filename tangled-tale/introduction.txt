Between 1880 and 1885 Lewis Carroll published ten "Knots" month by month in The Monthly Packet, a magazine for young women. Each is a short comic story with a mathematical problem buried in it. The intention, his preface says, was to smuggle the question in the way medicine used to be so deftly, and so ineffectually, hidden in the jam of our early childhood.

Two travellers in chain mail come down off a mountain arguing about how fast they walked going up. A tutor called Balbus sends his two pupils round a square to find lodgings, and then sets them a problem about the houses they found. An aunt drags her niece round the Metropolitan Railway betting on how many trains each of them will meet. Five sacks of money have to be valued, and they were weighed only two at a time. The Governor of Kgovjni gives a very small dinner party, to which he invites his father's brother-in-law, his brother's father-in-law, his father-in-law's brother and his brother-in-law's father.

The other half of the book is the half nobody quotes, and it is the better one. A month after each story Carroll printed his own review of the answers readers had sent in. He sorts his correspondents into a Class List under the pseudonyms they signed with — Balbus, Simple Susan, Sea Breeze, A Marlborough Boy, Galanthus Nivalis Major — praises the elegant ones, and takes the rest apart with enormous and affectionate relish. Bee's arithmetic is faulty. Old Hen merely scratched up the earth and pecked about. It is a tutor marking work by people he likes, delighted and merciless in the same paragraph, and there is no better record of what teaching mathematics actually sounds like.

The money is kept in pounds, shillings and pence, and explained once, in a bracketed note at the first Knot where it matters: twelve pence to the shilling, twenty shillings to the pound, twenty-one shillings to a guinea. Several of the answers come out as they do precisely because a shilling has twelve pence in it, so decimalising would quietly destroy the puzzles. The pseudonyms are left alone for a related reason — half the wit of the Answers is Carroll punning on them.

Every computed answer in the book was worked through again for this edition, which turned up two places where the surviving text had gone wrong rather than the author. The transcription of the Knot Six answers had corrupted two fractions, both to two-thirds, which made Carroll's own working contradict the order he prints two lines later. Restored, his arithmetic is exactly right. Three more of his mathematical books — Symbolic Logic, Pillow Problems and Euclid and His Modern Rivals — are in this collection.
