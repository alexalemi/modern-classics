ON THE GENEALOGY OF MORALS -- TEXT ANALYSIS AND TRANSLATION STRATEGY
Friedrich Nietzsche, Zur Genealogie der Moral. Eine Streitschrift
(Leipzig: C. G. Naumann, 1887; written in Sils-Maria in July 1887, in
about three weeks). Translated FROM THE GERMAN (the Colli-Montinari text
via Nietzsche Source, eKGWB/GM, which keeps the first edition's
spelling), with Horace B. Samuel's 1913 English (Standard Ebooks) as a
crib for sense only.

This is a scholarly modernization of a canonical philosophical text.
Every claim in it is Nietzsche's, rendered as his: measured, never
softened, never sensationalized, never annotated.


1. THE WORK AND ITS SHAPE

A "polemic" -- Nietzsche's word, on the title page -- in three essays,
written as a supplement to Beyond Good and Evil (1886) and the most
sustained argument he ever published. Its question: where did our
morality come from, and what is it worth? His answer is a history, told
as a psychologist and a philologist, of how three things came to be.

  PREFACE (8 sections). We seekers of knowledge do not know ourselves.
    How his doubts about the origin of good and evil began (as a boy of
    thirteen); his quarrel with Paul Rée and the "English" genealogists;
    the real question is the VALUE of morality, not just its history;
    how to read him (slowly, "like a cow": rumination).
  ESSAY ONE: "Good and Evil," "Good and Bad" (17). Two moralities.
    Noble morality says "we are good" first and "bad" (schlecht) is an
    afterthought: the common, the low. Slave morality starts from hatred
    of the masters -- "evil" (böse) -- and calls itself "good" only by
    contrast. The priestly revaluation; the Jews as the priestly people
    who began "the slave revolt in morality" (his thesis, restated from "Beyond Good and Evil");
    ressentiment that becomes creative; the lambs and the birds of prey;
    "the doer is a fiction added to the deed"; Rome against Judea; the
    closing Note proposing a prize essay on the history of moral concepts.
  ESSAY TWO: "Guilt," "Bad Conscience," and the Like (25). Man as the
    animal that is entitled to make promises; memory burned in by pain;
    guilt (Schuld) descends from debt (Schulden); punishment as the
    creditor's pleasure in cruelty; the origin of justice and the state
    (the "pack of blond beasts of prey" that founds it); bad conscience as
    instinct turned inward when man was shut inside society; the debt to
    the ancestors becomes the debt to God; Christianity's "stroke of
    genius" (God paying himself); the Greek gods as the opposite use of
    gods; the man of the future, who will redeem us from the ideal --
    "Zarathustra the godless".
  ESSAY THREE: What Is the Meaning of Ascetic Ideals? (28) Opens on a
    motto from Zarathustra; Nietzsche calls the essay a commentary on it.
    What ascetic ideals mean to artists (Wagner's Parsifal), philosophers
    (Schopenhauer, Kant's "disinterested" beauty), priests (the ascetic
    priest as physician and shepherd of the sick herd, who redirects
    ressentiment: "you yourself are to blame"), and to science (the will
    to truth is the last form of the ascetic ideal). "Man would rather
    will nothingness than not will" -- the essay's first and last line.

Structure (asserted by check.py against zeno.org's contents page and
Standard Ebooks' section ids, neither produced by our pipeline): Preface
8 + 17 + 25 + 28 = 78 numbered sections, plus the front note, the
Preface dateline, the Note closing Essay One, and the Essay Three motto.

2. WHOSE DIFFICULTY IS IT? (the governing decision)

Zarathustra's obstacle was a costume -- Common's King James grammar --
and removing it was the job. The Genealogy's crib screens differently:
calque 37.4, sentence 39.9 (45% of sentences over 35 words), archaism low.
Apply the whose-archaism-is-it test feature by feature:

  THE SENTENCE LENGTH IS NIETZSCHE'S. His German runs in long chains:
  clause, dash, parenthesis, colon, a second thought, an ironic aside,
  "…". That is his voice -- quick, combative, talking over the reader's
  objection -- and it is also the main thing that stops a fourteen-year-
  old. Ruling: SPLIT a long sentence where the meaning allows it, at its
  own joints (a colon, a semicolon, a dash that begins a new thought),
  and keep every clause. KEEP his rhetoric: the dash that throws in a
  second thought, the colon that delivers the punch, the rhetorical
  question, the "…" that trails off knowingly, the "— —" of a pause, the
  imagined interlocutor ("— 'But why are you still talking about
  nobler ideals?'"). A sentence he made short and hard stays short and
  hard ("Here is the workshop." "The deed is everything.").

  THE CALQUES ARE SAMUEL'S (and Levy's house style). They are what the
  calque score measured: "transvaluation" (Umwerthung), "resentment"
  for Ressentiment (which in English means mere annoyance), "the morality
  of morals" (Sittlichkeit der Sitte, a sense-destroying pun), "sacerdotal"
  for priesterlich, "the instinct of cruelty", "physiological failures",
  "pathos of distance", "ens", Latinate abstraction everywhere. Each of
  these gets a locked plain rendering in section 4, or, where the term is
  one the book TURNS ON, a locked term glossed once inside a sentence.

  THE PHILOLOGY IS THE ARGUMENT. Essay One argues from etymology
  (schlecht/schlicht, bonus/bellum, esthlos, malus/melas, the Gaelic
  "fin") and Essay Two from a pun (Schuld/Schulden). These cannot be
  "modernized away"; they are made readable (section 5).

THE HUME RULE APPLIES. Where the German is already clear, plain and
short, the English is clear, plain and short in the same shape. A file
in which every sentence has been rewritten has been damaged.


3. VOICE

A scholar who is spoiling for a fight. Four registers, often in one
section:
  - ARGUMENT: close, dialectical, every step visible; he anticipates
    objections and answers them.
  - POLEMIC: sarcasm, contempt, mock-politeness ("I beg your pardon"),
    name-calling of the "English psychologists", Dühring, the
    antisemites, the "beautiful souls". Sharp, funny, never shrill.
  - PROPHECY and lyric: the "great health", the man of the future, the
    "Let us take a look" passages, the end of Essay Two. Elevated, but in
    plain words.
  - DRAMA: he stages scenes -- the workshop where ideals are made (I 14,
    a dialogue with "Mr. Rash-and-Curious"), the lambs and the birds of
    prey, the creditor at the debtor's body, the priest and his sick
    flock.
He addresses "us", "we seekers of knowledge", "my readers", "you";
keep the second person where he uses it, and his "we".
He loves a coinage and a scare-quote: render the coinage with a
hyphenated or plain English coinage ("the will-to-nothingness"), and
keep his quotation marks around words he is holding at arm's length
(the "good", "free will", "objectivity").
Contractions: only in imagined voices and quoted sneers, never in his
argument. American spelling.


4. LOCKED VOCABULARY (and the reason for each)

CROSS-BOOK LOCKS, shared with zarathustra/ (same author, same edition):
  Übermensch -> overman (no instance in GM; lock carried for any echo)
  Wille zur Macht -> will to power
  der Erkennende / wir Erkennenden -> seeker(s) of knowledge
    ("we seekers of knowledge", Preface 1 and III 12, 24)
  Erkenntniss -> knowledge
  böse / schlecht -> evil / bad (zarathustra 056's lock; here it is the
    whole of Essay One -- see below)
  Pöbel -> the mob; Gesindel -> the rabble (distinct)
  Heerde -> the herd; Heerden-Thier -> herd animal
  Mitleid -> pity (never compassion: it praises what he attacks)
  Ekel -> disgust; der Ekel am Menschen -> disgust with man
  Weib -> woman; Weibchen / Weiblein -> his diminutive stays
    ("little woman", "little hysterical cultured woman")
  der Mensch -> man (the species, in the argument: "man is the sick
    animal"); people / human beings / humanity as the sentence needs
  Geist -> spirit; geistig -> spiritual, or "of the mind" where plainly
    intellect (geistigste Rache -> "the most spiritual revenge")
  heilig -> holy; Priester -> priest; Wahrheit -> truth
  Zarathustra's own lines quoted here use zarathustra's renderings
    (the Essay III motto; "Zarathustra the godless")

GOOD / BAD / EVIL -- the distinction the first essay turns on.
  gut -> good. schlecht -> bad. böse -> evil. NEVER interchange them,
  never use "wicked" or "base" for either. "Gut und Schlecht" -> "good
  and bad" (the noble pair); "Gut und Böse" -> "good and evil" (the
  slave pair). When he puts the words in quotes, keep the quotes.
  "Jenseits von Gut und Böse" -> "Beyond Good and Evil", which
  "at least does NOT mean 'Beyond Good and Bad'" (I 17).
  schlimm -> bad / harmful ("das Schlimme"), but where it could be
  mistaken for schlecht inside Essay One, prefer "harmful", "ill".
  schlecht / schlicht (I 4): "schlecht" once meant "schlicht" -- plain,
  simple, common. Keep both German words in this one passage and gloss
  them in the sentence (section 5).

MASTERS AND SLAVES.
  Herren-Moral -> master morality; Sklaven-Moral -> slave morality
  der Sklavenaufstand in der Moral -> the slave revolt in morality
    (his phrase, emphasised in I 7; locked, never "slave revolt in morals")
  vornehm -> noble (the "good = noble = powerful" equation of I 7
    turns on it; "aristocratic" only where he says aristokratisch)
  Vornehmheit -> nobility; die Vornehmen -> the noble
  edel -> noble too (where he uses it of character), wohlgeboren ->
    well-born; gemein -> common; niedrig -> low; Plebejer -> plebeian
  Herr -> master; Knecht -> servant; Sklave -> slave
  Pathos der Distanz -> "pathos of distance" (his term; glossed ONCE in
    I 2 inside the sentence -- the lasting feeling of height and
    difference that the noble have toward those below)
  Rangordnung -> order of rank
  die Herrschenden -> the rulers; die Ohnmächtigen -> the powerless

RESSENTIMENT -- kept in French, as he kept it.
  Nietzsche wrote the French word in a German book, on purpose: no
  German word said what he meant. English "resentment" means everyday
  annoyance and loses the technical sense (the impotent's lasting,
  inward, revengeful hatred, which in the end CREATES values). So:
  "ressentiment", lowercase, roman (not italic: his emphasis gets the
  asterisks), glossed ONCE inside the sentence at its first real use
  (I 10) -- e.g. "ressentiment -- the impotent, festering hatred of
  people who cannot strike back". "Mensch des Ressentiment" -> "the man
  of ressentiment". "resentment" is banned (check.py).

THE PRIESTLY REVALUATION.
  Umwerthung (aller Werthe) -> revaluation (of all values). Not Levy's
  "transvaluation", a word no reader uses.
  Werth -> value; Werthschätzung / Werthung -> valuation, evaluation
  Werthe schaffen -> create values
  Sittlichkeit der Sitte -> "the morality of custom" (his term from
  Daybreak; glossed once at its first use, Preface 4 (000): the long prehistoric age when being
  moral simply meant obeying custom). Not "morality of morals".
  das asketische Ideal -> the ascetic ideal; asketische Ideale ->
  ascetic ideals; der asketische Priester -> the ascetic priest
  Askese -> asceticism; Asket -> ascetic

GUILT AND DEBT -- the pun is the argument.
  German Schuld means BOTH "guilt" and "debt"; Schulden is "debts";
  schuldig "guilty" and "owing". English has no single word. Ruling:
  - Schuld (moral) -> guilt; Schulden / Schuld (money) -> debt(s);
    Schuldner -> debtor; Gläubiger -> creditor.
  - II 4, where he states the thesis ("that the central moral concept
    Schuld, 'guilt', took its origin from the very material concept
    Schulden, 'debts'"): give BOTH German words, once, in the sentence,
    and say plainly that in German they are one word. That one sentence
    makes the rest of the essay readable. Nowhere else does the German
    appear.
  - Where the argument depends on the double sense (II 4-8, 19-22),
    let the English carry it with "owe / owing / debt / guilt" side by
    side ("the feeling of guilt, of owing"). Never a footnote.
  - II 21 "Schuld und Pflicht" -> guilt and duty; "Verschuldung" ->
    indebtedness; "Schuldgefühl" -> the feeling of guilt.
  III 15 "Ich leide: daran muss irgend Jemand schuld sein" -> "I am
    suffering: someone must be to blame for this" ... "you yourself are
    to blame for yourself!" (schuld sein = to be to blame).

CONSCIENCE AND MEMORY.
  das schlechte Gewissen -> the bad conscience (always; never "guilty
  conscience": that collapses the very thing he is separating from guilt)
  Gewissen -> conscience; Gewissensbiss -> the sting of conscience
  Verinnerlichung -> internalization ("what I call the internalization
  of man", II 16)
  Gedächtniss -> memory; Vergesslichkeit -> forgetfulness (active!)
  Mnemotechnik -> the technique of memory (mnemonics)
  das souveraine Individuum -> the sovereign individual
  versprechen dürfen -> to be entitled to make promises ("an animal
  that is entitled to make promises")
  Verantwortlichkeit -> responsibility
  Strafe -> punishment; Vergeltung -> retribution; Rache -> revenge
  Grausamkeit -> cruelty; Gerechtigkeit -> justice; Gnade -> mercy
  (II 10's "self-overcoming of justice" is mercy, Gnade).
  Selbstaufhebung -> self-overcoming (II 10, III 27) -- WHY: he glosses
  it himself in III 27 as "Selbstüberwindung", and "self-cancellation"
  loses the law of life he means; keep the pair visible in III 27 as
  "self-overcoming" for Selbstüberwindung and "an act of self-
  cancellation" for Selbstaufhebung there only.

THE BLOND BEAST.
  die blonde Bestie -> the blond beast (I 11, three times; II 17 "a
  pack of blond beasts of prey"). Locked, never softened, never
  explained in the text. See section 7.
  Raubthier -> beast of prey; Raubvogel -> bird of prey; Lamm -> lamb
  Herren-Rasse -> master race (II 17: his phrase, kept; see section 7)
  Rasse -> race (in his 19th-century sense: a stock, a people, a class
  of descent; he says "races" of the noble Romans, Arabs, Japanese)

THE ASCETIC PRIEST AND THE SICK.
  der Kranke / die Kranken -> the sick; Krankheit -> sickness
  krankhaft -> morbid; Verunglückte (physiologisch) -> the failures, the
  physiologically botched ("those who turned out badly" -- plain words,
  not Samuel's "physiological failures")
  Wohlgerathenheit / die Wohlgerathenen -> those who turned out well
  Hirt -> shepherd; Heerde -> herd
  Heiland -> Savior (III 9, 14, 15, 17: the German word means both
    healer and savior, and he plays on the doctor-priest; carry it in
    the sentence at its first use in III 14: "the 'Savior,' the healer")
  Sünde -> sin; Sünder -> sinner; Erlösung -> redemption
  der Wille zur Wahrheit -> the will to truth
  Wissenschaft -> science, in his broad sense (all disciplined inquiry,
    scholarship included); "scholarship" where he means philology
  der freie Geist -> the free spirit; Freigeist -> free spirit (lowercase)
  das Nichts -> nothingness ("will nothingness")
  der Wille zum Nichts -> the will to nothingness
  horror vacui -> horror vacui, glossed in the sentence ("its horror of
    a vacuum") -- III 1, once

OTHER FIXED TERMS.
  Streitschrift -> polemic. Abhandlung -> essay. Vorrede -> preface.
  Genealogie -> genealogy. Herkunft -> origin / descent; Ursprung ->
  origin (he contrasts neither; "origin" is fine for both).
  Moral -> morality (the system); moralisch -> moral
  Vorurtheil -> prejudice. Instinkt -> instinct. Affekt -> affect,
  passion, emotion (by context; "affect" only in a list of technical
  psychology). Lust / Unlust -> pleasure / displeasure.
  "Englische Psychologen" -> "the English psychologists" (his target;
  he includes the German Paul Rée among them). Keep the label.
  der Täter / das Thun -> the doer / the deed (I 13; "the doer is merely
  a fiction added to the deed -- the deed is everything").
  das "Ding an sich" -> the "thing in itself"
  Spelling: AMERICAN (honor, color, skeptic).
  Book titles: in double quotes, as he gives them ("Beyond Good and
  Evil", "Daybreak", "The Gay Science", "Human, All Too Human", "The
  Wanderer and His Shadow"), never italics (the asterisks are his
  emphasis only).

Rule of use: a locked term is locked in meaning AND in form; once the
ledger marks a gloss spent, it is never re-glossed.


5. FOREIGN LANGUAGES, THE PHILOLOGY, CITATIONS

The reader is fourteen, reads no Latin or Greek, and has no first
edition of Nietzsche's earlier books. Nietzsche's readers had all three.
  GREEK (I 5, I 10, II 23's Homer, III 22, III 26 Anacreon): Greek
  script is TRANSLITERATED (esthlos, kakos, deilos, agathos, melas,
  gennaios, rhathymia, oizyros, anolbos, tlemon, dystychein, xymphora,
  eu prattein, chasm' odonton), in roman type, each followed by its
  meaning in the sentence. Reasons: a fourteen-year-old cannot sound
  out Greek letters, and Alex's e-reader fonts lack the script (the
  CrossPoint note). check.py bans Greek characters. Where Nietzsche's
  point is the etymology, make the point visible ("esthlos, which at
  root means one who IS, who is real, who is true").
  GERMAN WORDS THE ARGUMENT TURNS ON: kept, once, inside the sentence,
  with the English beside them: schlecht / schlicht (I 4), Schuld /
  Schulden (II 4), Heiland (III 14), Gut / Böse / Schlecht in the essay
  titles only where he discusses the words as words. Otherwise
  everything is English.
  LATIN, FRENCH, ITALIAN: TRANSLATED inside the sentence, every time.
  Tiny tags that English readers know may stay, with the meaning in the
  same sentence (pathos of distance is English already; "par
  excellence"; "fiat justitia"). Specifically: bonus / bellum / duonus
  (I 5) keep the Latin words because they ARE the argument; malus /
  melas likewise; "hic niger est" (Horace: "that man is black-hearted
  -- beware of him") translated in the sentence; I 15's long Latin
  passage from Tertullian (De spectaculis 29-30) and the line from
  Thomas Aquinas -> ENGLISH, complete, every clause, inside Nietzsche's
  quotation marks, with "Tertullian writes, in Latin:" or similar
  if needed; Nietzsche's own German parenthesis inside the Latin (that
  from here on Tertullian means the Jews, with the Talmudic name for
  Jesus's mother) stays as his aside. II 5's Twelve Tables "si plus
  minusve secuerunt, ne fraude esto" -> English. III 7 "pereat mundus,
  fiat philosophia, fiat philosophus, fiam!" -> English ("let the world
  perish, but let there be philosophy, the philosopher -- ME!"). III 9
  "nitimur in vetitum" (Ovid) -> English ("we strive for the
  forbidden"). Stendhal's "une promesse de bonheur" (III 6) -> "a
  promise of happiness"; "le désintéressement" -> "disinterestedness".
  Pascal's "il faut s'abêtir" (III 17) -> "one must make oneself
  stupid". "morbidezza" (III 1) -> "a little languid softness". The
  rule is the Verne rule: the reader gets the meaning in the sentence,
  never a footnote, never untranslated.
  CITATIONS OF HIS OWN BOOKS: Nietzsche cites earlier books by the page
  of their first editions ("Jenseits von Gut und Böse" p. 118;
  Morgenröthe S. 17 ff.). KEEP each citation, with its numbers, as
  given -- ("Beyond Good and Evil," p. 118) -- so that nothing is lost
  and a later sweep can convert them to section numbers if Alex
  decides so (flagged; the section equivalents are NOT to be supplied
  from memory). check.py's numeral warning watches them.
  "vergleiche oben Seite 30" (II 11) points back into this book, to
  I 14, where the men of ressentiment call their revenge "the triumph
  of justice" (checked at setup). Render: "(compare Essay One, section
  14, above)".

7. SENSITIVE CONTENT -- render HIS argument as his

The Genealogy is the Nietzsche text most often conscripted: by the
Nazis (the "blond beast", "master race", the Jews and the slave revolt)
and, in the other direction, softened by admirers. This edition does
neither. Each ruling is why the agent needs to add, cut, or note
NOTHING. The context belongs in the editor's introduction, never in the
text.

  THE BLOND BEAST (I 11, II 17). Nietzsche's image is the LION -- the
  "blond" beast of prey -- for the predatory freedom of every noble
  caste, and he names them himself in the same sentence: "the Roman,
  Arabian, Germanic, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, Scandinavian
  Vikings". It is not a racial category in his text; it is a metaphor
  across peoples, and in the same section he says that between the old
  Germanic tribes and "us Germans" there is "hardly a conceptual, let
  alone a blood relationship". Render every word of it, including the
  admiration and the horror he expresses, at full strength. Never gloss
  it, never apologize for it, never sharpen it.
  "MASTER RACE" (II 17, Herren-Rasse) and "ARYAN" (I 5, I 11, III 26):
  his 1880s philological vocabulary; Herren-Rasse in II 17 is the
  conquering caste that founds the state ("a pack of blond beasts of
  prey, a conquering master race"). Kept literally, as he wrote it,
  because the later meaning is exactly what a reader must be able to
  check against the text. I 5's colour etymology (malus/melas, the
  dark-haired pre-Aryan population, the "blond, conquering race",
  Virchow's map, the Celts as blond) is 19th-century philology and
  race-anthropology; render it as his argument, plainly, with his
  own hedges ("perhaps", "at least the Gaelic offered me").
  THE JEWS (I 7, I 8, I 9, I 16; III 22): his thesis that the slave
  revolt in morality begins with the Jews, "that priestly people", is
  a claim about the history of VALUES, stated with his mixture of
  hostility and awe (I 7: "the truly great haters in world history
  have always been priests"; I 16: "the priestly people of ressentiment
  par excellence, in whom there dwelt an unequaled genius for popular
  morality" -- set beside "similarly gifted peoples, the Chinese or the
  Germans", to feel "what is of the first rank and what of the fifth"). Render it exactly, in a measured voice, with his
  emphasis; do not soften "hatred" or "revenge", do not heighten them.
  "Israel" stays "Israel"; "Judäa" -> Judea; "Rom gegen Judäa, Judäa
  gegen Rom" -> "Rome against Judea, Judea against Rome" (pinned).
  III 22's contempt for the New Testament next to his praise of the
  Old Testament stays exactly.
  HIS ANTI-ANTISEMITISM (II 11, III 14, III 26): his contempt for the
  antisemites of his day -- II 11 "this plant blooms most beautifully
  today among anarchists and antisemites"; III 14 Dühring, "that Berlin
  apostle of revenge ... the most indecent and repulsive moral bigmouth
  in Germany today", "even among his own kind, the antisemites"; III 26 "these latest speculators in
  idealism, the antisemites, who roll their eyes in a Christian-Aryan-
  bourgeois manner" -- is AS STRONG as anything in the book and is
  rendered at the same full strength. Neither side of his position is
  allowed to soften the other.
  II 7 "NEGER". Nietzsche cites doctors who treated "der Neger" (taken,
  he says, "as representatives of prehistoric man") for internal
  inflammations, to argue that pain hurt less in earlier times.
  Ruling (the sensitive-content test: does the reader lose information
  or gain a false one?): the CLAIM stays exactly, with his parenthesis;
  the noun is rendered "Black Africans", the ordinary descriptive noun
  of today, as "Neger" was the ordinary descriptive noun of 1887 German
  ethnography. The English period word would add an insult the
  sentence does not make; the modern noun hides nothing he said.

  WOMEN: II 7's "hysterisches Bildungs-Weibchen" (his diminutive:
  "a single hysterical little cultured woman"), III 1's "the angelic
  look of a pretty, fat animal", III 14's "the sick woman", III 8 the
  mother instinct, the Zarathustra motto: his, set down plainly, the
  diminutives kept (the mulierculis precedent).
  CRUELTY AND PUNISHMENT (II 3, 5, 6, 13): the catalogue of old German

  oil, flaying, cutting flesh from the breast), the creditor's right to

9. RATIO AND CHECKS

English/German whitespace-word ratio: Samuel's literal crib runs
1.08-1.19 per file (1.13 overall). A complete modern rendering should
land about 1.10-1.28 (zarathustra ran 1.17 on a 1.09 crib): splitting
sentences, translating the Latin (I 15 alone is ~400 Latin words), and
the in-sentence glosses all add words. check.py FAILS outside 0.95-1.45
and WARNS outside 1.05-1.32. Run verify as
  python3 verify.py genealogy --min-ratio 0.95 --max-ratio 1.45
Expected English total: ~51,000-58,000 words.

check.py (source): structure against zeno and SE; manifest form;
the zeno word-for-word witness.
check.py --modern (per file): exact heading and part marker; the
section-numeral sequence; emphasis-span parity; verse-block parity;
renderability (no paragraph the page builder would set as a heading);
straight quotes; no "--", "_", brackets, ALL-CAPS lines or notes;
banned forms (resentment, transvaluation, morality of morals, Greek
script, German labels, archaisms); the LOCKS (pinned renderings);
German numerals absent from the English (warnings); ratio.

RENDERABILITY: assemble.is_subheading sets any short one-line
paragraph with mostly capitalized words and no closing full stop as a
heading. Nietzsche has very few one-line paragraphs, but the dateline,
the motto attribution and a book title alone on a line are all
candidates -- the locks above end on a full stop for that reason.
