This is the course Michael Faraday gave to children at the Royal Institution over Christmas 1859, the winter before the one that produced The Chemical History of a Candle. He was sixty-eight. The lectures had been postponed twice because he was ill, and he opens by saying so: he may manage only a few words, he will substitute experiments for talk, and he claims as usual the right to speak to young people as one of them. Then he lectures anyway, for six nights. The apology is the first thing in the book and is printed here exactly as he made it.

Where the Candle takes a single object and draws the whole of chemistry out of it, this course does the reverse. It takes the powers of matter one at a time — gravitation, cohesion, chemical affinity, heat, magnetism, electricity — and drives them all toward the title of the last lecture, the correlation of the physical forces. The claim is that these are not six powers but one, each convertible into the others. Chemical action carried along a wire comes out as light. A coil of copper becomes a magnet. A moving magnet makes a spark. Faraday had discovered that last effect himself in 1831, and in the fifth lecture he is showing a room of children the thing he found.

The experiments are the argument, and they are all on the bench: a stick of shellac rubbed on cloth dragging a sheet of paper about, a pail of water swung from the edge of a table, a bubble of air holding up half a hundredweight of iron, a rod of iron heated red at one end so that it can be twisted with pliers while the cold end will not bend. His assistant, Mr. Anderson, hands him things and is named. The stage directions the shorthand writer took down are kept in square brackets where they fall.

The book ends with something that is not a Christmas lecture. The address on lighthouse illumination was delivered to adults on 9 March 1860, by Faraday in his capacity as scientific adviser to Trinity House, and it is about putting the electric light into lighthouses. Its register is plainer and more official than the lectures, and it has been kept that way rather than warmed up to match them.

As with the Candle, the lectures were taken down and edited by William Crookes, and his explanatory notes are printed beside the passages they explain, marked as editor's notes and in his voice rather than Faraday's. All fifty-nine of the original figures are here; Victorian printers put several on one woodblock, so a single plate often carries three. Lecture Six finishes on two lines of As You Like It, which are the last words Faraday says to the children. Other volumes in the Royal Institution series are in this collection, the Candle among them.
