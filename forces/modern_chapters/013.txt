Lighthouse Illumination: The Electric Light

(Part 1 of 2)

Delivered before the Royal Institution on Friday, 9 March 1860.

There is no part of my life which gives me more delight than my connection with the Trinity House. The business of nations joining together to guide the mariner across the sea is of great interest to everyone, and it is infinitely more so to those concerned in carrying the work out. It has certainly astonished me, since I became connected with the Trinity House, to see how beautifully and how wonderfully the desire to do good shines out among the nations at large.

You will not regret having come here tonight if you follow me through the various attempts that have been made to achieve this great object of guiding all people safely across the dark and dreary waste of waters. It is wonderful to think how eagerly the various public bodies press for improvement — the Trinity House in this country, and commissions in France and other nations. And as the improvements go forward, we come to know such curious difficulties, and such odd ways of getting over them, as are not easily imagined.

I must ask you this evening to follow me from the simplest possible way of giving a signal by light to people at a distance, through to the methods we have arrived at today; and to consider the difficulties that arise in carrying these improvements out to a practical result, and the extraordinary care those who have to judge such matters must take to guard against the too hasty adoption of some supposed improvement — which, as has happened in a few cases, does harm instead of good.

If I try to make you understand these things partly by old models and partly by the ones we have here, it is only so that I may better illustrate what I look forward to as the higher method of lighting: by means of the electric lamp and the limelight.

[Figure 55: The rays of a single flame, drawn as leaving the centre of a sphere and going out equally in every direction to an infinite distance — the simplest possible signal light, and the most wasteful.]

There is nothing simpler than a candle set in a cottage window to guide a husband home. But when we want to make a similar guide on a large scale — not merely across a river or a moor, but across great expanses of sea — how can we make the signal, using only a candle?

I have shown in this diagram (Figure 55) what we may imagine to be the rays of a candle, or of any other source of light, going out from the centre of a sphere in all directions to infinite distances. After this simple kind of light had been used for some time, and had been found liable to be obscured by fog, or distance, or other circumstances, there came the attempt to make larger lights by means of fires. And after that a very important refinement was introduced in the way the light was handled: the principle of reflection.

For understand this — which is not known to everybody, and not known to many who ought to know it — that when we take a source of light, a single candle for instance, giving off a certain quantity of light, we can by no means increase that light. We can make arrangements around and about the light, as you see here, but we cannot increase the quantity of it. The utmost I can do is to direct the light the lamp gives me, by taking a certain portion of the rays going off on one side and reflecting them into the course of the rays that issue in the opposite direction.

First of all, let us consider how we may gather in the rays passing off from this candle. You will easily see that if I could take the half of the rays going out on one side and send them by some contrivance over to the other side, I should gain an advantage in light on the side I directed them to. This is done in a beautiful way by the parabolic mirror, by means of which I gather in all the rays that fall within it — upwards, downwards, sideways, anywhere within its sphere of action. They are all picked up and sent forward. So you see what a beautiful and important invention the parabolic reflector is for throwing the rays of light forward.

[Figure 56: A lens set in front of a candle. Instead of the light scattering in every direction, the rays are bent together and thrown as a bright patch onto the sheet of paper beyond.]

Before I go further into the subject of reflection, let me point out another way of dealing with the direction of light. Here is a candle, and I can use the principle of refraction to bend and direct its rays; for if I want to increase the light in any one direction, I must either take a reflector or use refraction.

I will put this lens (Figure 56) in front of the candle, and you will easily see that by its means I can throw a great light onto that sheet of paper. That is to say, instead of the light being thrown all about, it is refracted and concentrated onto the paper. So here is another means of bending light and sending it in one direction. And you see above a still better arrangement for the same purpose, one which reaches, I may say, the maximum of what can be done in directing light this way.

You know that without that arrangement of glass the light would be scattered in all directions; but with the lens there, all the light passing through it is thrown into parallel beams and cast horizontally along. There is consequently no loss of light: the beam goes forward at the same width, and will go on for five or ten miles, or as long as the imperfection of the atmosphere does not absorb it. And see what a glorious power it is, to be able to turn what was darkness on that paper a moment ago into brilliant light.

Whenever we have refraction of this sort, we are open to an evil that follows from the necessary imperfections in the shape of the lens. Dr. Tyndall will take this lens and show you, even in this small and well-made apparatus, the evil of spherical aberration, which we have to fight against.

This can be shown with the electric lamp. If you look at the screen you will see an image of the carbon points produced by this lens. That image is made by the rays passing through the middle of the lens, a piece of card with a hole in the centre being placed in front. But if, keeping the rest of the apparatus in the same position, I change that card for another which lets the rays pass only through the edge of the lens, you can see how much poorer the image is. To get it distinct I have to bring the screen much nearer the lamp. And so, if I take the card away altogether and let the light pass through every part of the lens, we cannot get a perfect image, because the different parts of the lens cannot act together.

This spherical aberration, then, is what we try to avoid by building up compound lenses in the way shown here. Look at this beautiful apparatus — is it not a most charming piece of workmanship? Buffon first, and Fresnel afterwards, built up lenses of this kind, ring within ring, each at its proper adjustment, to compensate for the effects of spherical aberration.

The ring round that centre lens is ground so as to remove what would otherwise cause spherical aberration; and the next ring being corrected in the same way, you will notice, if you look at the disc of light the apparatus upstairs throws, that there is nothing like the aberration there would have been if it were one great bull's-eye.

Here is one of Fresnel's lamps of the fourth order, made in that way (Figure 57). Observe the fine effect got from these different lenses as you see them revolving in front of you; and understand that all this upper part is made to form part of the lens, each prism throwing its rays in to increase the effect. And although you may think it imperfect, because if you happen to be sitting below or above the horizontal line you see little or none of the light, you must bear in mind that we want the rays to go in a straight line to the horizon.

So all that building up of rings of glass is for the purpose of producing one fine and glorious lens of large size, to send the rays all in one direction. Here is another apparatus used to pull the rays down into a horizontal sheet of light, so that the mariner may see it as a constant and uniform fixed light. The other lamp is a revolving one, and its light is seen only at certain moments as the lenses come round; and those are the points which make them valuable in their application.

[Figure 57: One of Fresnel's lamps of the fourth order. The great lens is built up of ring within ring, each ground to its own curve to correct the aberration, with prisms above bending their rays down into the beam.]

There are various orders and sizes of lights in lighthouses, meant to shine for twenty or thirty miles over the sea and to give whatever indications are wanted of them. But suppose we want more effect than these means can give: how are we to get more light? Here comes the difficulty. We cannot get more light, because we are limited by the condition of the burner.

In any of these cases, if the spreading of the ray — the divergence, as it is called — is not restrained, the light soon fails from weakness; and if it does not diverge at all, the light is made so narrow that perhaps only one person in a hundred can see it at any one time. The South Foreland lighthouse is, I think, three or four hundred feet above the level of the sea, and so a certain divergence of the beam is necessary for it to shine along the sea to the horizon.

I have drawn two wedges here. One has an angle of 15 degrees and shows the way the light opens out from this reflector, seen at half a mile's distance or more; the other has an angle of 6 degrees, which is Fresnel's beautiful angle. When the angle is less than 6 degrees the mariner cannot be quite sure of seeing the light — he may be below it or above it. In practice it is found that we cannot have an angle larger than 15 degrees or smaller than 6.

To get more light, then, we must have more burning: more cotton, more oil. But there are already four wicks in that lamp, set in concentric rings one within another, and we cannot increase them much further, because of the divergence a larger flame would cause — and the more the divergence, the more the light is spread out and lost. We are therefore restricted, by the conditions of the light and the apparatus, to a lamp of a certain size.

At Teignmouth some of the revolving lights have ten lamps and reflectors, all throwing their light forward at once. But even with ten lamps and reflectors we do not get light enough. What we want is a means of getting a more intense light in the space of a candle — not merely candle piled on candle, but a greater amount of light concentrated into the space of one. And it is here that the electric light becomes so valuable.
