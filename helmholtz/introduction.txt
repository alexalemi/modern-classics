Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894) was a German physician who became one of the great physicists of his century. He measured the speed of the nerve impulse, invented the ophthalmoscope, and stated the conservation of energy in its general form. On the Sensations of Tone was the fruit of eight years' work at Heidelberg, where he was professor of physiology, and it was first published in German in 1863. Its question is an old one: why do some combinations of notes sound smooth and others rough? Helmholtz answered it with physics and with the anatomy of the ear, and then followed the answer into the history of scales and harmony.

The book is in three Parts. The first shows that a musical tone is a compound of simple tones, its upper partials, and that the quality of a voice or an instrument depends on which partials are present and how strong they are. Much of the evidence comes from resonators, hollow vessels he designed to pick a single partial out of a sound. The second Part explains dissonance as beating between partials, and combinational tones as tones the ear hears that no instrument sounds. The third turns to music itself: the Greek modes, the church modes, the major and minor keys, and the laws of chords and part-writing. Nineteen appendices carry the mathematics and the apparatus.

The translator, Alexander J. Ellis (1814–1890), was an English mathematician and a student of pronunciation. His first translation appeared in 1875; this is his second edition of 1885, conformed to the fourth German edition. Ellis added a great deal, always inside square brackets, and wrote a twentieth appendix of his own on temperament, the history of musical pitch, the scales of other nations, and recent work on vowels. In his notes he measures intervals in cents, a unit he had devised himself.

A few things will read oddly. Pitch is given as vibrations in a second. Note names carry marks: c', c'' for higher octaves, and a small figure below a letter, as e₁, for a note a comma lower than the one reached by Fifths. Ellis cites his own pages by page and quarter, as "see p. 77c", the quarters being marked in his margins.

This is a restored edition, and the words are Helmholtz's and Ellis's, unchanged. There was no usable transcription, so every page was read from a scan of the 1895 reprint and then compared word by word with two more scans. The formulas are typeset, the tables are set as tables, and every figure and passage of music has been cut from the page and described. Each printed page and quarter is marked by a small number in the text, and Ellis's lettered references are links to them. The index is omitted. The edition also adds an ebook and this introduction.
