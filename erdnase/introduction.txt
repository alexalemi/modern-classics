In 1902 a book appeared, published by its author, with a plain green cover stamped The Expert at the Card Table. Its title page called it something longer, Artifice, Ruse and Subterfuge at the Card Table, and promised to describe "every known expedient, manoeuvre and stratagem of the expert card handler." The author signed himself S. W. Erdnase. Nobody has ever established who he was. The name read backwards is E. S. Andrews, which is the start of every theory and the end of none.

The book is in two halves, and the order matters. The first and longer half, Card Table Artifice, is a manual of cheating at cards: shuffles that only look like shuffles, cuts that leave the deck exactly as it was, dealing the second card or the bottom one while seeming to deal the top, stacking a hand during an ordinary shuffle, palming, and the player who works alone without a partner. The second half, Legerdemain, turns the same skills to conjuring, where the audience knows it is being fooled and enjoys it, and ends with a set of card tricks. The author says in his preface that the book may caution the unwary and inspire the crafty, and that whatever it does, if it sells it will have served his main purpose, because he needs the money.

It is written by someone who clearly did these things with his own hands. The descriptions go finger by finger, and the second half assumes the first: where every conjurer tells the beginner to start with the shift, he tells him to start with the blind shuffles of the card table. The prose is dry, exact and occasionally very funny, and it is not old-fashioned enough to need a retelling. The vocabulary he uses for a break, a jog, a crimp or a stock is still the vocabulary of card magic.

The book has never gone out of print, and card magicians still learn from it. Three of its shifts carry his name or his initials.

This is a restored edition, and the words are the author's. No transcription of the book could be found, so the text was read page by page from a photographic facsimile of the first edition and checked against the scan's OCR. His spellings stand, including "slights," "manoeuvers" and "Penuckle," and so do the printer's few slips. Each of the one hundred and one drawings from life by M. D. Smith has its printed number and a new description for readers who cannot see it. The copyright notice printed under many of them is not repeated.
