On the Tree on the Mountainside

Zarathustra’s eye had seen that a certain young man was avoiding him. And as he walked alone one evening through the mountains that surround the town called “the Motley Cow”: look, as he walked he came upon this young man, sitting leaning against a tree and gazing with tired eyes into the valley. Zarathustra took hold of the tree the young man was sitting by, and spoke like this:

If I wanted to shake this tree here with my hands, I would not be able to.

But the wind, which we do not see, torments it and bends it wherever it likes. We are bent and tormented worst of all by invisible hands.

At that the young man stood up in dismay and said: “I hear Zarathustra, and I was just now thinking of him.” Zarathustra replied:

“Why does that frighten you? — But it is with man as it is with the tree.

The more he wants to rise up into the height and the light, the more strongly his roots strive earthward, downward, into the dark, the deep — into evil.”

“Yes, into evil!” cried the young man. “How is it possible that you have discovered my soul?”

Zarathustra smiled and said: “Many a soul will never be discovered, unless it is first invented.” “Yes, into evil!” cried the young man once more.

“You spoke the truth, Zarathustra. I no longer trust myself since I have wanted to rise into the heights, and no one trusts me anymore — how does that happen?

I change too fast: my today refutes my yesterday. When I climb, I often skip the steps — and no step forgives me for that.

When I am up there, I always find myself alone. No one speaks to me; the frost of solitude makes me shiver. What do I even want in the heights?

My contempt and my longing grow together; the higher I climb, the more I despise the one who climbs. What does he even want in the heights?

How ashamed I am of my climbing and stumbling! How I mock my own violent panting! How I hate the one who flies! How tired I am in the heights!”

Here the young man fell silent. And Zarathustra looked at the tree beside which they stood, and spoke like this:

This tree stands alone here on the mountainside; it has grown high up, far above man and beast.

And if it wanted to speak, it would have no one who could understand it: so high has it grown.

Now it waits and waits — what is it waiting for? It lives too close to the seat of the clouds: perhaps it is waiting for the first lightning?

When Zarathustra had said this, the young man cried out with violent gestures: “Yes, Zarathustra, you speak the truth. It was my going under I longed for when I wanted to rise into the heights, and you are the lightning I was waiting for! Look, what am I anymore, since you appeared among us? It is *envy* of you that has destroyed me!” — So spoke the young man, and wept bitterly. But Zarathustra put his arm around him and led him away with him.

And when they had walked together for a while, Zarathustra began to speak like this:

It tears my heart. Better than your words can say it, your eye tells me all your danger.

You are not free yet; you are still *searching* for freedom. Your searching has kept you up all night and made you too wakeful.

You want to go up into the free heights; your soul thirsts for the stars. But your wicked instincts thirst for freedom too.

Your wild dogs want to be free; they bark for joy in their cellar when your spirit sets out to unlock every prison.

To me you are still a prisoner who dreams up freedom for himself: ah, the soul of such prisoners grows shrewd, but also cunning and bad.

The one who has freed his spirit still has to purify himself. Much prison and mold is still left in him: his eye still has to become pure.

Yes, I know your danger. But by my love and my hope I implore you: do not throw away your love and your hope!

You still feel noble, and the others still feel that you are noble too, the ones who resent you and send you evil looks. Know that a noble man stands in everyone’s way.

A noble man stands in the way of the good as well: and even when they call him a good man, they mean by it to push him aside.

The noble man wants to create what is new, and a new virtue. The good man wants what is old, and wants the old to be preserved.

But the danger for the noble man is not that he will become a good man, but that he will become insolent, a scoffer, a destroyer.

Ah, I have known noble men who lost their highest hope. And then they slandered all high hopes.

Then they lived insolently in brief pleasures, and hardly cast a goal beyond the day.

“Spirit is also sensual pleasure” — that is what they said. Then the wings of their spirit broke: now it crawls about and dirties whatever it gnaws.

Once they thought of becoming heroes: pleasure-seekers is what they are now. To them the hero is a grief and a horror.

But by my love and my hope I implore you: do not throw away the hero in your soul! Hold your highest hope holy! —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
