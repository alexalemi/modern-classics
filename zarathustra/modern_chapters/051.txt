On the Virtue That Makes Small

I

When Zarathustra was back on solid land, he did not go straight to his mountains and his cave, but took many roads and asked many questions and found out this and that, so that he said of himself in jest: “Look, a river that flows back to its source in many windings!” For he wanted to learn what had happened *to man* in the meantime: whether he had become bigger or smaller. And once he saw a row of new houses; he was astonished and said:

“What do these houses mean? Truly, no great soul set them up as a parable of itself!

Did some silly child take them out of its toy box? If only another child would put them back in its box!

And these rooms and chambers: can *men* go in and out of them? They seem to me made for silk dolls; or for nibblers, who perhaps also let themselves be nibbled.”

And Zarathustra stood still and thought. At last he said sadly: “*Everything* has become smaller!

Everywhere I see lower gates: one of *my* kind can still get through them, but — he has to stoop!

Oh, when will I come home again to where I no longer have to stoop — no longer have to stoop before the small!” — And Zarathustra sighed and gazed into the distance. —

But that same day he gave his discourse on the virtue that makes small.

II

I walk among this people and keep my eyes open: they do not forgive me for not envying their virtues.

They snap at me because I tell them: small people need small virtues — and because I find it hard to swallow that small people are *needed*!

I am still like the rooster here in a strange farmyard, whom even the hens peck at; but I hold no grudge against these hens for it.

I am polite to them, as to every small annoyance; to be prickly toward small things seems to me a wisdom for hedgehogs.

They all talk about me when they sit around the fire in the evening — they talk about me, but nobody thinks — of me!

This is the new stillness I have learned: their noise around me spreads a cloak over my thoughts.

They make noise among themselves: “What does this dark cloud want with us? Let us make sure it does not bring us a plague!”

And recently a woman snatched back her child, who wanted to come to me: “Take the children away!” she screamed; “eyes like that scorch children’s souls.”

They cough when I speak: they think coughing is an objection to strong winds — they guess nothing of the roaring of my happiness!

“We have no time for Zarathustra yet” — so they object; but what does a time matter that “has no time” for Zarathustra?

And even when they praise me: how could I fall asleep on *their* glory? Their praise is a belt of thorns to me: it still scratches me when I take it off.

And this too I learned among them: the one who praises acts as though he were giving something back, but in truth he wants to be given more!

Ask my foot whether it likes their lauding and luring tune! Truly, to such a beat and tick-tock it wants neither to dance nor to stand still.

They would like to lure and laud me into small virtue; they would like to coax my foot into the tick-tock of small happiness.

I walk among this people and keep my eyes open: they have become *smaller*, and are getting smaller and smaller: — and it is their teaching about happiness and virtue that does it.

For they are modest even in virtue — because they want comfort. And only modest virtue gets along with comfort.

They too learn, in their way, to stride and to stride forward: that is what I call their *hobbling* —. With it they become a stumbling block to anyone in a hurry.

And many of them walk forward and look back while they do it, with a stiffened neck: I like to run into those.

Foot and eye should not lie, nor give each other the lie. But there is much lying among the small people.

Some of them will, but most of them are only willed. Some of them are genuine, but most of them are bad actors.

There are actors among them without knowing it, and actors without wanting it —, the genuine ones are always rare, especially the genuine actors.

There is little of the man here: that is why their women make themselves manly. For only one who is man enough will — redeem *the woman* in woman.

And this hypocrisy I found the worst among them: that even those who command feign the virtues of those who serve.

“I serve, you serve, we serve” — so prays even the hypocrisy of the rulers here — and woe if the first lord is *only* the first servant!

Ah, my eye’s curiosity strayed even into their hypocrisies; and I guessed well all their fly-happiness and their buzzing around sunlit windowpanes.

So much kindness, so much weakness I see. So much justice and pity, so much weakness.

Round, fair, and kind they are with one another, as grains of sand are round, fair, and kind with grains of sand.

To embrace a small happiness modestly — that they call “submission”! and meanwhile they are already squinting modestly after a new small happiness.

At bottom, in their simplicity, they want one thing most of all: that nobody hurt them. So they get in first with everyone and do them good.

But this is *cowardice*: even if it is called “virtue.” —

And when for once they speak roughly, these small people: *I* hear in it only their hoarseness — for every draft makes them hoarse.

Shrewd they are; their virtues have shrewd fingers. But they lack fists: their fingers do not know how to creep behind fists.

Virtue for them is what makes modest and tame: with it they have made the wolf into a dog, and man himself into man’s best domestic animal.

“We have set our chair in the *middle*” — so their smirk tells me — “just as far from dying gladiators as from contented sows.”

But this is — *mediocrity*: even if it is called moderation. —

III

I walk among this people and let many a word fall: but they know neither how to take nor how to keep.

They are surprised that I did not come to rail against lusts and vices; and truly, I did not come to warn against pickpockets either!

They are surprised that I am not ready to smarten and sharpen their cleverness still more: as if they did not already have enough know-it-alls, whose voices squeak at me like slate pencils!

And when I cry: “Curse all the cowardly devils in you that like to whimper and fold their hands and worship”: then they cry: “Zarathustra is godless.”

And their teachers of submission cry it most of all —; but it is precisely into their ears that I love to shout: Yes! I *am* Zarathustra, the godless!

These teachers of submission! Wherever things are small and sick and scabby, they crawl, like lice; and only my disgust keeps me from cracking them.

Well then! This is my sermon for *their* ears: I am Zarathustra the godless, who says: “Who is more godless than I, that I may delight in his instruction?”

I am Zarathustra the godless: where do I find my equals? And all those are my equals who give themselves their own will and throw off all submission.

I am Zarathustra the godless: I cook every accident in *my* own pot. And only when it is cooked through there do I welcome it, as *my* food.

And truly, many an accident came to me like a master: but my *will* spoke to it more masterfully still — and at once it lay on its knees, begging —

— begging that it might find shelter and a heart with me, and coaxing me with flattery: “Just look, oh Zarathustra, how only a friend comes to a friend!” —

But why do I speak, where no one has *my* ears! And so I will shout it out into all the winds:

You are getting smaller and smaller, you small people! You are crumbling away, you comfortable ones! You will perish yet —

— of your many small virtues, of your many small omissions, of your many small submissions!

Too sparing, too yielding: that is your soil! But for a tree to become *great*, it wants to strike hard roots around hard rocks!

What you leave undone, too, weaves at the web of all mankind’s future; even your nothing is a spiderweb, and a spider that lives on the blood of the future.

And when you take, it is like stealing, you small virtuous ones; but even among rogues *honor* says: “One should steal only where one cannot rob.”

“It will all work itself out” — that too is a teaching of submission. But I tell you, you comfortable ones: *it helps itself*, and will help itself to more and more of you!

Ah, if only you would throw off all *half* willing, and become resolute in sloth as in action!

Ah, if only you understood my word: “Go ahead and do whatever you will — but first be such as *are able to will*!”

“Go ahead and love your neighbor as yourselves — but first, I tell you, be such as *love themselves* —

— who love with the great love, who love with the great contempt!” Thus speaks Zarathustra the godless. —

But why do I speak, where no one has *my* ears! It is still an hour too early for me here.

I am my own forerunner among this people, my own cockcrow through dark alleys.

But *their* hour is coming! And mine is coming too! Hour by hour they become smaller, poorer, more barren — poor weeds! poor soil!

And *soon* they shall stand before me like dry grass and steppe, and truly! weary of themselves — and thirsting more for *fire* than for water!

Oh blessed hour of the lightning! Oh mystery before noon! — One day I will yet make running fires of them, and heralds with tongues of flame: —

— one day they shall yet proclaim with tongues of flame: It is coming, it is near, the great noon!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
