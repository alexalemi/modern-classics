On Reading and Writing

Of all that is written, I love only what someone writes with his own blood. Write with blood, and you will discover that blood is spirit.

It is not easy to understand the blood of another: I hate the idlers who read.

Whoever knows the reader does nothing more for the reader. Another century of readers — and spirit itself will stink.

That everyone may learn to read ruins, in the long run, not only writing but thinking too.

Once spirit was God, then it became man, and now it is even becoming mob.

Whoever writes in blood and in proverbs does not want to be read, but to be learned by heart.

In the mountains the shortest way is from peak to peak: but for that you must have long legs. Proverbs should be peaks: and those they are spoken to should be great and tall.

The air thin and pure, danger near, and the spirit full of a joyful malice: these things go well together.

I want goblins around me, for I am brave. Courage that scares away ghosts makes goblins for itself — courage wants to laugh.

I no longer feel what you feel: this cloud that I see beneath me, this blackness and heaviness that I laugh at — that is exactly your thundercloud.

You look upward when you long to be lifted up. And I look down, because I am lifted up.

Which of you can laugh and be lifted up at the same time?

Whoever climbs the highest mountains laughs at all tragic plays and all tragic solemnity.

Brave, carefree, mocking, violent — that is how wisdom wants us: she is a woman, and she only ever loves a warrior.

You tell me: “Life is hard to bear.” But then why do you have your pride in the morning and your resignation in the evening?

Life is hard to bear: but do not act so delicate with me! We are all of us fine, sturdy pack donkeys, he-donkeys and she-donkeys alike.

What do we have in common with the rosebud, which trembles because a drop of dew lies on its body?

It is true: we love life, not because we are used to living, but because we are used to loving.

There is always some madness in love. But there is always some reason in madness, too.

And to me as well, who am fond of life, it seems that butterflies and soap bubbles, and whatever is like them among human beings, know the most about happiness.

To see these light, foolish, dainty, nimble little souls flutter about — that moves Zarathustra to tears and to songs.

I would believe only in a god who could dance.

And when I saw my devil, I found him serious, thorough, deep, solemn: he was the spirit of gravity — through him all things fall.

One kills not with anger but with laughter. Come, let us kill the spirit of gravity!

I have learned to walk: since then I let myself run. I have learned to fly: since then I do not need to be pushed before I can move from the spot.

Now I am light, now I fly, now I see myself beneath myself, now a god dances through me.

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
