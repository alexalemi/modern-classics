On the Apostates

I

Ah, does everything already lie withered and gray that only lately stood green and bright on this meadow? And how much honey of hope I carried from here into my beehives!

These young hearts have all grown old already — and not even old! only tired, common, comfortable: — they call it “We have become pious again.”

Only lately I saw them running out in the early morning on brave feet: but their feet of knowledge grew tired, and now they even slander their morning bravery!

Truly, many of them once lifted their legs like a dancer; the laughter in my wisdom beckoned to them: — then they thought better of it. Just now I saw one of them bent over — crawling to the cross.

Once they fluttered around light and freedom like gnats and young poets. A little older, a little colder: and already they are dark-mongers and whisper-mongers and stove-squatters.

Did their hearts lose courage, perhaps, because solitude swallowed me like a whale? Did their ears listen for me long and longingly, *in vain*, for my trumpet calls and herald calls?

— Ah! There are always only a few whose hearts have a long courage and high spirits; and in such as these the spirit, too, stays patient. But the rest are *cowardly*.

The rest: that is always by far the most of them, the everyday, the superfluous, the many-too-many — all of these are cowardly! —

Whoever is of my kind will also meet the experiences of my kind along the way: so that his first companions must be corpses and jesters.

But his second companions — they will call themselves his *believers*: a living swarm, much love, much folly, much beardless veneration.

Whoever is of my kind among men should not bind his heart to these believers; whoever knows the fleeting, cowardly human kind should not believe in these springtimes and bright meadows!

If they *could* be otherwise, they would also *will* otherwise. Half-and-halfers spoil every whole. That leaves wither — what is there to lament in that!

Let them go and fall, oh Zarathustra, and do not lament! Better still, blow among them with rustling winds, —

— blow among these leaves, oh Zarathustra: so that everything *withered* runs away from you all the faster! —

II

“We have become pious again” — so these apostates confess; and some of them are still too cowardly to confess even that.

I look them in the eye — I say it to their faces and to the red in their cheeks: you are the kind who *pray* again!

But it is a disgrace to pray! Not for everyone, but for you and me and whoever has his conscience in his head as well. For *you* it is a disgrace to pray!

You know it well: the cowardly devil in you, who would like to fold his hands and lay his hands in his lap and have an easier time of it: — this cowardly devil talks you into it: “There *is* a God!”

*With that*, though, you belong to the light-shy kind, whom light never leaves in peace; now you must stick your head deeper into night and fog every day!

And truly, you chose your hour well: for the night birds are flying out again just now. The hour has come for all the light-shy people, the hour of evening and of rest, when they do not — “rest.”

I hear it and smell it: their hour has come for the hunt and the procession — not a wild hunt, to be sure, but a tame, lame, sniffing hunt of soft-treaders and soft-prayers, —

— a hunt for soulful, mousy cowerers: all the mousetraps of the heart are set up again! And wherever I lift a curtain, a little night moth comes tumbling out.

Was it perhaps crouching there together with another little night moth? For everywhere I smell small, tucked-away congregations; and wherever there are little prayer closets, there are new prayer-brothers inside them, and the stale air of prayer-brothers.

They sit together for long evenings and say: let us become like little children again and say “dear God”! — their mouths and stomachs ruined by the pious confectioners.

Or they spend long evenings watching a cunning, lurking cross-spider, which preaches shrewdness to the spiders themselves and teaches like this: “Under crosses, the spinning is good!”

Or they sit all day with fishing rods beside swamps and think that makes them *deep*; but whoever fishes where there are no fish, I would not even call shallow!

Or, pious and pleased, they learn to strum the harp from a songwriter who would like to harp his way into the hearts of young ladies: — for he has grown tired of the old ladies and their praises.

Or they learn to shudder from a learned half-madman who waits in darkened rooms for the spirits to come to him — and the spirit runs off altogether!

Or they listen to an old, restless purr-and-growl piper, who learned the gloom of sounds from gloomy winds; now he pipes as the wind blows and preaches gloom in gloomy tones.

And some of them have even become night watchmen: they now know how to blow horns and go around at night and wake up old things that fell asleep long ago.

Five words about old things I heard last night at the garden wall: they came from such old, gloomy, dried-up night watchmen.

“For a father, he doesn’t care enough about his children: human fathers do it better!” —

“He’s too old! He doesn’t care about his children at all anymore” — so the other night watchman answered.

“*Has* he got any children? Nobody can prove it, if he doesn’t prove it himself! I’ve wanted him to prove it properly for ages.”

“Prove it? As if *he* had ever proved anything! Proving comes hard to him; he sets great store by people believing him.”

“Yes! Yes! Faith saves him, faith in him. That’s the way of old people! It’s the same with us!” —

— Thus spoke the two old night watchmen and light-scarecrows to each other, and then tooted mournfully on their horns: so it happened last night at the garden wall.

But my heart writhed with laughter and wanted to break and did not know where to go? and sank into my diaphragm.

Truly, this will yet be my death, that I choke with laughter when I see donkeys drunk and hear night watchmen doubting God like this.

Is the time for all such doubts not *long* past? Who may still wake up such old, sleepy, light-shy things!

The old gods came to their end long ago, after all: — and truly, they had a good, merry end for gods!

They did not “twilight” themselves to death — that is a lie people tell! On the contrary: they once — *laughed* themselves to death!

That happened when the most godless word came from a god himself — the word: “There is One God! You shall have no other god besides me!” —

— an old grim-beard of a god, a jealous one, forgot himself like that: —

And all the gods laughed then, and rocked on their chairs, and cried: “Is this not precisely godliness, that there are gods, but no God?”

Whoever has ears, let him hear. —

Zarathustra spoke like this in the town he loved, which is called: the Motley Cow. For from here he had only two more days to go before he would be back in his cave and with his animals; and his soul rejoiced all the while at the nearness of his homecoming. —
