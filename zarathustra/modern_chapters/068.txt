The Leech

And Zarathustra went on, thoughtful, farther and deeper, through forests and past marshy hollows; but as happens to anyone who thinks about heavy things, he stepped on a man without noticing. And look, all at once a cry of pain and two curses and twenty foul insults spattered into his face: so that in his fright he raised his stick and struck the man he had stepped on as well. But the next moment he came to his senses; and his heart laughed at the folly he had just committed.

“Forgive me,” he said to the man he had stepped on, who had got up in a fury and sat down again, “forgive me, and before anything else, hear a parable.

As a wanderer who dreams of faraway things bumps, without noticing, on a lonely road into a sleeping dog, a dog lying in the sun:

— as both of them start up and fly at each other like mortal enemies, these two who are frightened to death: that is how it went with us.

And yet! And yet — how little was missing for them to caress each other, this dog and this solitary one! For they are both — solitary ones!”

— “Whoever you may be,” said the man who had been stepped on, still in a fury, “you are treading on me with your parable too, and not only with your foot!

Look, am I a dog, then?” — and with that the sitting man stood up and pulled his naked arm out of the swamp. For at first he had been lying stretched out on the ground, hidden and unrecognizable, like those who lie in wait for swamp game.

“But what are you doing!” cried Zarathustra in alarm, for he saw that a great deal of blood was flowing down the naked arm, “what has happened to you? Did an evil animal bite you, you unhappy man?”

The bleeding man laughed, still angry. “What business is it of yours!” he said, and was about to walk on. “Here I am at home and in my own domain. Let whoever wants to ask me: but I will hardly answer a clod.”

“You are wrong,” said Zarathustra with pity, and held him fast, “you are wrong: here you are not at home, but in my kingdom, and in it no one shall come to harm.

But call me whatever you like — I am who I must be. I myself call myself Zarathustra.

Come! Up there is the way to Zarathustra’s cave: it is not far — will you not tend your wounds at my place?

Things have gone badly for you in this life, you unhappy man: first the animal bit you, and then — man stepped on you!” —

But when the man who had been stepped on heard Zarathustra’s name, he was transformed. “What is happening to me!” he cried out. “*Who* else do I care about in this life, except this one man, Zarathustra, and that one animal that lives on blood, the leech?

For the leech’s sake I lay here by this swamp like a fisherman, and my arm, hung out as bait, had already been bitten ten times, when a still finer leech bites after my blood: Zarathustra himself!

Oh happiness! Oh wonder! Praised be this day that lured me into this swamp! Praised be the best, the liveliest cupping glass alive today, praised be the great leech of conscience, Zarathustra!” —

Thus spoke the man who had been stepped on; and Zarathustra was glad of his words and their fine, reverent manner. “Who are you?” he asked, and gave him his hand. “Between us there is much to clear up and to cheer up: but already, it seems to me, a pure, bright day is dawning.”

“I am *the conscientious one of the spirit*,” answered the one who was asked, “and in matters of the spirit hardly anyone takes things more strictly, more narrowly, and more harshly than I do, except the one I learned it from, Zarathustra himself.

Better to know nothing than to half-know a great deal! Better to be a fool on one’s own account than a sage by other people’s lights! I — go to the ground of things:

— what does it matter whether it is great or small? Whether it is called swamp or sky? A hand’s breadth of ground is enough for me: so long as it is truly ground, solid ground to stand on!

— a hand’s breadth of ground: a man can stand on that. In a true conscience-science there is nothing great and nothing small.”

“Then perhaps you are the expert on the leech?” asked Zarathustra. “And you pursue the leech down to its ultimate grounds, you conscientious one?”

“Oh Zarathustra,” answered the man who had been stepped on, “that would be something enormous; how could I dare to take that on!

But what I am master and expert of is the leech’s *brain*: — that is *my* world!

And it is a world, too! But forgive me that my pride speaks up here, for here I have no equal. That is why I said, ‘Here I am at home.’

How long I have been pursuing this one thing, the brain of the leech, so that the slippery truth might no longer slip away from me here! Here is *my* kingdom!

— for its sake I threw everything else away, for its sake everything else became all the same to me; and right beside my knowledge lies my black ignorance.

My conscience of the spirit wants it so from me: that I know one thing and know nothing of everything else. I am disgusted by all the half-spirited, all the hazy, the hovering, the rapturous.

Where my honesty stops, I am blind, and I want to be blind. But where I want to know, I want to be honest as well — that is, hard, strict, narrow, cruel, inexorable.

That *you* once said, oh Zarathustra, ‘Spirit is the life that itself cuts into life’ — that led me, and led me astray, to your teaching. And truly, with my own blood I have increased my own knowledge!”

— “As is plain to see,” Zarathustra broke in; for the blood was still flowing down the naked arm of the conscientious one. Ten leeches, you see, had bitten into it.

“Oh you strange fellow, how much this plain sight here teaches me — namely, you yourself! And perhaps I should not pour all of it into your strict ears!

Come, then! Here we part! But I would gladly find you again. Up there is the way to my cave: tonight you shall be my dear guest there!

I would also gladly make it up to your body that Zarathustra trod on you with his feet: I am thinking it over. But now a cry of distress calls me away from you in haste.”

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
