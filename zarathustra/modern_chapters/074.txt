At Noon

— And Zarathustra ran and ran and found no one anymore, and was alone and found himself again and again, and enjoyed and sipped his solitude and thought of good things, — for hours. But around the hour of noon, when the sun stood directly over Zarathustra’s head, he passed an old, crooked, gnarled tree, which was embraced all around by the rich love of a grapevine and hidden from itself: from it yellow grapes hung down in plenty toward the wanderer. Then he felt the urge to quench a little thirst and break off a bunch of grapes for himself; but when he was already stretching out his arm for it, he felt the urge for something else even more: namely, to lie down beside the tree, at the hour of perfect noon, and sleep.

This Zarathustra did; and as soon as he lay on the ground, in the stillness and secrecy of the many-colored grass, he had already forgotten his little thirst and fell asleep. For, as Zarathustra’s proverb says: One thing is more necessary than another. Only his eyes stayed open: — for they could not get enough of seeing and praising the tree and the love of the vine. But as he fell asleep, Zarathustra spoke to his heart like this:

Still! Still! Did the world not just now become perfect? What is happening to me?

As a delicate wind, unseen, dances on an inlaid sea, light, light as a feather: so — sleep dances on me.

It closes no eye of mine, it leaves my soul awake. It is light, truly! light as a feather.

It persuades me, I do not know how?, it dabs at me inside with a coaxing hand, it compels me. Yes, it compels me, so that my soul stretches out: —

— how long and tired it grows, my strange soul! Did a seventh day’s evening come to it right at noon? Has it wandered too long already, blissful, among good and ripe things?

It stretches out long, long, — longer! It lies still, my strange soul. Too many good things it has tasted already; a golden sadness weighs on it, it twists its mouth.

— Like a ship that has run into its stillest bay: — now it leans against the earth, tired of the long voyages and the uncertain seas. Is the earth not more faithful?

As such a ship lays itself alongside the land and nestles against it: — then it is enough for a spider to spin its thread to it from the land. No stronger ropes are needed there.

Like such a tired ship in the stillest bay: so I too rest now close to the earth, faithful, trusting, waiting, tied to it with the faintest threads.

Oh happiness! Oh happiness! Do you want to sing, oh my soul? You are lying in the grass. But this is the secret, solemn hour when no shepherd plays his flute.

Take care! Hot noon is sleeping on the meadows. Do not sing! Still! The world is perfect.

Do not sing, you songbird of the grass, oh my soul! Do not even whisper! Look — still! old noon is sleeping, it moves its mouth: is it not just now drinking a drop of happiness —

— an old brown drop of golden happiness, golden wine? Something flits across it, its happiness laughs. So — a god laughs. Still! —

— “For happiness, how little is enough for happiness!” So I spoke once, and thought myself clever. But it was a blasphemy: *that* I have learned now. Clever fools speak better.

Precisely the least, the softest, the lightest, a lizard’s rustling, a breath, a flit, a twinkling of an eye — *little* makes up the *best* happiness. Still!

— What happened to me: Listen! Did time fly away? Am I not falling? Did I not fall — listen! into the well of eternity?

— What is happening to me? Still! It stings me — woe — in the heart? In the heart! Oh break, break, heart, after such happiness, after such a sting!

— What? Did the world not just now become perfect? Round and ripe? Oh, the golden round ring, the ripe ring — where is it flying off to? Let me run after it! Quick!

Still — — (and here Zarathustra stretched himself and felt that he was asleep.) —

Up! he said to himself, you sleeper! You noon-sleeper! Come on, get up, you old legs! It is time and overtime, many a good stretch of road is still left for you —

Now you have slept your fill, but for how long? Half an eternity! Come on, get up now, my old heart! How long will it be after such a sleep before you may — wake yourself up?

(But then he was already falling asleep again, and his soul spoke against him and resisted and lay down again) — “Leave me alone! Still! Did the world not just now become perfect? Oh, the golden round ball!” —

“Get up,” said Zarathustra, “you little thief, you stealer of days! What? Still stretching, yawning, sighing, falling down into deep wells?

Who are you, then! Oh my soul!” (and here he took fright, for a sunbeam fell down from the sky onto his face)

“Oh heaven above me,” he said with a sigh, and sat up straight, “are you watching me? Are you listening to my strange soul?

When will you drink this drop of dew that fell down on all earthly things, — when will you drink this strange soul —

— when, well of eternity! you bright, dreadful abyss of noon! when will you drink my soul back into yourself?”

Thus spoke Zarathustra, and rose from his resting place by the tree as if from a strange drunkenness: and look, there the sun still stood directly over his head. From this, though, one might rightly conclude that Zarathustra had not slept long that time.
