On the Pale Criminal

You do not want to kill, you judges and sacrificers, until the animal has nodded? Look, the pale criminal has nodded: out of his eye speaks the great contempt.

“My ‘I’ is something that shall be overcome: my ‘I’ is to me the great contempt for man”: that is what speaks out of this eye.

That he judged himself was his highest moment: do not let the exalted one sink back into his lowliness!

There is no redemption for one who suffers so from himself, unless it is a quick death.

Your killing, you judges, should be an act of pity and not of revenge. And as you kill, see to it that you yourselves justify life!

It is not enough that you be reconciled with the one you kill. Let your sadness be love for the overman: that is how you justify your still-being-alive!

“Enemy” you shall say, but not “villain”; “sick man” you shall say, but not “scoundrel”; “fool” you shall say, but not “sinner.”

And you, red judge, if you were to say out loud everything you have already done in your thoughts, then everyone would cry: “Away with this filth and this poisonous worm!”

But the thought is one thing, the deed is another, and the image of the deed another again. The wheel of cause does not roll between them.

An image made this pale man pale. He was equal to his deed when he did it: but he could not bear its image once it was done.

Now he always saw himself as the doer of one deed. Madness, I call this: the exception turned itself, for him, into the essence.

A stroke of chalk spellbinds a hen; the stroke he struck spellbound his poor reason — madness *after* the deed, I call this.

Listen, you judges! There is another madness besides: and it comes before the deed. Ah, you did not crawl deep enough into this soul for me!

So says the red judge: “Why did this criminal murder? He wanted to rob.” But I tell you: his soul wanted blood, not loot: he thirsted for the bliss of the knife!

But his poor reason did not understand this madness, and talked him round. “What does blood matter!” it said. “Do you not want at least to get some loot out of it? To take some revenge?”

And he listened to his poor reason: its words lay on him like lead — so he robbed when he murdered. He did not want to be ashamed of his madness.

And now the lead of his guilt lies on him again, and again his poor reason is so stiff, so paralyzed, so heavy.

If only he could shake his head, his burden would roll off: but who will shake this head?

What is this man? A heap of diseases that reach out into the world through the spirit: there they want to seize their prey.

What is this man? A tangle of wild serpents that seldom have peace with one another — so they go off on their own and seek prey in the world.

Look at this poor body! What it suffered and craved, this poor soul interpreted for itself — it interpreted it as murderous lust and greed for the bliss of the knife.

Whoever falls sick now is overcome by the evil that is evil now: he wants to cause pain with whatever causes him pain. But there were other times, and another evil and good.

Once, doubt was evil, and the will to a self. Back then the sick man became a heretic and a witch: as heretic and witch he suffered and wanted to cause suffering.

But this will not go into your ears: it harms your good people, you tell me. But what do I care about your good people!

Much about your good people disgusts me, and truly not their evil. How I wish they had a madness to perish of, like this pale criminal!

Truly, I wish their madness were called truth, or loyalty, or justice: but they have their virtue in order to live long, and in a wretched contentment.

I am a railing beside the torrent: let whoever can grasp me, grasp me! But I am not your crutch. —

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
