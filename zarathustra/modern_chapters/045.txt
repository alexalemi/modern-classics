The Stillest Hour

“What has happened to me, my friends? You see me distraught, driven away, unwillingly obedient, ready to go — ah, to go away from *you*!

Yes, Zarathustra must go back into his solitude once more: but this time the bear goes back into his cave without any joy!

What has happened to me? Who commands this? — Ah, my angry mistress wills it so; she spoke to me: have I ever yet told you her name?

Yesterday, toward evening, *my stillest hour* spoke to me: that is the name of my terrible mistress.

And this is how it happened, — for I must tell you everything, so that your hearts do not harden against the one who is suddenly leaving!

Do you know the terror of someone falling asleep? —

He is terrified down to his toes, because the ground gives way under him and the dream begins.

I tell you this as a parable. Yesterday, at the stillest hour, the ground gave way under me: the dream began.

The hand moved on, the clock of my life drew breath — never had I heard such stillness around me: so that my heart was terrified.

Then it spoke to me without a voice: ‘You know it, Zarathustra?’ —

And I cried out in terror at this whisper, and the blood drained from my face: but I kept silent.

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘You know it, Zarathustra, but you do not speak it!’ —

And at last I answered, like someone defiant: ‘Yes, I know it, but I will not speak it!’

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘You *will* not, Zarathustra? Is that really true? Do not hide yourself in your defiance!’ —

And I wept and trembled like a child and said: ‘Ah, I would like to, but how can I! Spare me this, at least! It is beyond my strength!’

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘What do you matter, Zarathustra! Speak your word and break!’ —

And I answered: ‘Ah, is it *my* word? Who am I? I am waiting for someone worthier; I am not worthy even to break on it.’

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘What do you matter? You are not yet humble enough for me. Humility has the toughest hide.’ —

And I answered: ‘What has the hide of my humility not borne already! I live at the foot of my own height: how high are my peaks? No one has told me yet. But I know my valleys well.’

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘Oh Zarathustra, whoever has mountains to move moves valleys and lowlands too.’ —

And I answered: ‘My word has not yet moved any mountains, and what I said did not reach people. I did go to people, but I have not yet arrived among them.’

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘What do you know *about that*! The dew falls on the grass when the night is at its most silent.’ —

And I answered: ‘They mocked me when I found my own way and walked it; and in truth my feet trembled then.’

And so they said to me: ‘You have unlearned the way, now you are unlearning how to walk as well!’

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘What does their mockery matter! You are someone who has unlearned how to obey: now you shall command!

Do you not know *who* is most needed by all? The one who commands great things.

To do great things is hard: but the harder thing is to command great things.

This is what is most unforgivable in you: you have the power, and you will not rule.’ —

And I answered: ‘I lack the lion’s voice for any commanding.’

Then it spoke to me again, like a whisper: ‘It is the stillest words that bring the storm. Thoughts that come on doves’ feet guide the world.

Oh Zarathustra, you shall go as a shadow of what must come: in that way you will command, and lead the way by commanding.’ —

And I answered: ‘I am ashamed.’

Then it spoke to me again without a voice: ‘You must still become a child, and without shame.

The pride of youth is still on you, you became young late: but whoever wants to become a child must overcome even his youth.’ —

And I thought it over for a long time, and trembled. But at last I said what I had said at first: ‘I will not.’

Then there was laughter all around me. Woe, how that laughter tore my insides and slit my heart open!

And it spoke to me for the last time: ‘Oh Zarathustra, your fruits are ripe, but you are not ripe for your fruits!

So you must go back into solitude: for you must still grow mellow.’ —

And again it laughed, and fled: then it grew still around me, as if with a twofold stillness. But I lay on the ground, and the sweat poured from my limbs.

— Now you have heard everything, and why I must go back into my solitude. I have kept nothing silent from you, my friends.

But you have heard this from me as well: *who* is still the most silent of all men — and wants to be!

Ah, my friends! I would still have something to say to you, I would still have something to give you! Why do I not give it? Am I stingy, then?” —

But when Zarathustra had spoken these words, the force of his pain and the nearness of parting from his friends overwhelmed him, so that he wept aloud; and no one knew how to comfort him. But in the night he went away alone and left his friends.
