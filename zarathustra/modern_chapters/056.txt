On the Three Evils

I

In a dream, in my last morning dream, I stood today on a headland — beyond the world; I held a pair of scales and *weighed* the world.

Oh, that the dawn came to me too early: she glowed me awake, the jealous one! She is always jealous of the glowing of my morning dreams.

Measurable for one who has time, weighable for a good weigher, reachable in flight for strong wings, guessable for divine nutcrackers: that is how my dream found the world: —

My dream, a bold sailor, half ship, half squall, silent as butterflies, impatient as falcons: how did it have the patience and the leisure today for weighing the world!

Did my wisdom perhaps speak to it in secret, my laughing, wide-awake daytime wisdom, which mocks all “infinite worlds”? For it says: “Where there is force, *number* becomes the mistress too: she has more force.”

How surely my dream looked upon this finite world, not greedy for the new, not greedy for the old, not fearing, not pleading: —

— as if a full apple offered itself to my hand, a ripe golden apple with cool, soft, velvety skin: — that is how the world offered itself to me: —

— as if a tree beckoned to me, a broad-branched, strong-willed tree, bent into a backrest and a footrest for the one weary of the road: that is how the world stood on my headland: —

— as if delicate hands carried a shrine toward me — a shrine open for the delight of bashful, reverent eyes: that is how the world offered itself to me today: —

— not riddle enough to scare human love away, not solution enough to put human wisdom to sleep: — a humanly good thing the world was to me today, the world of which so much evil is said!

How I thank my morning dream that I weighed the world like this, early today! As a humanly good thing it came to me, this dream and comforter of the heart!

And so that I may do as it did by day, and copy and learn its best from it: I will now put the three most evil things on the scales and weigh them well, humanly. —

Whoever taught blessing also taught cursing: which are the three best-cursed things in the world? These I will put on the scales.

Sensual pleasure, lust for power, selfishness: these three have so far been cursed the best and bad-mouthed and lie-mouthed the worst — these three I will weigh well, humanly.

Come on, then! Here is my headland, and there is the sea: *it* rolls toward me, shaggy, fawning, the faithful old hundred-headed dog-monster that I love.

Come on, then! Here I will hold the scales over the rolling sea: and I choose a witness too, to watch — you, you hermit tree, you strong-scented, broad-vaulted tree that I love! —

On what bridge does the Now cross over to the Someday? By what compulsion does the high compel itself down to the low? And what bids even the highest still — to grow upward? —

Now the scales stand level and still: three heavy questions I threw in, three heavy answers the other pan carries.

II

Sensual pleasure: to all hair-shirted despisers of the body, their thorn and stake, and cursed as “the world” by all believers in worlds behind: for it mocks and fools all muddlers and misleaders.

Sensual pleasure: to the rabble, the slow fire on which it is burned; to all wormy wood, to all stinking rags, the furnace ready to rut and reek.

Sensual pleasure: to free hearts, innocent and free, the garden happiness of the earth, the overflowing thanks of all the future to the Now.

Sensual pleasure: only to the withered a sweetish poison, but to the lion-willed the great cordial for the heart, and the wine of wines, reverently saved.

Sensual pleasure: the great parable-happiness for a higher happiness and the highest hope. For to much is marriage promised, and more than marriage, —

— to much that is more foreign to itself than man and woman: — and who has fully grasped *how foreign* man and woman are to each other!

Sensual pleasure: — but I want fences around my thoughts and around my words as well: so that the swine and the swooners do not break into my gardens! —

Lust for power: the red-hot scourge of the hardest of the hard-hearted; the gruesome torture that saves itself up for the cruelest himself; the somber flame of living pyres.

Lust for power: the spiteful gadfly set upon the vainest peoples; the mocker of all uncertain virtue; the rider on every horse and every pride.

Lust for power: the earthquake that breaks and breaks open everything rotten and hollow; the rolling, rumbling, punishing breaker of whitewashed tombs; the flashing question mark beside premature answers.

Lust for power: before whose gaze man crawls and cringes and slaves and becomes lower than serpent and swine: — until at last the great contempt cries out of him —,

Lust for power: the terrible teacher of the great contempt, who preaches “away with you!” to the very faces of cities and empires — until it cries out of them themselves, “away with *me*!”

Lust for power: which climbs alluringly, though, even to the pure and the solitary, and up to self-sufficient heights, glowing like a love that paints purple blisses alluringly on earthly skies.

Lust for power: but who would call it a *lust*, when what is high longs downward for power! Truly, there is nothing sick and nothing craving in such longing and descending!

That the solitary height may not isolate itself forever and be content with itself; that the mountain may come to the valley and the winds of the heights to the lowlands: —

Oh, who could find the right christening name and virtue name for such a longing! “Gift-giving virtue” — that is what Zarathustra once named the unnameable.

And it was then too that it happened — and truly, it happened for the first time! — that his word called *selfishness* blessed, the whole, healthy selfishness that wells up from a mighty soul: —

— from a mighty soul, to which belongs the high body, the beautiful, victorious, refreshing body, around which every single thing becomes a mirror:

— the supple, persuasive body, the dancer, whose parable and essence is the soul that delights in itself. The self-delight of such bodies and souls calls itself: “virtue.”

With its words of good and bad, such self-delight shields itself as if with sacred groves; with the names of its happiness it banishes from itself everything contemptible.

It banishes from itself everything cowardly; it says: Bad — that is cowardly! Contemptible in its eyes is the one who always worries, sighs, whimpers, and whoever picks up even the smallest advantages.

It also despises all woe-wallowing wisdom: for truly, there is also wisdom that blooms in the dark, a nightshade wisdom, which always sighs: “All is vanity!”

Shy mistrust counts for little with it, and so does everyone who wants oaths instead of looks and hands: and all all-too-mistrustful wisdom as well — for that is the way of cowardly souls.

Still less does it count the quick-to-please, the doglike one, who lies down on his back at once, the humble one; and there is wisdom too that is humble and doglike and pious and quick-to-please.

Hateful to it, even disgusting, is the one who never wants to defend himself, who swallows down poisonous spit and evil looks, the all-too-patient one, the all-enduring, the all-satisfied: for that is the serf’s kind.

Whether someone is servile before gods and divine kicks, or before men and stupid human opinions: it spits on *every* serf’s kind, this blessed selfishness!

Bad: that is what it calls everything that is bowed and stingily servile, unfree blinking eyes, pressed-down hearts, and that false, yielding kind that kisses with broad, cowardly lips.

And sham wisdom: that is what it calls everything that serfs and graybeards and the weary make jokes about; and especially all the wicked, witless, over-witty foolery of priests!

But the sham-wise, all the priests, the world-weary, and whoever has a soul of the woman’s and the serf’s kind — oh, what foul play their game has always made with selfishness!

And precisely this was supposed to be virtue and to be called virtue, *that* one made foul play with selfishness! And “selfless” — that is what all these world-weary cowards and cross-spiders wished to be, and with good reason!

But for all of them the day is coming now, the change, the sword of judgment, *the great noon*: then many things shall be revealed!

And whoever calls the “I” whole and holy and selfishness blessed, truly, he also speaks what he knows, as one who foreknows: “Look, it is coming, it is near, the great noon!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
