On the Spirit of Gravity

I

My mouth — belongs to the people: I talk too coarsely and too heartily for the silky rabbits. And my word sounds stranger still to all ink-squids and pen-foxes.

My hand — is a fool’s hand: woe to all tables and walls, and whatever else still has room for fools’ doodles, fools’ daubs!

My foot — is a horse’s hoof; with it I trot and tramp over stick and stone, criss-cross across country, and I am devilish with delight at every fast run.

My stomach — is it an eagle’s stomach, perhaps? For it loves lamb best of all. But it is certainly a bird’s stomach.

Fed on innocent things, and on little, ready and impatient to fly, to fly away — that is my nature now: how could there not be something of the bird’s nature in it!

And above all, that I am the enemy of the spirit of gravity, that is the bird’s nature: and truly, deadly enemy, archenemy, primal enemy! Oh, where has my enmity not already flown and flown astray!

I could sing a song about that — and I *will* sing it: although I am alone in an empty house and must sing it to my own ears.

There are other singers, of course, whose throats are softened, whose hands grow eloquent, whose eyes grow expressive, whose hearts grow wakeful only in a full house: — those I do not resemble. —

II

Whoever one day teaches men to fly will have moved all the boundary stones; all the boundary stones themselves will fly into the air for him; he will baptize the earth anew — as “the Light One.”

The ostrich runs faster than the fastest horse, but even it still sticks its head heavily into the heavy earth: so it is with the man who cannot yet fly.

Earth and life are heavy to him; and that is how the spirit of gravity *wants* it! But whoever wants to become light, and a bird, must love himself: — that is what *I* teach.

Not, of course, with the love of the sick and the craving: for with them even self-love stinks!

One must learn to love oneself — that is what I teach — with a whole and healthy love: so that one can bear to be with oneself and does not go roaming about.

Such roaming about christens itself “love of the neighbor”: with this word the best lying and the best hypocrisy have been done so far, and especially by those who were heavy on everyone.

And truly, this is no commandment for today and tomorrow, to *learn* to love oneself. Rather, of all arts this is the finest, the most cunning, the last, and the most patient.

For to its owner, all that is his own is well hidden; and of all treasure pits, one’s own is dug up last — so the spirit of gravity arranges it.

Almost in the cradle we are handed heavy words and values: “good” and “evil” — that is what this dowry calls itself. For its sake we are forgiven for being alive.

And that is why the little children are allowed to come, so that they can be stopped in good time from loving themselves: so the spirit of gravity arranges it.

And we — we faithfully drag what we are handed, on hard shoulders and over rough mountains! And when we sweat, they tell us: “Yes, life is hard to bear!”

But only man is hard for himself to bear! The reason is that he drags too much that is foreign on his shoulders. Like the camel, he kneels down and lets himself be well loaded.

Especially the strong, load-bearing man in whom reverence dwells: he loads too many *foreign* heavy words and values onto himself — and now life seems to him a desert!

And truly! Much that is one’s *own* is hard to bear, too! And much that is inside man is like the oyster: that is, disgusting and slippery and hard to grasp —,

— so that a noble shell with noble ornament must plead for it. But this art too one must learn: to *have* a shell, and a fair appearance, and a prudent blindness!

Again, many things about man deceive us, because many a shell is poor and sad and too much a shell. Much hidden goodness and strength is never guessed; the most exquisite delicacies find no one to taste them!

Women know this, the most exquisite of them: a little fatter, a little thinner — oh, how much fate lies in so little!

Man is hard to discover, and hardest of all for himself; often the spirit lies about the soul. So the spirit of gravity arranges it.

But he has discovered himself who says: This is *my* good and evil: with that he has struck dumb the mole and dwarf who says, “Good for all, evil for all.”

Truly, I do not like those either for whom each and every thing is good and this world is even the best. Those I call the all-satisfied.

All-satisfaction, which knows how to taste everything: that is not the best taste! I honor the stubborn, choosy tongues and stomachs that have learned to say “I” and “Yes” and “No.”

But to chew and digest everything — that is the true swine’s way! Always to say Yeah-aw — only the donkey has learned that, and whoever is of his spirit! —

Deep yellow and hot red: that is what *my* taste wants — it mixes blood into all colors. But whoever whitewashes his house betrays to me a whitewashed soul.

Some are in love with mummies, the others with ghosts; and both alike are enemies of all flesh and blood — oh, how both go against my taste! For I love blood.

And I do not want to live and linger where everyone spits and spews: that is now *my* taste — I would sooner live among thieves and perjurers. No one carries gold in his mouth.

But still more repugnant to me are all lickspittles; and the most repugnant animal of a man that I found, I baptized parasite: it did not want to love, and yet wanted to live on love.

Wretched I call all those who have only one choice: to become evil beasts or evil beast-tamers: among such I would build myself no huts.

Wretched I call those too who must always *wait* — they go against my taste: all the tax collectors and shopkeepers and kings and other keepers of countries and counters.

Truly, I learned waiting too, and learned it thoroughly,

— but only waiting for *myself*. And above all I learned standing and walking and running and jumping and climbing and dancing.

But this is my teaching: whoever wants to learn to fly one day must first learn to stand and walk and run and climb and dance: — one does not fly one’s way into flying!

With rope ladders I learned to climb up to many a window, with nimble legs I clambered up high masts: to sit on high masts of knowledge seemed to me no small bliss, —

— to flicker like little flames on high masts: a small light, to be sure, but still a great comfort to storm-driven sailors and the shipwrecked! —

By many ways and in many manners I came to my truth; not by one ladder did I climb to the height where my eye roams into my distance.

And I only ever asked the way unwillingly — that always went against my taste! I would rather question and try out the ways themselves.

A trying and a questioning was all my going: — and truly, one must also *learn* to answer such questioning! But that — is my taste:

— not a good one, not a bad one, but *my* taste, which I no longer either hide or am ashamed of.

“This — is now *my* way — where is yours?” That is how I answered those who asked me “the way.” For *the* way — it does not exist!

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
