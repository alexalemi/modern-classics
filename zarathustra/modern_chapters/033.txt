The Dance Song

One evening Zarathustra was walking through the forest with his disciples; and as he was looking for a well, look, he came upon a green meadow quietly ringed by trees and bushes, and on it girls were dancing together. As soon as the girls recognized Zarathustra, they stopped dancing; but Zarathustra stepped up to them with a friendly gesture and spoke these words:

“Do not stop dancing, you lovely girls! No spoilsport has come to you with an evil eye, no enemy of girls.

I am God’s advocate before the devil: and the devil is the spirit of gravity. How could I, you light ones, be an enemy of divine dances? Or of girls’ feet with pretty ankles?

It is true that I am a forest and a night of dark trees: but whoever is not afraid of my darkness will also find slopes of roses under my cypresses.

And he will probably find the little god too, the one girls love best: he lies beside the well, quiet, with his eyes closed.

Truly, he fell asleep on me in broad daylight, that waster of days! Did he chase too many butterflies, perhaps?

Do not be angry with me, you beautiful dancers, if I punish the little god a little! He will probably cry and weep — but he is laughable even when he weeps!

And with tears in his eyes he shall ask you for a dance; and I myself will sing a song to his dance:

A dancing song and a mocking song on the spirit of gravity, my supreme, almighty devil, of whom they say that he is ‘the lord of the world’.” —

And this is the song that Zarathustra sang while Cupid and the girls danced together.

Into your eye I looked not long ago, oh life! And I seemed to myself to be sinking into the unfathomable.

But you pulled me out with a golden fishing rod; you laughed mockingly when I called you unfathomable.

“That is how all fish talk,” you said; “what *they* cannot fathom is unfathomable.

But I am only changeable and wild, and in everything a woman, and not a virtuous one:

Even if to you men I am called ‘the deep one’, or ‘the faithful one’, ‘the eternal one’, ‘the mysterious one’. —

But you men always present us with your own virtues — ah, you virtuous ones!”

Thus she laughed, the unbelievable one; but I never believe her or her laughter when she speaks evil of herself.

And when I talked alone with my wild wisdom, she said to me angrily: “You will, you crave, you love: that is the only reason you *praise* life!”

I almost gave her an evil answer then and told the angry one the truth; and there is no more evil answer than to “tell the truth” to one’s wisdom.

For this is how things stand among the three of us. From the bottom of my heart I love only life — and truly, most of all when I hate her!

But that I am fond of wisdom, and often too fond: that is because she reminds me so very much of life!

She has life’s eye, life’s laugh, and even life’s little golden fishing rod: how can I help it that the two look so alike?

And once, when life asked me: Who is she, then, this wisdom? — I said eagerly: “Ah, yes! Wisdom!

One thirsts for her and is never satisfied, one looks through veils, one snatches through nets.

Is she beautiful? What do I know! But even the oldest carp are still caught with her as bait.

She is changeable and stubborn; I have often seen her bite her lip and comb her hair against the grain.

Perhaps she is evil and false, and in everything a female; but just when she speaks badly of herself, that is when she seduces most.”

When I said this to life, she laughed maliciously and closed her eyes. “Whom are you talking about?” she said. “About me, perhaps?

And even if you were right — is *that* something to say to my face like this! But now go on and speak of your wisdom too!”

Ah, and now you opened your eye again, oh beloved life! And again I seemed to myself to be sinking into the unfathomable. —

Thus sang Zarathustra. But when the dance was over and the girls had gone away, he grew sad.

“The sun has long since gone down,” he said at last; “the meadow is damp, a coolness is coming from the forests.

Something unknown is around me and looks on thoughtfully. What! You are still alive, Zarathustra?

Why? What for? By what? Where to? Where? How? Is it not folly to go on living? —

Ah, my friends, it is the evening that asks such things from within me. Forgive me my sadness!

Evening has come: forgive me that evening has come!”

Thus spoke Zarathustra.
