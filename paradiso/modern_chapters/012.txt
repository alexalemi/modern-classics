Canto Thirteen

	Let him imagine, who longs to understand well
	what I saw now — and let him hold the image
	while I speak, like a firm rock —

	fifteen stars which in different quarters
	so quicken the sky with such clearness
	that it beats through every thickness of the air;

	let him imagine that Wagon for which the lap
	of our sky is enough by night and by day,
	so that it never fails as the pole turns;

	let him imagine the mouth of that horn
	which begins at the point of the axle
	round which the first wheel goes,

	and that they have made two signs of themselves in heaven
	like the one the daughter of Minos made
	when she felt the chill of death;

	and that each has its rays inside the other,
	and that both turn in such a way
	that one goes first and the other after;

	and he will have something like the shadow
	of the real constellation and of the double dance
	that was circling the point where I was —

	since it is as far beyond our experience
	as the heaven that outruns all the others
	is beyond the moving of the Chiana.

	There they sang no Bacchus and no Paean,
	but three persons in the divine nature,
	and that nature and the human in one person.

	The singing and the wheeling finished their measure,
	and those holy lights attended to us,
	making themselves happy from care to care.

	Then the light in which the marvellous life
	of God's poor man had been told to me
	broke the silence among the agreeing gods,

	and said: "When one sheaf is threshed,
	when its seed is already stored,
	sweet love invites me to thresh the other.

	You believe that into the breast from which the rib
	was drawn to shape the beautiful cheek
	whose palate cost the whole world dear,

	and into that which, pierced by the lance,
	both before and after gave such satisfaction
	that it outweighs every fault on the scales,

	all the light that human nature is allowed
	to have was poured
	by that Power which made both of them;

	and so you wonder at what I said above,
	when I told you that the good shut in the fifth light
	never had a second.

	Now open your eyes to what I answer you,
	and you will see that your believing and my saying
	come together in the truth like the centre in a circle.

	What does not die and what can die
	are nothing but a splendour of that idea
	which our Lord, by loving, brings to birth;

	for that living light which so pours out
	from its shining source that it is not disjoined
	from him, nor from the love that makes a third with them,

	gathers its shining, out of its own goodness,
	as if reflected, in nine subsistences,
	remaining eternally one.

	From there it comes down to the last powers,
	from act to act, becoming so much less
	that it makes nothing but brief contingent things;

	and by these contingent things I mean
	the generated things, which the wheeling heaven produces
	with seed and without seed.

	Their wax, and what shapes it,
	are not always one way; and so under the ideal seal
	the light shines through more and less.

	From this it comes that one and the same tree,
	by species, gives better and worse fruit;
	and that you are born with different wits.

	If the wax were exactly ready
	and the heaven at the top of its power,
	the light of the seal would show entire;

	but nature always gives it short,
	working like the artist
	who has the habit of the art and a trembling hand.

	So, if the warm love disposes and stamps
	the clear vision of the first power,
	all perfection is gained there.

	So the earth was once made worthy
	of all the perfection of a living creature;
	so the Virgin was made pregnant;

	so that I approve your opinion
	that human nature never was, and never will be,
	what it was in those two persons.

	Now, if I went no further forward,
	your words would begin: 'Then how was
	this man without an equal?'

	But so that what does not appear may appear well,
	think who he was, and the reason that moved him
	to ask, when he was told 'Ask.'

	I have not spoken so that you cannot see
	that he was a king, and asked for wisdom
	so that he might be a king who was equal to it;

	not to know the number of the movers
	up here, nor whether necessary and contingent
	ever made a necessary;

	nor whether a first motion must be granted,
	nor whether in a half circle a triangle can be made
	that has no right angle.

	So that, if you note this and what I said before,
	that peerless seeing which the arrow
	of my meaning strikes is kingly prudence;

	and if you turn clear eyes on the word 'rose',
	you will see it has to do only with kings,
	who are many, and the good ones rare.

	Take my saying with this distinction,
	and so it can stand with what you believe
	about the first father and about our Beloved.

	And let this always be lead on your feet,
	to make you move slowly, like a tired man,
	both to the yes and to the no that you do not see:

	for he is very low among the fools
	who affirms and denies without distinction
	in the one case as in the other;

	because it often happens that a hasty opinion
	bends the wrong way,
	and then affection binds the understanding.

	Far worse than uselessly does a man put out from shore,
	because he does not come back as he set off,
	who fishes for the truth and has not the art.

	And of this there are open proofs in the world:
	Parmenides, Melissus, Bryson and many others,
	who went along and did not know where;

	so did Sabellius and Arius, and those fools
	who were like swords to the Scriptures
	in making straight faces crooked.

	Nor let people be too confident in judging,
	like a man who reckons up the corn in the field
	before it is ripe;

	for I have seen the briar first show itself
	stiff and fierce all winter,
	and afterwards carry the rose on its top;

	and I have seen a ship run straight and fast
	over the sea for the whole of its voyage,
	and perish at the end, entering the harbour mouth.

	Do not let dame Bertha and master Martin believe,
	because they see one man steal and another make an offering,
	that they see them inside the divine counsel;

	for that one may rise, and this one may fall."
